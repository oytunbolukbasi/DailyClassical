### The big picture

- Mozart entered the symphony in his own catalogue of works on 10 August 1788. It is the last of three symphonies he wrote that summer, after No. 39 (June) and No. 40 (July), and the last symphony he ever wrote.
- Nobody knows for certain whether Mozart heard it performed. The nickname "Jupiter" is not his: according to his son, it was probably coined by the London concert manager Johann Peter Salomon, and it was in use in London early in the 19th century.
- The orchestra has trumpets and drums, which give the outer movements their ceremonial, festive sound. There are no clarinets.
- The finale is famous for its ending: five short tunes, each heard separately before, are played at the same time.
- In one line: power and grace side by side (I), a hushed song with dark undercurrents (II), a gliding dance (III), a finale that weaves five tunes together (IV).

### I. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** A grand, confident opening movement in [[sonata form]]. Loud ceremonial passages with trumpets and drums alternate with quiet, graceful ones, and near the end of the exposition Mozart slips in a light tune from one of his own comic arias.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, power and grace: three loud hammer-strokes from the whole orchestra, answered by a soft, gentle phrase in the strings.
- Theme 2: a calm, singing melody in the strings, in G major.
- The aria tune: a light, tripping melody in the violins, borrowed from *Un bacio di mano*, a song Mozart had written a few months earlier for a comic opera singer.

1. The movement keeps switching between loud and soft, often within a few seconds. Listen for which instruments drop out when the music goes quiet.
2. The comic aria tune at about 2:40 is light and almost silly, yet in the development it becomes the main material.

### II. Andante cantabile

**Summary.** A slow, songlike movement for [[muted]] violins, in the rhythm of a stately dance in three. The surface is calm, but twice the music turns suddenly dark and restless. Trumpets and drums are silent here.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a slow, dignified melody on muted violins, which gives it a soft, veiled sound.
- The shadow: restless, off-beat figures in a minor key that interrupt the calm.

### IV. Molto allegro

**Summary.** A fast finale in [[sonata form]] that is also a showcase of [[counterpoint]]: Mozart builds it from five short themes and keeps combining them. At the very end, all five are heard at once.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: four long, quiet notes in the violins (C, D, F, E), followed by a quick, lively answer.
- Four companions: short figures, each with its own clear rhythm, which Mozart introduces one by one.
- Theme 2: a gentle, graceful tune in G major.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Violins softly play four long notes, then a quick answer; soon the whole orchestra bursts in | Theme 1, the four-note motif |
| ≈ 0:30 | The four-note theme enters in one string section after another, piling up | A [[fugato]] |
| ≈ 1:05 | A gentler, graceful tune | Theme 2, in G major |
| ≈ 2:20 | The four long notes again | The [[exposition]] is repeated |
| ≈ 4:40 | The four-note theme in minor keys, tossed between high and low instruments | The [[development]] |
| ≈ 5:40 | The four long notes return in the home key | The [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 7:35 | The stormy development again | The second half is repeated |
| ≈ 10:30 | Quiet again: the four-note theme, now passed between instruments | The [[coda]] begins |
| ≈ 10:45 | Layer after layer: five different themes enter and play together | The five themes combined, in five-part counterpoint |
| Near the end | Fanfares with trumpets and drums | The symphony ends in festive C major |

**Things to notice**

1. At about 10:45, try to follow one theme while the others go on around it. Then try to hear them all at once.
2. The four long notes are slow, but the music around them never stops racing. That contrast of slow and fast runs through the whole movement.

**In this recording.** Mackerras plays every repeat Mozart wrote, including the second-half repeats in the Andante and the finale that many conductors leave out. That is why these two movements last more than ten minutes each.

### Threads

- **Power and grace.** The first movement sets loud, ceremonial music against quiet, delicate music. The finale ends the symphony in the same contrast.
- **Four notes.** The four-note figure in the trio of the minuet becomes the main theme of the finale.
- **Learned and light.** Mozart builds the finale on strict counterpoint, yet the music stays quick and lively throughout.
