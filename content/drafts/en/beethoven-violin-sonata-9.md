### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote the sonata in spring 1803 for George Bridgetower, a young virtuoso who had grown up in England and was visiting Vienna. They gave the first performance on 24 May 1803 in the Augarten, with Beethoven at the piano. The ink was barely dry: Bridgetower read the slow movement from Beethoven's own copy, over his shoulder.
- Beethoven's working manuscript carries a joking dedication to Bridgetower. They later fell out; Bridgetower remembered a "silly quarrel about a girl". When the sonata was published in Bonn in 1805, it was dedicated instead to Rodolphe Kreutzer, the leading French violinist. Kreutzer, according to Berlioz, never played it and found it unintelligible. His name stuck anyway.
- Beethoven described it as written "in a very concertante style, almost like a concerto". The violin and the piano are equals, and both parts are hard. The finale was first written for an earlier A major sonata, Op. 30 No. 1, and moved here.
- In 1889 Leo Tolstoy named a novella after it, *The Kreutzer Sonata*, a dark story of jealousy in which the music sets off the drama. Janáček's First String Quartet (1923) takes its title from Tolstoy's story.
- In one line: a slow door into a furious duel (I), a calm song and five variations (II), a whirling dance (III).

### I. Adagio sostenuto – Presto

**Summary.** The violin begins alone, slowly, with full chords; the piano answers and the music darkens. Then a fierce Presto breaks out in A minor, a [[sonata form]] in which the two players trade blows. Only a quiet, hymn-like second theme brings relief.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: slow, rich chords on the violin alone, two or three notes at a time.
- Theme 1, Presto: a short, rising figure that snaps into hard, clipped chords, passed between violin and piano.
- Theme 2: a soft, hymn-like tune in long notes, in E major.
- The closing theme: a driving, stamping tune in E minor that powers the end of each section.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to who leads. In the Presto the tune keeps jumping from violin to piano and back, often mid-phrase: neither player accompanies the other for long.
2. Notice how the quiet second theme moves in long, even notes while everything around it races. It sounds slower, though the tempo has not changed.

**In this recording.** Perlman and Ashkenazy recorded the sonata in Kingsway Hall, London, in October 1973, as part of their complete Beethoven sonatas for Decca. They take the repeat of the exposition (≈ 3:35), which is why the movement lasts almost twelve minutes. On the Spotify album the sonata is tracks 1–3, followed by Sonata No. 10.

### II. Andante con variazioni

**Summary.** After the storm, a gentle, singing tune in F major, followed by five [[variation]]s. Each variation keeps the tune's shape but dresses it differently: first the piano shines, then the violin, then the music turns dark and slow, before the light returns.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a calm, simple tune in two halves, each heard twice, shared between piano and violin.
- Variation 1: the piano decorates the tune with [[trill]]s over quick triplets; the violin adds little repeated notes.
- Variation 2: the violin's turn, with fast, glittering runs.
- Variation 3: in F minor, slow and serious.
- Variation 4 and 5: light filigree, then a broader, more searching ending.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the tune itself under the decoration. In every variation its outline and length stay the same, so you can always tell where you are in it.
2. Variation 3 is the only one in the minor. Notice how much darker the same tune sounds.

### III. Finale. Presto

**Summary.** One loud piano chord, then a whirling dance in 6/8, like a [[tarantella]]. The two players chase each other almost without pause; a few quieter, slower bars in two-time break the rhythm before the race starts again.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a quick, galloping tune in groups of three, tossed between violin and piano.
- The second theme: a smoother tune, interrupted by short passages in a slower two-time.

**In this recording.** Perlman and Ashkenazy repeat the finale's exposition. Recordings that leave it out, like Oistrakh and Oborin's (about 7 minutes), are almost two minutes shorter.

### Threads

- **Two equals.** Beethoven's own description, "almost like a concerto", fits: there is no accompanist. The piano is as much a soloist as the violin, and the tunes keep changing hands.
- **A minor and A major.** The sonata is called "in A major", but the first movement's main body is in A minor. Only the first bars and the finale belong to the major.
- **Calm in the middle.** Between two fast, fierce movements stands a slow set of variations. The contrast is the shape of the whole piece.
