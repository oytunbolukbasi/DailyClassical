### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the concerto in the early 1760s for Joseph Franz Weigl, a longtime friend and the principal cellist of Prince Nikolaus Esterházy's orchestra, where Haydn worked. Around 1765 Haydn noted its opening theme in his own catalogue of his works.
- Then the music disappeared. Only the theme in Haydn's catalogue showed that it had existed, until 1961, when the musicologist Oldřich Pulkert found a copy in the National Museum in Prague. The first modern performance was given on 19 May 1962 by Miloš Sádlo with the Czechoslovak Radio Symphony Orchestra, conducted by Charles Mackerras. A few doubts have been raised about its authorship, but most experts accept it as Haydn's.
- The orchestra is small: strings, two oboes and two horns. The form still looks back to the Baroque concerto, with the orchestra's refrain, the [[ritornello]], framing the soloist's passages.
- It has since become a staple of the cello repertoire, recorded by Rostropovich, Jacqueline du Pré, Yo-Yo Ma and many others.
- In one line: a dignified, sunny march with brilliant solo writing (I), a long, quiet song for cello and strings (II), a breathless race (III).

### I. Moderato

**Summary.** The orchestra opens with a firm, upright theme in dotted rhythms. When the cello enters it plays the same theme in rich chords on all four strings at once, and then shows off: rapid repeated notes, leaps between low and very high, long runs. One theme dominates the movement, broken into small pieces that Haydn develops one by one. Near the end the orchestra stops for the soloist's [[cadenza]].

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a stately, dotted melody, first in the orchestra, then in full cello chords.
- The solo writing: fast repeated notes and sudden jumps into the cello's highest register.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** The oboes and horns fall silent; only the strings accompany the cello. After a calm opening for the strings, the cello enters on a single long note while the orchestra starts the theme again underneath it, and only then takes up the melody itself. Haydn repeats this gesture several times: the cello appears out of a held note, as if it had been listening. Near the end comes a second [[cadenza]].

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a slow, singing melody in F major for the strings.
- The long note: the cello's way of entering, holding one note while the music moves beneath it.

### III. Allegro molto

**Summary.** A fast, bustling finale. The orchestra sets off alone, and once again the cello enters on a long note before racing away. It leaps so quickly between its low and high strings that it sounds like two instruments talking to each other. The last of the long-note entries is on a piercing high G.

**Main ideas**

- The running theme: quick, driving notes in the violins, taken up by the cello.
- The two voices: rapid jumps between the cello's bass and treble, as if in conversation.

### Threads

- **The long note.** In all three movements the cello enters on a single sustained note. It is Haydn's signature in this concerto.
- **A friend's instrument.** The concerto was written for a particular player, Weigl, and it is full of things he could do well: chords, fast repeated notes, very high passages.
- **Lost and found.** For two centuries only a few notes in Haydn's catalogue showed that this concerto had existed. Since 1962 it has become part of every cellist's repertoire.
