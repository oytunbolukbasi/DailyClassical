### The big picture

- The piece answers a poem by Stéphane Mallarmé, *L'après-midi d'un faune*. A faun, half man and half goat, wakes in the heat of a Sicilian afternoon, plays his pipes, remembers or dreams of two nymphs he chased, and drifts back to sleep.
- Debussy called his music "a very free illustration" of the poem: not a story told in order, but a succession of scenes and desires in the heat of the afternoon. Mallarmé had doubted that music could add anything to his verse. After the first performance he wrote to Debussy that the music went further than his text, into nostalgia and light.
- The first performance was in Paris on 22 December 1894, conducted by Gustave Doret, with Georges Barrère playing the flute solo. Pierre Boulez, who conducts the recording here, said that the flute of the faun brought new breath to the art of music.
- The orchestra is small and soft: three flutes, oboes, cor anglais, clarinets, bassoons, four horns, two harps, two tiny antique cymbals and strings. There are no trumpets, trombones or timpani.
- In 1912 Vaslav Nijinsky turned the music into a ballet for the Ballets Russes. Its frankly sensual ending caused a scandal.
- In one line: a flute wakes in the heat, desire rises and fades, and the faun drifts back to sleep.

### I. Très modéré

**Summary.** One continuous movement of 110 bars, built almost entirely from the flute's opening melody. The melody slides down and back up, and never quite settles in a key. It passes from instrument to instrument, changing colour each time, and Debussy's harmony, with touches of the [[whole-tone scale]], seems to float without a home. In the middle, exactly halfway through the score, a broad new melody rises in D-flat major. Then the flute returns and the afternoon dissolves into sleep.

**Main ideas**

- The faun's melody: a solo flute, low and alone, sliding down by small steps and climbing back, so that the key stays uncertain.
- The middle melody: a broad, warm tune in D-flat major, first in the woodwinds, then in the strings.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the first bars: a flute with no accompaniment at all. Count how many different instruments play this melody before the end.
2. Notice how rarely the music is loud. The single climax lasts only a few seconds.

**In this recording.** Pierre Boulez recorded the piece with the Cleveland Orchestra in March 1991. On the Spotify album it is track 1, followed by Debussy's *Images* and *Printemps*.

### Threads

- **One melody.** Almost everything comes from the flute's opening tune, transformed by new harmonies and new instruments.
- **Colour, not drama.** The music changes mood through orchestral colour: a harp, muted horns, a shimmer of strings, the tiny ring of antique cymbals.
- **A turning point.** Its floating harmony and free form opened the way for much of the music of the 20th century.
