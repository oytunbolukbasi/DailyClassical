### The big picture

- In 1721 Bach, then music director to Prince Leopold at Köthen, copied out six concertos in his own hand and sent them to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg-Schwedt, with a dedication in French dated 24 March. He called them *Six concertos for several instruments*; the name "Brandenburg" came later.
- No. 3 is written for strings only, in three equal groups: three violins, three violas and three cellos, with a bass line and harpsichord, the [[continuo]], underneath. There is no soloist. The nine players form three teams of three, and any of them may step forward with the tune for a moment.
- The middle movement is famous for being almost empty: Bach wrote one bar with two slow chords. He probably expected a player to improvise something before or around them, a short [[cadenza]]. Every ensemble fills the gap in its own way.
- Bach later reused the first movement, with added oboes and horns, as the opening Sinfonia of his cantata *Ich liebe den Höchsten von ganzem Gemüte*, BWV 174 (1729). The concerto is the shortest of the six.
- In one line: a busy, cheerful game of teams (I), a pause on two chords (II), a whirling dance (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** Bach gave this movement no tempo marking; it is always played at a brisk pace. A bright refrain for everyone, the [[ritornello]], is built from a three-note figure that bounces between two notes. Between its returns the three teams toss short figures back and forth, and the texture keeps changing from a solid mass of strings to a web of nine separate parts.

**Main ideas**

- The bouncing figure: three quick notes, up and back, repeated and passed around.
- The exchanges: one group plays a figure, another answers, a third joins in.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** In Bach's score the slow movement is one bar: two chords, the second leaving the music hanging, so that the finale follows as an answer. In this recording Trevor Pinnock fills the gap with a short slow movement Bach wrote for an early version of his Sonata in G for violin and harpsichord (BWV 1019a), ending on the same kind of open chord.

**Main ideas**

- The two chords: a quiet, questioning close that leads straight into the finale.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the last chord does not settle. The finale, which follows at once, is the answer.

### III. Allegro

**Summary.** A fast, whirling dance in groups of three notes, close to a jig. It starts with the violins and spreads through the whole band. The movement has two parts, the second much longer than the first, and each is played twice.

**Main ideas**

- The whirl: quick notes in threes, rushing up and down the scale, passed from group to group.

**In this recording.** The English Concert play on period instruments with a small string group; the album credits list a first, second and third viola and cello, as in Bach's score. Trevor Pinnock directs from the harpsichord. The recording was made in Henry Wood Hall in London in 1982.

### Threads

- **Strength in threes.** Three violins, three violas, three cellos; the opening figure is three notes; the finale moves in groups of three; there are three movements.
- **Everyone a soloist.** In a Baroque concerto a small group of soloists usually stands out from the band. Here the band is the soloist: every part takes the lead at some point.
- **The empty middle.** A whole movement of one bar, two chords long. Bach leaves it open, and each performance answers the question differently.
