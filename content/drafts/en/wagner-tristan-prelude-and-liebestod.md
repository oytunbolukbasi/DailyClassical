### The big picture

- *Tristan und Isolde* is an opera in three acts, composed between 1857 and 1859 in Zurich, Venice and Lucerne. Two lovers, bound by a love potion and by a love they cannot live out, find union only in death. It was first staged in Munich on 10 June 1865.
- Wagner was reading the philosopher Schopenhauer, for whom desire is endless and never satisfied. He was also in love with Mathilde Wesendonck, the wife of his patron.
- The opera's first chord, the [[Tristan chord]], does not resolve the way the ear expects. The Prelude keeps reaching for a resolution and never gets it. It comes only at the very end of the opera, in Isolde's last song.
- Wagner himself paired the Prelude with that final scene for concerts. Confusingly, he called the Prelude the "Liebestod" (love-death) and the ending "Transfiguration". The name Liebestod stuck to the ending after Liszt used it for his piano version in 1867.
- This recording is live, from Salzburg in 1987, with the soprano Jessye Norman singing Isolde's words. The Liebestod is often played without the voice, the soprano's line given to the orchestra.
- In one line: longing that never arrives (I), arrival in death (II).

### I. Prelude

**Summary.** Slow waves of longing. A short rising phrase and an unresolved chord are repeated, each time a little higher, then grow into long surges that build to one enormous climax and fall back without ever arriving. Wagner marked it "slow and languishing".

**Main ideas**

- The opening: the cellos rise by a few notes; woodwinds answer with a strange, unresolved chord, the Tristan chord, and an oboe line that climbs and stops.
- The glance: a long, warm melody for the cellos, a little later.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The cellos alone, a short rising phrase; then woodwinds hold a sour, hanging chord; the oboe climbs; silence | The [[Tristan chord]]. The question the whole opera asks |
| ≈ 0:45 | The same phrase and chord again, a step higher; silence | The question repeated |
| ≈ 1:30 | A third time, higher still; now the music begins to flow | Longing sets the music in motion |
| ≈ 2:00 | The cellos sing a long, warm melody | A new theme, often called "the glance": the moment the lovers first looked at each other |
| ≈ 4:00–8:30 | Wave after wave: the strings swell, the music seems about to arrive, then slips away | Each [[crescendo]] falls back before it lands |
| ≈ 9:00 | The full orchestra at its loudest; the brass blaze out the opening chord | The climax. Still no resolution |
| ≈ 10:00 | The music collapses; the opening phrase returns, broken up in the woodwinds | Exhaustion |
| Last minute | Low, quiet strings sink into darkness | In the opera, the curtain would rise here |

**Things to notice**

1. Sing the cellos' first three notes back to yourself, then listen for them throughout the Prelude. They are everywhere.

### II. Liebestod

**Summary.** Tristan has died in Isolde's arms. She sings over his body, sees him smiling and alive, and is carried away on waves of music until she sinks, as the opera's last words have it, "unconscious – highest bliss". The resolution denied in the Prelude finally arrives.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The soprano begins softly, over hushed strings: "Mild und leise wie er lächelt" ("How softly and gently he smiles") | Isolde's vision. The melody comes from the lovers' duet in Act II |
| ≈ 1:30 | The strings begin to rise in waves under the voice | The vision grows |
| ≈ 3:30 | Larger waves; the voice soars above the full orchestra | Isolde is swept up |
| ≈ 4:30–5:30 | The climax: the whole orchestra at full power, the voice riding on top; then the music opens out into a radiant major chord | The long-delayed resolution |
| ≈ 6:00 | The voice sinks on its last words | "Unbewusst, höchste Lust": unconscious, highest bliss |
| Last minute | The orchestra alone; an oboe recalls the Tristan chord one last time, and it melts into a calm B major chord | The longing is finally stilled |

**In this recording.** It was recorded at a concert in Salzburg in August 1987, while a film about Karajan was being made; he was 79. Jessye Norman sings Isolde.

### Threads

- **Longing and release.** The Prelude refuses to resolve; the Liebestod does. Together they make one arc of almost twenty minutes.
- **One chord.** The Tristan chord opens the Prelude and returns, finally resolved, in the last bars.
- **Waves.** Both pieces move in surges that build and fall back. Listen to the shape, more than to the tunes.
