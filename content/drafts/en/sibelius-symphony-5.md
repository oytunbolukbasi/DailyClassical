### The big picture

- The symphony was written for Sibelius's 50th birthday, 8 December 1915, which Finland marked as a national holiday. He conducted the first performance that evening in Helsinki.
- He was not satisfied. He revised it in 1916 and again from 1917 to 1919, joining the first two of its four movements into one. The version heard today is the last, first played on 24 November 1919.
- In April 1915, while he was working on it, he saw sixteen swans flying by and called it one of the great experiences of his life. The swinging horn theme of the finale grew from that moment.
- There are no big tunes at the start. Short fragments, first heard in the horns and woodwinds, slowly join up into larger shapes. It is the only one of Sibelius's symphonies with every movement in a major key.
- In one line: a slow horn call that speeds up into a whirling dance (I), a light, plucked intermezzo (II), rushing strings and the great swan hymn (III).

### I. Tempo molto moderato – Allegro moderato – Presto

**Summary.** Two movements in one. It begins slowly, with a soft horn call and woodwinds fluttering around it, sinks into a dark passage for bassoon, then rises to a broad climax. Without any break the music then turns into a quick dance and keeps accelerating to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: a soft, rising phrase of a few notes. Much of the symphony grows out of it.
- The woodwind pairs: woodwinds fluttering in twos, like birds answering the horn.
- The dance: a light, quick tune in three, which takes over in the second half.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A soft horn call, with other horns holding long notes beneath; the woodwinds answer with fluttering pairs of notes | The opening idea. Almost everything that follows is built from it |
| ≈ 1:30 | A short, lilting woodwind figure over trembling strings, then a quiet, hymn-like phrase in horns and woodwinds | More fragments are added, each only a few notes long |
| ≈ 3:10 | The horn call returns, now over a shimmering string [[tremolo]] | The ideas go round a second time, changed |
| ≈ 6:15–7:45 | A bassoon plays a slow, mournful line on its own while the strings murmur and slide underneath | The darkest place in the symphony: the [[development]] |
| ≈ 8:00–9:00 | The music brightens and broadens; trumpets ring out the horn call | The climax of the slow part, *Largamente* |
| ≈ 9:45 | With no pause, the music lightens into a quick dance in three | *Allegro moderato*: the slow movement has turned into a [[scherzo]] |
| ≈ 11:00 | A trumpet plays a jaunty tune over the drums | A contrasting middle section, built from the same material |
| Last 2 minutes | Faster and faster; the whole orchestra races to a blaze of E-flat major | *Presto*: the [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to catch the moment when the slow music becomes fast. Sibelius speeds up so gradually that the join is very hard to hear.
2. Compare the horn call at the start with the trumpets at the climax. It is the same idea, grown from a whisper into a proclamation.

**In this recording.** Colin Davis recorded the Fifth and Seventh symphonies with the Boston Symphony Orchestra in January 1975, the start of a complete Sibelius cycle in Boston. His opening is broad and patient, so the first movement runs longer than in many recordings. On the Spotify album the symphony is tracks 1–3, followed by the Seventh.

### II. Andante mosso, quasi allegretto

**Summary.** A short, light movement between two large ones. It is a set of [[variation]]s on a very simple tune, first plucked by the strings with the flutes. The variations become busier and faster, then settle back into calm.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a plain tune of short, detached notes that climb step by step.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft, held chords in the woodwinds and horns | A short introduction |
| ≈ 0:15 | Plucked strings and flutes play a simple tune of short notes | The theme, played [[pizzicato]] |
| ≈ 1:30 | The tune again, with woodwinds chirping around it | The first [[variation]]s |
| ≈ 3:00–4:30 | The strings take up their bows; the music grows fuller and gradually faster | *Poco a poco stretto* |
| ≈ 5:00 | A calm passage; long, quiet woodwind lines | *Tranquillo* |
| ≈ 6:15–7:30 | The pace quickens again and the texture thickens | A second build-up |
| Near the end | The plucked tune comes back at its first speed and the movement closes softly | The theme's last appearance |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the simple plucked tune. It never disappears for long: every variation keeps its outline, even when the rhythm changes around it.

### III. Allegro molto – Largamente assai

**Summary.** The strings rush in like a wind. Then the horns begin a slow, swinging figure, the theme Sibelius linked to the swans. Both ideas come back, and the swinging theme grows into a grand hymn for the whole orchestra. The symphony ends with six chords separated by silence.

**Main ideas**

- The rushing strings: quick, whispering notes passed through the string section.
- The swan theme: a slow, swinging figure in the horns, two notes to each swing, with a broad melody sung above it.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Violas, then all the strings, rush in with quick, whispering notes; the woodwinds join | The finale begins |
| ≈ 1:30 | The horns start a slow, swinging figure, like a great bell rocking; above it, woodwinds and strings sing a broad melody | The swan theme. The scholar Donald Tovey compared it to the god Thor swinging his hammer |
| ≈ 3:00–4:15 | The rushing strings return, quieter and stranger | *Misterioso*: the opening music comes back, changed |
| ≈ 4:30 | The swinging figure returns, slower and broader, in the brass and then the whole orchestra | The swan theme, now in the home key, E-flat major |
| ≈ 6:00–7:00 | The swinging slows almost to a stop; the harmonies grind against each other, then open out | *Largamente assai*: the climax |
| Last 40 seconds | Six loud chords, each followed by a silence | The ending. It was not in the 1915 version; Sibelius added it in his revisions |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the six final chords and listen to the silences between them. The silences are part of the music.
2. In the swan theme, keep your ear on the horns. They keep swinging, steady as a pendulum, while the melody above them changes.

### Threads

- **Pieces of a mosaic.** Sibelius wrote in his diary that working on this symphony felt like God throwing down pieces of a mosaic for him to fit together. Listen for small fragments that only later join up into tunes.
- **Slow to fast, fast to slow.** The first movement starts slowly and ends at top speed; the finale starts fast and ends in a broad, slow hymn.
- **Swans.** The finale's swinging theme came from sixteen swans in flight, seen in April 1915 while he was writing the first version.
- **Major keys.** It is Sibelius's only symphony in which every movement is in a major key. The mood is grand and bright, even in its darkest passages.
