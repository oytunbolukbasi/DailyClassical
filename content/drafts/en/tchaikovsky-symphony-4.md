### The big picture

- Tchaikovsky began the symphony in the spring of 1877 and finished it in January 1878 in San Remo. In between, he married in the summer of 1877; by the autumn the marriage had collapsed and he had fled abroad. He completed the scoring in Venice and San Remo, and wrote that no earlier orchestral work had cost him such labour.
- The score is dedicated "to my best friend": Nadezhda von Meck, the wealthy widow who paid him an allowance on condition that they never meet. He called it "our symphony" in his letters to her.
- At her request he wrote down a programme. The opening fanfare, he said, is Fate, "the seed of the whole symphony", a force that stops happiness from reaching its goal. Life is an alternation of hard reality with fleeting dreams of happiness.
- Nikolai Rubinstein conducted the first performance in Moscow on 22 February 1878. Tchaikovsky was in Florence. He kept a special love for the work; ten years later he still called it his best symphonic piece.
- In one line: Fate against dreams in a long, stormy waltz (I), an evening song of memories (II), a scherzo plucked by the strings (III), a folk festival that Fate interrupts (IV).

### I. Andante sostenuto – Moderato con anima

**Summary.** The longest movement, almost as long as the other three together. A brass fanfare opens it and comes back to cut off each section, like a gate slamming shut. Between the fanfares come two kinds of music: a restless, limping waltz in F minor, and softer, dreamlike themes that drift into far-off keys.

**Main ideas**

- The fanfare: horns and bassoons, then trumpets, hammering out one repeated note in a stiff, dotted rhythm. Tchaikovsky's "Fate".
- Theme 1: a sighing, syncopated melody in the violins that sways in three. The score marks it "in waltz movement".
- The dream themes: a wistful clarinet solo, then a gentle, swaying tune over soft drum rolls.

1. Count the fanfares. Each one ends a section: listen for the moment it breaks into the dream music.
2. Try to tap the waltz in Theme 1. The accents keep landing between the beats, which is why it never feels like a dance you could actually dance.

**In this recording.** Mravinsky led the Leningrad Philharmonic for fifty years, from 1938 until his death in 1988. In September 1960 the orchestra made its only visit to Britain, and Deutsche Grammophon recorded this symphony in London during that month. The Fifth and Sixth followed in Vienna in November.

### II. Andantino in modo di canzona

**Summary.** A slow song, as the title says. Tchaikovsky described it as the melancholy of evening, when you sit alone with a book that falls from your hand and memories crowd in. The oboe's tune frames the movement; a livelier middle section rises to a loud climax, and then the tune returns, decorated, and fades.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a plain, sad melody for solo oboe over plucked strings.
- The middle section: a firmer, dance-like tune that builds up energy.

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the oboe and the cellos in the first two minutes: the same tune, two colours.
2. When the tune returns after the climax, listen to the flutes and clarinets around it. Tchaikovsky's memories come back blurred and decorated.

### III. Scherzo: Pizzicato ostinato – Allegro

**Summary.** The strings put away their bows and pluck the whole movement. Tchaikovsky wrote that this was a new orchestral effect he had designed himself. Woodwinds and brass get their own sections, and at the end the three groups toss the music between them. Tchaikovsky's picture: idle fancies after a little wine, a drunken peasant song, a military band passing in the distance.

**Main ideas**

- The strings: a quick, whispering tune, all [[pizzicato]].
- The woodwinds: a jaunty, rustic tune, like a village band.
- The brass: a tiny, distant march, very quiet and clipped.

**Things to notice**

1. The three groups never play the same tune together: listen for how they hand the music on.
2. The only percussion in this movement is the timpani. Everything else that sounds like a drum is plucked strings.

### IV. Finale: Allegro con fuoco

**Summary.** A crash of cymbals and rushing scales, and the finale is a folk festival. Tchaikovsky's advice: if you find no joy in yourself, go out among the people. In the middle of the celebration the Fate fanfare from the first movement returns. Then the crowd sweeps the lonely listener back into the fun.

**Main ideas**

- The festival: loud, rushing scales and chords for the whole orchestra.
- The folk song: a small, plaintive tune, "In the Field a Birch Tree Stood", that is repeated over and over in changing colours.
- The fanfare from the first movement.

### Threads

- **Fate.** The opening fanfare returns in the first movement as a structural marker and once in the finale. Tchaikovsky told a friend that the symphony's central idea mirrored Beethoven's Fifth.
- **Dreams and reality.** In the first movement the dream themes are always broken off. The finale finds a different answer: joy in other people.
- **Colour as structure.** Tchaikovsky separates the orchestra into groups: strings, woodwinds and brass each have their own territory in the scherzo, and the fanfare is always brass.
- **A symphony with a story.** Tchaikovsky wrote the programme for von Meck, but warned her that music does not yield to detailed explanation. The story is a way in, not a map.
