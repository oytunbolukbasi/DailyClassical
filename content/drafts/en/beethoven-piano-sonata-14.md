### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it in 1801, at thirty, and it was published in Vienna in 1802. He dedicated it to his young piano pupil, Countess Giulietta Guicciardi.
- He called it *Sonata quasi una fantasia*, "a sonata in the manner of a fantasy", and he gave the same heading to its companion, Op. 27 No. 1. Instead of a fast opening, it begins with a slow, quiet movement and saves its weight for the finale.
- "Moonlight" is not Beethoven's word. In the 1820s the critic and poet Ludwig Rellstab compared the first movement to moonlight on water, and by the late 1830s publishers in German and English were using the name.
- At the top of the first movement Beethoven wrote that it should be played very delicately and "senza sordino": without dampers, that is, with the [[sustain pedal]] held down so the notes keep ringing.
- Its fame wore on him. He is said to have told his pupil Carl Czerny that he had written better things.
- In one line: a slow, hushed lament (I), a short, light dance (II), a storm (III).

### I. Adagio sostenuto

**Summary.** Three layers move very slowly and very quietly: deep octaves in the bass, a steady ripple of broken chords in the middle, and a sad, simple tune on top. The music never gets louder than *p*, and the pedal blurs it all into one sound.

**Main ideas**

- The ripple: broken chords in groups of three, played by the right hand from beginning to end.
- The tune: one note repeated in a long-short rhythm, like a slow bell, then rising and falling by small steps.
- The bass: long, low octaves that sink step by step.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Low octaves and a soft ripple of broken chords, with no tune yet | Four bars of accompaniment set the mood |
| ≈ 0:21 | A tune enters on top: one note repeated in a long-short rhythm, like a distant bell | The melody, sung above the ripple. One player, three layers |
| ≈ 0:50 | The chords shift under the tune; for a moment the colour brightens, then darkens again | The music leaves C-sharp minor, passes through a major key and moves on |
| ≈ 2:25–3:35 | The tune falls silent. A low note is held while the ripple climbs high up the keyboard and sinks back, again and again | A [[pedal note]] in the bass prepares the return |
| ≈ 3:37 | The tune with its bell-like repeated note, back where it began | The opening returns, like a [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 5:12 | The long-short rhythm of the tune moves down into the bass, under the ripple | The [[coda]] |
| ≈ 5:45 | Slow, low chords, quieter still, and silence | The end, *pp* |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the pedal. Beethoven asked for the dampers to stay off the strings, so each chord melts into the next instead of stopping cleanly.
2. Count the loud moments. There are none: everything stays soft, which makes the finale's first bars all the more of a shock.

**Two beats, not four.** Beethoven marked the movement *alla breve*, two slow beats in a bar. It is a slow movement, but not a funeral march, and many pianists keep it moving.

### II. Allegretto

**Summary.** A short, light dance in the major, a pause for breath between the two dark movements. It is a [[minuet]] with a [[trio]], and it lasts little more than two minutes.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: gentle chords that answer each other, first smooth, then short and detached.
- The trio: lower and heavier, with accents that fall off the beat.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A gentle, lilting tune in chords, smooth phrases answered by short, bouncing ones | The dance, in D-flat major |
| ≈ 0:16 | The tune wanders a little further, then comes home; this part is played twice | The second half of the dance, repeated |
| ≈ 0:56 | Lower and heavier, with accents that land just after the beat | The [[trio]] |
| ≈ 1:44 | The lilting opening tune again | The dance returns, this time without repeats |
| ≈ 2:12 | Two quiet closing chords | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. D-flat major sounds the same on the piano as C-sharp major, so this is the home key of the sonata turned to the major. The story goes that Franz Liszt called the movement "a flower between two abysses".

### III. Presto agitato

**Summary.** The heaviest movement comes last. Broken chords race up the keyboard and end in two hard, loud chords, again and again. It is in [[sonata form]], fast and furious, and it ends in the minor.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: rising waves of fast notes, each crowned by two loud, sharp chords.
- Theme 2: a tense melody in the right hand over a fast, steady pattern in the left.
- Closing idea: insistent repeated chords and short, pressing phrases.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Waves of fast notes surge up the keyboard, each crashing into two loud chords | Theme 1. The ripple of the first movement, now at full speed |
| ≈ 0:21 | A held chord and a moment of silence | A breath before the next section |
| ≈ 0:33 | A melody in the right hand over a rushing left hand, with sudden loud accents | Theme 2, in G-sharp minor |
| ≈ 1:08 | Hammered chords and short phrases that will not let go | The closing section of the [[exposition]] |
| ≈ 1:44 | The rising waves again, now starting in a new key | The [[development]]. Kempff goes straight on, without repeating the exposition |
| ≈ 2:42 | The waves and the two hard chords back in C-sharp minor | The [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 4:10 | The rising waves grow into a long, rolling build-up, with the left hand churning low | The [[coda]] begins |
| ≈ 4:58 | A free run up and down the keyboard, then two slow bars, almost a stop | A short [[cadenza]]-like passage |
| ≈ 5:05 | Fast again: broken chords plunge down the whole keyboard; two final loud chords | The end, in C-sharp minor |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the first bars with the first movement. Both are built from broken chords in C-sharp minor: slow and soft there, fast and violent here.
2. Listen for the moment near ≈ 4:58 when everything slows almost to a halt before the last rush.

**In this recording.** Kempff does not take the repeat of the exposition, so his finale is about a minute and a half shorter than many others. Gilels and Barenboim, in the recordings recommended above, both play it.

### Threads

- **Slow to fast.** Most sonatas of the time open with their weightiest, fastest movement. This one does the opposite: it starts almost in a whisper and ends in a storm.
- **Broken chords.** The rippling chords of the first movement come back in the finale as rushing waves. Same idea, opposite mood.
- **The pedal.** Beethoven's instruction "without dampers" gives the first movement its haze. Modern pianos ring longer than his did, so pianists change the pedal more often than the score suggests.
- **C-sharp.** All three movements stay on the same keynote: minor, then major (written as D-flat), then minor again.
