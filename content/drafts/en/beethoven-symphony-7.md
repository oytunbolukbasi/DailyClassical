### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote the Seventh in 1811–12, partly while taking the cure at the Bohemian spa town of Teplitz. It is dedicated to Count Moritz von Fries.
- It was first performed on 8 December 1813 in Vienna, with Beethoven conducting, at a charity concert for soldiers wounded at the Battle of Hanau, weeks after Napoleon's defeat at Leipzig. The same concert introduced his patriotic *Wellington's Victory*. The orchestra included the violinist Louis Spohr and the composers Hummel, Meyerbeer and Salieri.
- The audience demanded the second movement again on the spot. Beethoven himself called the symphony one of his best works.
- Each movement is driven by one rhythm, repeated with tremendous energy. Richard Wagner called the Seventh "the apotheosis of the dance".
- In one line: a slow awakening and a leaping dance (I), a solemn procession (II), a whirlwind with a hymn at its heart (III), a wild, stamping finale (IV).

### I. Poco sostenuto – Vivace

**Summary.** A long, slow introduction climbs through vast scales and visits two distant keys. Then a single note is tapped back and forth until it turns into a dance. From there the movement never lets go of one skipping rhythm: long, short, long.

**Main ideas**

- The scales: long, slow climbs through the strings in the introduction.
- The rhythm: a skipping long-short-long figure in six beats, on which the whole fast section is built.
- Theme 1: a light, dancing tune, first on the flute, then shouted by the whole orchestra.

### II. Allegretto

**Summary.** A solemn procession in A minor, built on one walking rhythm: long, short-short, long, long. It starts very low and quiet, and each time it returns another layer is added on top. It was so popular at the first performance that it had to be played again at once.

**Main ideas**

- The rhythm: long, short-short, long, long. The tune is almost all on one note; it is mostly rhythm.
- The countermelody: a sad, singing line that weaves around the rhythm.
- The major-key melody: a calm song for clarinets and bassoons in the middle.

2. The first and last sounds are the same unresolved chord.

### III. Presto – Assai meno presto

**Summary.** A [[scherzo]] at top speed in F major, full of sudden loud bursts. Twice it gives way to a slow, glowing [[trio]] in D major, a hymn-like tune said to be based on an Austrian pilgrims' song, sung by the woodwinds while the violins hold one high note. The movement ends with a joke: the hymn starts a third time and is cut off.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: quick, scurrying music tossed between strings and woodwinds, with sudden loud outbursts.
- The trio: a slow, swaying hymn in the woodwinds and horns over a long-held note in the violins.

### IV. Allegro con brio

**Summary.** Fast and furious from the first bar to the last. Two violent chords set off a whirling tune with a heavy kick on the second beat, and the energy never drops. Near the end the basses grind a two-note growl underneath while the orchestra rises to a dynamic Beethoven rarely asked for: *fff*, as loud as possible.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a whirling tune in the violins, with heavy accents off the main beat.
- The growl: a two-note pattern in the basses that drives the coda.

### Threads

- **Rhythm first.** Each movement is driven by one rhythm: the skipping long-short-long of the first, the walk of the second, the scurry of the third, the stamp of the finale.
- **Long held notes.** The repeated E before the *Vivace*, the drone of the trio, the ground bass of both codas: under the dance there is always something that does not move.
- **Three keys.** A major is home, but C major and F major keep breaking in: in the introduction, in the first movement's development and in the scherzo, which is in F.
