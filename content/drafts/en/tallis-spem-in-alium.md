### The big picture

- *Spem in alium* is a [[motet]] for forty independent voices: eight choirs of five singers each (soprano, alto, tenor, baritone, bass). Tallis probably wrote it around 1570, near the end of a long career as a singer, organist and composer at the courts of four Tudor monarchs.
- Nobody knows why it was written. A law student, Thomas Wateridge, wrote down a story in 1611: an Italian song in many parts had been sung in England, a duke asked whether no Englishman could do as well, and Tallis wrote a song in forty parts, which was sung in the long gallery of Arundel House in London. The duke was so pleased that he took the gold chain from his own neck and put it round Tallis's. Another theory links the forty voices to Elizabeth I's fortieth birthday in 1573.
- The Latin words come from the Book of Judith: "I have never put my hope in any other but you, God of Israel, who can be angry and yet gracious, and who forgives all the sins of suffering mankind. Lord God, creator of heaven and earth, look upon our lowliness."
- The singers were probably meant to stand in a horseshoe, so that the music moves around the listeners. Tallis uses all forty voices together only a few times; most of the time the sound travels from choir to choir.
- In one line: one voice grows into forty, the sound travels round the room and back, and then all forty pray together.

### I. Spem in alium

**Summary.** One continuous piece of about ten minutes, without instruments. It begins with a single voice; the others enter one by one, and the sound moves across the eight choirs like a wave and back again. Twice the whole choir sings together, and in the closing section all forty voices join in one long prayer.

**Main ideas**

- The opening phrase: a slow, calm line that each new voice takes up in turn. This weaving of independent lines is [[polyphony]] at its richest.
- The full choir: all forty voices on the same chords, a huge but soft-edged sound.
- The answering choirs: groups of voices calling to each other across the space.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen with headphones if you can. In a stereo recording the choirs are spread out, and you can hear the music drift from side to side.
2. Wait for the silence at ≈ 7:20. After seven minutes of almost constant sound, Tallis stops everyone for a moment before the last plea.

**In this recording.** The Tallis Scholars sing with forty singers, one to each part, in the chapel of Merton College, Oxford. The recording first appeared in 1985; the Spotify album is a later Gimell collection that keeps the motet on a single track.

### Threads

- **Space as part of the music.** Tallis wrote not only for forty voices but for forty places in a room. The movement of the sound is as important as the harmony.
- **Hope and humility.** The text begins with hope in God alone and ends with a plea to "look upon our lowliness". The music grows from one voice to forty and ends in a single, shared prayer.
- **A puzzle without an answer.** We do not know when, where or for whom it was first sung. The earliest surviving copies were made in 1610, with English words, for the investiture of Henry, Prince of Wales.
