### The big picture

- Franck wrote the sonata in 1886, at 63, as a wedding present for the Belgian violinist Eugène Ysaÿe, who was 28. On the morning of the wedding, 26 September 1886 in Arlon, a friend handed it over; after a hurried rehearsal, Ysaÿe and the pianist Marie-Léontine Bordes-Pène played it to the guests.
- The first public performance followed on 16 December in Brussels. According to Vincent d'Indy, a pupil of Franck who was there, the daylight faded during the concert and no lamps were allowed in the gallery, so the two players performed the last three movements from memory, almost in the dark.
- The sonata is a famous example of [[cyclic form]]: the violin's first gentle tune comes back, transformed, in every movement, and other themes travel between movements too. The finale is a [[canon]], one instrument following the other with the same melody.
- Franck first thought of the opening movement as slow. Ysaÿe preferred it a little quicker, and Franck marked it Allegretto. The piano part is unusually hard, with wide stretches written by a composer who had very large hands.
- In one line: a dreamy song (I), a storm (II), a free improvisation (III), a sunny canon (IV).

### I. Allegretto ben moderato

**Summary.** A quiet, swaying song. The piano opens with a few soft, unsettled chords, and the violin enters with the gentle rocking tune that is the seed of the whole sonata. The piano has a broader, more passionate tune of its own, which the violin never plays.

**Main ideas**

- The violin's tune: calm and rocking, rising a little and falling back, in a lilting rhythm of three-beat groups.
- The piano's tune: louder and fuller, a song for the piano alone.

**Listening stops**

1. Hum the violin's first tune when it enters. You will meet it again, changed, in all three later movements.

### II. Allegro

**Summary.** A storm in D minor. The piano rushes in with dark, swirling figures, and the violin answers with an urgent, leaping tune. In the middle the music almost stops, and the dreamy world of the first movement returns for a moment, before the storm breaks again and finally clears into D major.

**Main ideas**

- The storm: fast, rolling figures in the piano, low and loud.
- The violin's cry: a passionate tune that leaps upwards.
- The calm: a slower, singing theme for the violin over rippling piano notes.

### III. Recitativo-Fantasia. Ben moderato – Molto lento

**Summary.** The most free and mysterious movement. The piano softly recalls the opening tune; the violin answers alone, in phrases like speech, as in a [[recitative]]. Then a new, warm melody arrives, grows to a powerful climax and sinks back, and the movement ends in darkness.

**Main ideas**

- The recitative: the violin alone, free in rhythm, like someone thinking aloud.
- The new melody: a long, warm tune for the violin over rippling notes in the piano. It will come back in the finale.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

### IV. Allegretto poco mosso

**Summary.** Sunlight after the dark. The piano begins a simple, warm tune, and a moment later the violin plays exactly the same tune, one bar behind, as in a round. This [[canon]] keeps returning, and between its returns the melody from the third movement comes back, once quietly and once at full power.

**Main ideas**

- The canon tune: simple and singing, in A major, shared between the two instruments one bar apart.
- The returning melody: the warm tune from the third movement.

**Listening stops**

### Threads

- **One tune, many lives.** The violin's first tune returns in the second movement's interlude, opens the third, and stands behind the finale. That is [[cyclic form]].
- **Two equals.** The piano is never just an accompanist. It opens the first two movements on its own, owns a theme in the first and leads the canon in the last.
- **From dark to light.** The keys tell the story: A major, a D-minor storm, a dark F-sharp-minor ending, then the sunlight of A major again.
