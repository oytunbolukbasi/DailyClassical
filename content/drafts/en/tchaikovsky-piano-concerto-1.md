### The big picture

- Tchaikovsky wrote the concerto in Moscow between November 1874 and February 1875, while he was teaching at the Moscow Conservatory.
- On Christmas Eve 1874 he played it to Nikolai Rubinstein, the head of the conservatory, who he hoped would perform it. Rubinstein tore it apart and demanded changes. Tchaikovsky, by his own account, refused to change a single note. Rubinstein later became one of the concerto's champions.
- The first performance was given far from Russia, in Boston, on 25 October 1875, by the German pianist Hans von Bülow, to whom the concerto is dedicated. Tchaikovsky revised it in 1879 and again in 1888; the 1888 version is the one usually played.
- Folk songs run through it: a tune Tchaikovsky heard from blind street musicians at a market in Kamianka, in Ukraine (I), a light French song (II) and a Ukrainian song to greet the spring (III).
- In one line: a giant introduction and a jumpy folk tune (I), a gentle pastoral with a fast scherzo in the middle (II), a whirling dance that ends in a blaze (III).

### I. Allegro non troppo e molto maestoso – Allegro con spirito

**Summary.** It opens with one of the best-known tunes in classical music, played by the strings while the piano pounds out huge chords. Then the tune disappears for good. The rest of the movement, in [[sonata form]], is built on a skittish Ukrainian folk tune and a gentler, more consoling melody, with three [[cadenza]]s for the piano.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: four falling notes on the horns, loud, at the very start.
- The big tune: a broad, sweeping melody in D-flat major, first in the violins and cellos.
- The folk tune: quick, jumpy and nervous, first in the piano, in B-flat minor.
- The consoling theme: smooth and warm, in the strings, over a held bass note.

**Things to notice**

1. The big tune is heard only three times, all in the first three minutes. After that the concerto never mentions it again.
2. In the development, listen for a soft, steady drum roll on one low note under the music, like a held breath.

**In this recording.** Argerich played the concerto live in Munich in February 1980 with Kirill Kondrashin, who had conducted Van Cliburn's prize-winning performance of the same concerto in Moscow in 1958. Kondrashin died in 1981, and Philips first released this performance in 1982 as a tribute to him. On the Spotify album the Tchaikovsky is tracks 4–6, after Rachmaninoff's Third Concerto.

### II. Andantino semplice – Prestissimo – Tempo I

**Summary.** A gentle, pastoral movement with a surprise in the middle. A flute sings a simple tune over plucked strings, and the piano repeats it. Then the music suddenly races: a light, fast [[scherzo]], built on a popular French song. At the end the gentle tune returns.

**Main ideas**

- The flute tune: simple, slow and rocking, in D-flat major.
- The French song: a light, skipping melody. Its words mean "One must have fun, dance and laugh".

### III. Allegro con fuoco

**Summary.** A fast, stamping dance in three beats, built on a Ukrainian song that greets the spring. Against it Tchaikovsky sets a broad, singing melody. The two alternate, like a [[rondo]], and at the end the singing melody returns in the full orchestra, now in B-flat major, before a dash to the finish.

**Main ideas**

- The spring dance: quick, accented, in three beats, first in the piano.
- The broad melody: a long, warm tune in the strings, related to a Russian folk song.

**Things to notice**

1. The finale's broad melody arrives three times. Notice how it grows each time, until the last statement fills the whole orchestra.
2. The concerto began in B-flat minor and ends in B-flat major.

### Threads

- **Folk songs.** Each movement borrows a popular tune: a song of Ukrainian street musicians, a French song and a Ukrainian spring song.
- **The vanishing tune.** The most famous melody in the concerto is used only in the introduction. Analysts have found its shapes hidden in the themes of all three movements.
- **Piano against orchestra.** Here the soloist really does fight: huge chords against the full orchestra, and three cadenzas in the first movement alone.
