### The big picture

- Dvořák wrote the quintet between 18 August and 3 October 1887, much of it at Vysoká, his summer house in the Bohemian countryside. He had just been revising his first piano quintet, written fifteen years earlier in the same key; instead of patching up the old piece, he wrote a new one that shares nothing with it but the key and the five instruments.
- It was first played in Prague on 6 January 1888, at the Rudolfinum. A month later Tchaikovsky visited Prague and heard it at a soirée in his honour. He wrote in his diary that he "very much liked his quintet".
- Two of the four movements are named after Slavic folk forms: a [[dumka]], a lament that swings between brooding and high spirits, and a [[furiant]], a fiery Czech dance. Dvořák wrote his own tunes in folk style; he quotes no real folk song.
- The piano is not a soloist with a string quartet behind it. The five players share the themes, and the cello and viola get some of the best tunes.
- In one line: a warm song that keeps flaring up (I), a lament with sudden bursts of joy (II), a whirling dance with a dreamy centre (III), a high-spirited romp with a fugue and a hushed chorale before the final dash (IV).

### I. Allegro ma non tanto

**Summary.** The cello sings a long, warm melody over a gentle, rippling piano. Within half a minute the mood darkens: the same tune bursts out loud in the minor. The whole movement plays with this double face of its themes, sunny and stormy. It is in [[sonata form]], with a second theme for the viola.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, cello: a broad, singing melody in A major over even, rocking piano chords.
- Theme 2, viola: a busier, more restless melody, in the minor.

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the cello's opening melody through the movement. It turns up in the violin, in the piano, in the minor, at full volume, and almost never the same way twice.

**In this recording.** Curzon recorded this quintet with the Vienna Philharmonic Quartet, led by the orchestra's concertmaster Willi Boskovsky, in the Sofiensaal in Vienna in October 1962; John Culshaw produced it. They do not repeat the first part of the first movement, which is why it lasts about ten minutes; performances that take the repeat last around thirteen. On the Spotify album the quintet is tracks 5–8, after Dvořák's String Quartet, Op. 51, played by the Boskovsky Quartet.

### II. Dumka. Andante con moto

**Summary.** A dumka is a Slavic lament, and Dvořák makes it a pattern of sudden contrasts. A sad, swaying melody in F-sharp minor returns again and again, each time richer; between its returns come a brighter, gentler section and, in the middle, a fast, wild dance built from the lament itself. The scheme is A–B–A–C–A–B–A, a kind of [[rondo]].

**Main ideas**

- The lament: a slow, melancholy tune on the piano, over a gently rocking accompaniment, then taken up by the viola.
- The bright episode: a softer, more hopeful tune in D major for the violins.
- The fast episode: the lament's melody turned into a quick, stamping dance.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment around the middle when the slow lament suddenly turns into a fast dance. The tune is the same; only the speed and the mood have changed.
2. Each time the lament returns, notice what has been added around it: a new counter-melody, a richer piano part, a different instrument on the tune.

### III. Scherzo (Furiant). Molto vivace

**Summary.** A furiant is a fast Czech folk dance, and here it whirls. The first violin leads with a quick, swirling tune while the viola and cello pluck the beat beneath. In the middle a slower, dreamy section uses the same material more quietly, with piano and violin trading the melody. Then the dance returns and ends abruptly.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a fast, spinning tune in the first violin over plucked strings.
- The trio: a slower, misty version of the dance, piano and violin taking turns.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Dvořák's furiant does not use the dance's trademark stumble between two and three beats; it keeps a steady, driving three. What makes it a furiant is its fire.

### IV. Finale. Allegro

**Summary.** A cheerful, bustling finale, light on its feet, with three themes. In the middle the second violin starts a little [[fugue]] on the main tune and the others join one by one. Near the end everything stops for a hushed, hymn-like passage; then the music gathers speed and the quintet rushes to a brilliant finish.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a quick, skipping tune, bright and good-humoured.
- Theme 2: a smoother, singing melody.
- The chorale: the main tune slowed right down and played very softly, like a hymn.

**Listening stops**

### Threads

- **Folk spirit without folk songs.** The dumka and the furiant are Slavic forms, but every tune is Dvořák's own. The national colour comes from rhythms, moods and sudden contrasts, not quotations.
- **Light and shade.** The first movement's sunny tune that turns stormy, the dumka's flips from lament to dance: the quintet keeps switching between brooding and joy, and joy wins.
- **Five equals.** The cello opens the work, the viola introduces the second theme, the second violin starts the fugue. The piano is the glue, not the star.
