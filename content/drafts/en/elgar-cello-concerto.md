### The big picture

- In 1918, the night after he came home from hospital, Elgar wrote down the melody that became the concerto's main theme. He finished the work in 1919 at Brinkwells, a cottage in the Sussex woods, after three chamber works written there.
- The first performance, on 27 October 1919 with Felix Salmond and the London Symphony Orchestra, was a disaster: another conductor used up most of the rehearsal time. The critic Ernest Newman still called the music "lovely stuff, very simple".
- It is Elgar's last major work. His wife Alice died in April 1920, and he never finished another large-scale piece.
- The concerto became famous only in the 1960s, through this recording by Jacqueline du Pré, then aged 20.
- In one line: a proud cello recitative and a sad, swaying song (I), a flickering, nervous [[scherzo]] (II), a short, slow lament (III), a striding finale that looks back before it ends (IV).

### I. Adagio – Moderato

**Summary.** The cello begins alone with loud, solemn chords, like a speech. Then the violas, on their own, introduce a sad, swaying melody that the cello takes over. A lighter middle section in E major brings brief relief before the melody returns, and the movement leads straight into the next.

**Main ideas**

- The opening chords: a short, proud [[recitative]] for the cello, marked *nobilmente* ("nobly").
- The main theme: a sad, swaying melody in 9/8, first on the violas.

On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–4, followed by *Sea Pictures* with Janet Baker.

### II. Lento – Allegro molto

**Summary.** After some hesitant starts, plucked chords and short bursts of speed, the cello sets off on a very fast, very light perpetual motion. It is a flickering [[scherzo]] that never settles, with the orchestra adding quick comments.

**Main ideas**

- The flicker: rapid repeated notes on the cello, very quiet and light.

### III. Adagio

**Summary.** A short slow movement and the heart of the concerto: a single melody for the cello, sung almost without a break over a soft orchestra. One theme runs through the whole movement.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a long, tender melody that rises and falls in short phrases.

### IV. Allegro – Moderato – Allegro, ma non troppo – Poco più lento – Adagio

**Summary.** The longest movement. After a short, loud start and another recitative, the cello launches a striding, proud theme. Near the end the music slows, a new aching theme appears, the slow movement's melody returns, and the cello's opening chords come back before a brief, abrupt ending.

**Main ideas**

- The striding theme: a bold, march-like tune marked *risoluto*, with sudden changes of key.
- The memories: the Adagio's melody and the opening recitative, recalled near the end.

### Threads

- **After the war.** Written in 1918–19, the concerto is contemplative and elegiac rather than heroic, very different from Elgar's earlier, grander music.
- **A recitative that returns.** The cello's opening chords frame the whole work and come back just before the end.
- **The cello as speaker.** Free, speech-like passages appear in three of the four movements.
- **Rediscovered.** Neglected for decades, the concerto became one of the two most performed cello concertos, with Dvořák's, after du Pré's recording.
