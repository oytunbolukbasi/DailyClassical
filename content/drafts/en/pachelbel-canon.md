### The big picture

- Johann Pachelbel (1653–1706) was one of the most admired organists of his day. Born in Nuremberg, he worked in Vienna, Eisenach, Erfurt, Stuttgart and Gotha before returning home in 1695 as organist of St Sebaldus Church. He was a close friend of the Bach family and taught Johann Christoph Bach, the eldest brother of Johann Sebastian.
- The Canon and its Gigue are written for three violins and [[continuo]]. Nobody knows when or why. The only surviving source is a copy made around 1840, now in Berlin. One scholar has suggested it was written for Johann Christoph Bach's wedding in October 1694, which Pachelbel probably attended, but that is a guess.
- The canon is built on a [[ground bass]]: eight notes, two bars long, repeated twenty-eight times without change. Above it the three violins play a strict [[canon]], each entering two bars after the one before with exactly the same music.
- For more than two centuries the piece was forgotten. It was first printed in 1919. A slow, lush recording by Jean-François Paillard's chamber orchestra in 1968 started its fame; by the late 1970s it was everywhere, from weddings to advertisements, and its chord pattern has been borrowed by many pop songs.
- In one line: a calm round that grows richer at every turn of the bass (I), then a quick dance to end (II).

### I. Canon

**Summary.** The low strings and harpsichord start a slow eight-note bass line, and it never stops. The three violins come in one after another with the same melody, so the music is a round, like "Frère Jacques". Every two bars the bass begins again; above it the violins move from long notes to running notes to rushing scales and back, a set of [[variation]]s that grows out of the round.

**Main ideas**

- The bass: eight slow notes, stepping down and leaping back up, played by the low strings and harpsichord from the first bar to the last.
- The canon tune: a calm melody in the first violin, copied exactly by the second and then the third violin, two bars later each time.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen only to the bass for a minute. It is the same eight notes every time; everything else changes around it.
2. Try to follow one violin's melody and hear it echoed two bars later by the next.

**In this recording.** The English Concert play period instruments, and Pinnock takes the canon at a flowing pace. Paillard's 1968 version, the one that made the piece famous, is much slower and adds parts of its own. On the Spotify album the Canon and Gigue are tracks 1–2, followed by other Baroque favourites, among them Handel's *Arrival of the Queen of Sheba*.

### II. Gigue

**Summary.** A short, fast dance in a skipping rhythm, its beats divided into threes. There is no repeating bass here: the violins chase each other in imitation, like a lively [[fugue]] in miniature. It has two halves of ten bars, and each half is played twice.

**Main ideas**

- The gigue tune: a quick, leaping phrase thrown from one violin to the next.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A quick, bouncing tune in one violin, taken up by the others in turn | The first half of the [[gigue]] |
| ≈ 0:20 | The same music again | The first half repeated |
| ≈ 0:40 | A new phrase, again passed from voice to voice | The second half |
| ≈ 1:00 | The second half once more, to the final chord | The repeat, and the end |

**Things to notice**

1. After the slow, patient canon, notice how quickly the gigue is over: about a minute and a half.

### Threads

- **Repetition.** Both movements depend on things coming back: the bass in the canon, the imitation and the repeats in the gigue. The pleasure is in hearing what changes when so much stays the same.
- **A pair, not a single piece.** Pachelbel wrote the canon with a gigue to follow it. The gigue is usually left out today, but it was part of the plan: a slow piece, then a quick dance.
- **A late fame.** A piece by a famous organist that nobody played for two hundred years became, through one recording in 1968, one of the most familiar pieces of classical music.
