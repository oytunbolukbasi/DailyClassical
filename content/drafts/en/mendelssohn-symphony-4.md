### The big picture

- Mendelssohn spent 1830 and 1831 travelling, and from the autumn of 1830 he was in Italy: Venice, Florence, Rome, Naples. He began a symphony there. In February 1831 he wrote to his sister Fanny from Rome that it would be "the jolliest piece I have ever done, especially the last movement".
- He finished it in Berlin on 13 March 1833 for the Philharmonic Society of London, and conducted the first performance in London on 13 May 1833. He was 24.
- He was never satisfied with it. He revised the last three movements in 1834 and never published the symphony. It appeared only in 1851, four years after his death, which is why it carries a higher number than works written after it.
- There is no folk music in the first three movements. The finale is the exception: it borrows the leaping saltarello of Rome and the whirling tarantella of Naples. It also does something rare for a symphony of its time: it begins in A major and ends in A minor.
- In one line: sunlight and speed (I), a grave procession (II), a gentle dance with distant horns (III), a breathless Roman dance in the minor (IV).

### I. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The symphony starts at full speed: quick repeated chords in the woodwinds, and the violins leap in with a wide, bright tune. The movement is in [[sonata form]], with a gentle second theme and a surprise in the middle: a new, nervous little tune that the instruments pass between them in strict imitation.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, violins: a wide, leaping tune in a quick, skipping rhythm over chattering woodwind chords.
- Theme 2, clarinets and bassoons: a smooth, gentle melody that moves in small steps.
- The new theme: a quiet, clipped figure in the minor that appears only in the middle of the movement.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the woodwinds' repeated chords under the opening tune. They keep up a steady patter for minutes, like a pulse.
2. The new tune in the middle of the movement is easy to miss on first hearing. Once you know it, listen for it again near the end, where it drives the coda.

**In this recording.** Abbado recorded the symphony with the London Symphony Orchestra in October 1984, in St John's Smith Square, London. He plays the repeat of the opening section, which is why the first movement lasts over ten minutes. On the Spotify album the symphony is tracks 1–4, followed by the "Reformation" Symphony.

### II. Andante con moto

**Summary.** A slow, steady walk in D minor. A grave melody, sung by oboe, bassoon and violas together, moves over a bass line that never stops walking. It is often heard as a procession of pilgrims, and tradition links it to religious processions Mendelssohn saw in Italy. Twice the clarinets bring a warmer, major-key song, and at the end the procession passes into the distance.

**Main ideas**

- The call: a short, wailing figure for winds and violas, heard at the very start and again at the end.
- The march: a plain, chant-like melody over evenly walking notes in the cellos and basses.
- The episode: a gentler, rising tune for clarinets in the major.

1. Follow the bass. It walks at the same steady pace from the first bars to the last, even when the melody stops.

### III. Con moto moderato

**Summary.** Not a fast scherzo but an unhurried dance in three, close to an old-fashioned [[minuet]]. Its long, smooth melody flows from the strings into the woodwinds. In the middle, the [[trio]], two horns and bassoons play a soft fanfare, as if from far away.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a long, smooth string melody in A major, warm and a little wistful.
- The trio: quiet horn calls, answered by quick, light figures in the violins and flutes.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The violins play a long, flowing melody at an easy pace | The dance, in A major |
| ≈ 1:00 | The woodwinds take part, and the melody spreads through the orchestra | The second half of the dance |
| ≈ 2:15 | Two horns and bassoons play a soft, rounded fanfare | The [[trio]], in E major |
| ≈ 3:00 | Violins and flutes answer with quick, light figures; the horn calls grow louder, then fade | The trio rises to a brief climax |
| ≈ 3:50 | The flowing melody returns | The dance again, with new details in the accompaniment |
| Last minute | The horn calls come back quietly; the movement ends softly | A [[coda]] that remembers the trio |

**Things to notice**

1. In the trio, listen for the horns and bassoons together: a soft, rounded sound, like a call heard across a valley.

### IV. Saltarello: Presto

**Summary.** A dance that never stops for breath. Its title is the saltarello, a jumping dance from Rome, and later Mendelssohn adds the smooth, whirling patter of the Neapolitan tarantella. The music stays in the minor almost to the end and finishes in A minor, not in the A major where the symphony began.

**Main ideas**

- The [[saltarello]]: a leaping tune in a hopping, three-note rhythm, first on the flutes.
- The [[tarantella]]: an unbroken stream of quick notes that winds up and down in the strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Loud, abrupt chords from the whole orchestra | The introduction |
| ≈ 0:07 | Two flutes play a leaping tune in a hopping rhythm, over light strings | The [[saltarello]] theme, in A minor |
| ≈ 0:35 | The violins and then the whole orchestra take up the dance | The tune spreads, louder and fuller |
| ≈ 1:30 | Quieter: a smooth, unbroken stream of quick notes winds through the violins | The [[tarantella]], the second dance |
| ≈ 2:30–3:30 | The two dances are thrown together and pushed through new keys; short bursts of the leaping tune | The middle of the movement, where the ideas are worked |
| ≈ 3:45 | The flutes' leaping tune returns | The opening dance comes back |
| ≈ 4:20 | Very quiet: the stream of quick notes sinks low and almost disappears | The calm before the last surge |
| Last 30 seconds | A sudden build to full volume and hard, final chords in A minor | The symphony ends in the minor |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the notes grouped in threes under the flute tune. That restless triple patter runs through almost the whole movement.
2. Compare the last chord with the first chord of the symphony. The key is the same, A, but the major has turned to minor.

### Threads

- **Light and shade.** The outer movements are bright and fast; the middle two are grave and gentle. Many writers hear the middle movements as more "northern" in mood, less Italian than the outer ones.
- **Never finished.** Mendelssohn conducted the symphony only at its premiere and then kept revising it. The version heard almost everywhere today is the one he first performed in 1833.
- **Woodwinds.** From the patter of the opening to the flutes' saltarello, the woodwinds carry much of the symphony's colour.
