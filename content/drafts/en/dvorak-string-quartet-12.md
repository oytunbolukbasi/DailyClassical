### The big picture

- Dvořák spent the summer of 1893 in Spillville, Iowa, a village of Czech immigrants that his secretary Josef Kovařík came from. He arrived in early June and wrote to a friend how happy the family was there, away from busy New York.
- He sketched the quartet in three days and finished it in thirteen more, writing at the end "Thank God! I am content. It was fast." It came just after the *New World* Symphony.
- He wanted, he said, to write something "very melodious and straightforward", with "dear Papa Haydn" before his eyes. The four players often play simple tunes over simple accompaniments.
- Almost every theme uses the [[pentatonic scale]], five notes instead of seven. It gives the music its open, folk-like sound, though Dvořák used it in Czech-flavoured music too.
- In one line: a viola's open-air song (I), a lament in the minor (II), a quick dance with a bird calling overhead (III), a chugging, train-like finale (IV).

### I. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** The violins set up a soft, rippling shimmer and the viola, usually the quietest voice in a quartet, sings the first theme. It is a cheerful [[sonata form]] movement with a gentler second tune, a busier middle and a bright ending.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, open-air: a simple, rising tune on the viola, built on five notes.
- Theme 2, tender: a gentler melody for the first violin, decorated with little turns.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The violins ripple softly on one unchanging chord; the viola enters with a cheerful, rising tune | Theme 1, on the viola |
| ≈ 0:30 | The first violin takes the tune up high; the music begins to move to new chords | The theme passes to the violin |
| ≈ 1:30 | A gentler, lyrical melody on the first violin, decorated with little turns | Theme 2, in A major |
| ≈ 3:00 | The rippling and the viola tune return | The [[exposition]] is repeated (check by ear) |
| ≈ 5:00–6:30 | Darker and busier; short pieces of the themes are passed around, and at the end the instruments come in one after another with the same tune | The [[development]], ending in a [[fugato]] |
| ≈ 6:45 | The opening tune returns, then the cello sings alone for a moment | The [[recapitulation]]; the cello bridges to Theme 2 |
| Last 40 seconds | Quick, bright and loud, all four players together | The [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. In the first seconds, listen to the chord under the viola. It does not change for a dozen bars, which gives the opening its calm, open feel.
2. Sing the viola's tune back to yourself and count its notes. There are only five different ones.

**In this recording.** The Pavel Haas Quartet, from Prague, recorded the quartet in the Rudolfinum in June 2010, and the disc was named the Gramophone Recording of the Year in 2011. On the Spotify album Quartet No. 13 comes first; the *American* is tracks 5–8.

### II. Lento

**Summary.** A slow, aching song in D minor. The first violin sings it over a soft, pulsing accompaniment; the cello repeats it. A more urgent middle section rises and falls, and at the end the cello sings the song again over the thinnest of accompaniments.

**Main ideas**

- The lament: a long, sad melody built on the same five-note scale, now in the minor.
- The pulse: second violin and viola rocking gently underneath.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A soft, rocking pulse in second violin and viola; then the first violin sings a long, sad melody | The main theme. Some have linked it to an African American spiritual or a Kickapoo tune; neither link is proven |
| ≈ 1:15 | The cello takes the melody, deep and warm | The theme in the cello |
| ≈ 2:30–4:30 | The music becomes more urgent; pieces of the melody climb and swell | The middle section |
| ≈ 5:15 | The cello sings the melody again over a thin accompaniment, part bowed and part plucked | The theme returns |
| Last 30 seconds | The music fades away | A quiet ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the violin and the cello in the first minutes. It is the same melody; in the cello it sounds older and darker.

### III. Molto vivace

**Summary.** A short, quick dance full of off-beat accents. High above, the first violin imitates a bird Dvořák heard in Iowa. The dance alternates twice with a slower, smoother version of itself in the minor.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a bouncing, slightly lopsided tune, with accents in unexpected places.
- The bird: a short, insistent call high in the first violin.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A loud, bouncing tune in second violin and cello together, full of off-beat accents | The dance |
| ≈ 0:15 | A short, chattering call high up on the first violin | Dvořák's birdsong. He thought it was a scarlet tanager; it was probably a red-eyed vireo |
| ≈ 0:50 | The music slows to half speed and turns smooth and sad, in the minor | The dance tune transformed, in F minor |
| ≈ 1:40 | The bouncing dance returns, bird call and all | The dance again |
| ≈ 2:15 | The minor version again, now with a pulsing, three-to-a-beat accompaniment | The slower section, varied |
| ≈ 3:10 | The dance one last time | The ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the bird. It interrupts the dance from above, as if it did not care about the rhythm below.

### IV. Finale: Vivace ma non troppo

**Summary.** A cheerful [[rondo]]: one main tune keeps coming back between contrasting episodes. Underneath, the second violin and viola keep up a steady chugging rhythm that many hear as a train, one of Dvořák's passions.

**Main ideas**

- The chug: a steady, repeated rhythm in second violin and viola.
- The rondo tune: a bright, skipping melody on the first violin, five notes again.
- The hymn: a quiet, slow, [[chorale]]-like episode in the middle.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Second violin and viola start a steady chugging rhythm; the first violin tries out fragments | The introduction |
| ≈ 0:25 | The first violin plays the bright, skipping main tune | The rondo theme |
| ≈ 0:55 | A more lyrical tune, in a new key | The first episode |
| ≈ 1:40 | The main tune returns | The rondo theme again |
| ≈ 2:15–3:15 | Everything slows and softens; the four players move together like a quiet hymn | The middle episode, a chorale |
| ≈ 3:30 | The chugging and the main tune come back | The rondo theme again |
| ≈ 4:15 | The lyrical tune again | The first episode returns |

**Things to notice**

1. Keep your ear on the second violin and viola. Their chugging rhythm hardly stops, and once you hear it as a train it is hard to unhear.

### Threads

- **Five notes.** The [[pentatonic scale]] runs through all four movements and gives the quartet its open, simple sound.
- **Every voice has a turn.** The viola opens the first movement, the cello sings the Lento, and the inner voices drive the finale.
- **America through Czech ears.** Written in America and named after it, but the critic Paul Griffiths argued that the only American thing about it is where it was written.
- **Haydn's simplicity.** Dvořák aimed for clear tunes and clear forms, and said that thinking of Haydn made it all turn out simply.
