### The big picture

- Bach most likely wrote the six cello suites between 1717 and 1723, when he was music director at the court of Prince Leopold in Köthen. No copy in his own hand survives. The main source is a copy made by his second wife, Anna Magdalena Bach, headed *Suites à Violoncello Solo senza Basso*: suites for cello alone, without a bass.
- A [[suite]] is a set of dances in one key. Each of Bach's six opens with a prelude and then follows the same order: [[allemande]], [[courante]], [[sarabande]], a pair of lighter dances (in this suite, two minuets) and a [[gigue]] to finish.
- A cello mostly plays one note at a time, yet Bach makes it sound like several instruments. Broken chords, low notes that ring on under the tune and leaps between registers let the ear fill in a bass line and a harmony that are only hinted at.
- For a long time the suites were little known and rarely played in public; many saw them as studies. The cellist Pablo Casals found an edition in a Barcelona shop as a boy of 13, played them in public for years and made the first complete recording: Suite No. 1 was recorded in Paris in June 1938.
- In one line: a ripple of broken chords (I), a flowing conversation (II), a quick run (III), a slow dance in chords (IV), two minuets, the second in the minor (V), a jig to end (VI).

### I. Prélude

**Summary.** The famous one. A single pattern of broken chords, rising and falling, repeats bar after bar while the harmony changes underneath. Halfway through the music comes to rest, then sets off again, more restless, and builds through a long climb over one repeated low note to a full, ringing close.

**Main ideas**

- The ripple: each bar is one chord played as a wave of notes, starting from a low note, rising and falling back.
- The climb: near the end, a low note sounds again and again while the notes above it creep upwards, step by step.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A wave of notes rising from the cello's low G and falling back, the same shape in every bar | The home chord, G major, spread out as an [[arpeggio]] on the open strings |
| ≈ 0:35 | The wave keeps its shape but the chords begin to darken and lead away from home | The harmony moves towards new keys |
| ≈ 1:25 | The music settles on a chord and seems to pause for breath | Halfway: a resting point on D, the key a fifth above G |
| ≈ 1:30–2:00 | The pattern breaks up into runs and wider leaps; the mood is more searching | The second half wanders further from home |
| ≈ 2:00 | One low note keeps sounding between the moving notes, while the line above it climbs slowly upwards | A long [[pedal note]] on D: the tension builds |
| ≈ 2:30 | The climb reaches the top; broad chords sweep across all four strings | The climax |
| Last 15 seconds | A last, full G major chord | Home again |

**Things to notice**

1. In the first bars, listen for the lowest note of each wave. Those notes, one per bar, make a slow bass line of their own.
2. Before the climax, notice how often the same low D returns, like a drone under the rising line.

### II. Allemande

**Summary.** A moderate dance in four, though nobody would dance to it now: a continuous, flowing line of quick notes, with a few chords for weight. Like every dance in the suite it has two halves, and each half is played twice.

**Main ideas**

- The flowing line: running notes that move mostly by step, with an upbeat into each phrase.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A short upbeat, then a calm, unbroken stream of notes | The first half of the [[allemande]] |
| ≈ 1:05 | The opening comes back | The first half is repeated |

### III. Courante

**Summary.** "Running." A quicker dance in three, full of energy, with fast notes that leap across the strings. Two halves again, each repeated.

**Main ideas**

- The run: quick notes that bounce between low and high, with a firm accent at the start of each bar.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A brisk, bright line in three beats | The first half of the [[courante]] |
| ≈ 0:40 | The opening again | The first half is repeated |

**Things to notice**

1. Notice how the line jumps between a low note and a high one, so that one cello seems to play two parts in turn.

### IV. Sarabande

**Summary.** A slow, dignified dance in three, played in chords. In a [[sarabande]] the weight often falls on the second beat of the bar, which gives it a stately, lingering step.

**Main ideas**

- The chords: two or three notes sounded at once, as [[double stop]]s and broken chords, with a slow tune on top.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Slow, full chords; the music leans on the middle beat of each bar | The first half of the [[sarabande]] |
| ≈ 0:50 | The opening phrase again | The repeat |
| ≈ 1:40 | The tune rises higher and moves into other keys | The second half |
| ≈ 2:30 | The second half again | The repeat |
| Near the end | A soft, final chord on G | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the beats in three. You may feel the second beat as the strongest, which is the mark of a sarabande.

### V. Menuet I – Menuet II

**Summary.** Two minuets, light dances in three. The first is bright and in G major; the second turns to G minor and is one of only five movements in all six suites written as a single line, with no chords at all. Then the first minuet returns.

**Main ideas**

- Menuet I: a cheerful tune with chords on the strong beats.
- Menuet II: a quieter, more wistful tune in the minor, one note at a time.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A graceful tune in three, with some chords | Menuet I, in G major |
| ≈ 0:30 | The tune moves on, then comes back to its start | The second half of Menuet I, with repeats |
| ≈ 1:20 | The mood darkens: a gentle tune in the minor, with no chords | Menuet II, in G minor |
| ≈ 2:45 | The bright first tune again | Menuet I returns, usually without its repeats |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the two minuets. The first has chords; the second is one bare line, and it sounds more private.

### VI. Gigue

**Summary.** A fast, skipping jig in groups of three notes, the usual way to end a suite. It is short and cheerful, and over in two minutes.

**Main ideas**

- The jig: a bouncing long-short rhythm, quick and light.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A quick, skipping tune in a long-short rhythm | The [[gigue]] |
| ≈ 0:20 | The first part again | The repeat |
| ≈ 0:40 | The tune moves into new keys and grows busier | The second half |
| Near the end | A bright, firm close on G | The suite ends where it began |

**Things to notice**

1. Tap the beat: the notes fall in threes, long-short, the lilt of a jig.

**In this recording.** Pierre Fournier recorded all six suites for Deutsche Grammophon's early-music label, Archiv Produktion, in the Beethovensaal in Hanover in December 1960. The set has been reissued many times since it first appeared in 1961.

### Threads

- **One instrument, many voices.** Bach writes for a single cello, but almost every movement suggests a bass, a tune and the harmony between them. The listener's ear does the rest.
- **The open G.** The cello's two lowest strings are C and G, and this suite in G major keeps returning to the resonance of the open G string, from the first note of the prelude to the last chord of the gigue.
- **A dance suite.** Allemande, courante, sarabande, minuets and gigue were real dances of the time, in a fixed order. Bach keeps their rhythms but turns them into music for listening.
