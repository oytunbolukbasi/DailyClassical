### The big picture

- The Philharmonic Society of London asked Saint-Saëns for a new work, and he wrote the symphony in the first months of 1886, at the same time as his comic *Carnival of the Animals*. He conducted the premiere himself at St James's Hall, London, on 19 May 1886; in the first half of the same concert he played Beethoven's Fourth Piano Concerto.
- He meant to dedicate it to Franz Liszt, his friend and supporter. Liszt died in July 1886, and the score was published "to the memory of Franz Liszt".
- Saint-Saëns wrote the symphony in two movements, each in two parts, and said it still "contains, in principle, the four traditional movements". This recording, like many, gives the four parts as four tracks, and they follow each other with hardly a break.
- The organ plays in only two of the four parts, and there is a piano too, sometimes played by two pianists at once. One theme runs through the whole work and keeps changing character, a method Saint-Saëns learned from Liszt. Some hear the opening notes of the chant [[Dies irae]] in it; others think the likeness is chance.
- "I gave everything to it I was able to give," Saint-Saëns said. "What I have here accomplished, I will never achieve again." He never wrote another symphony.
- In one line: a sighing introduction and restless strings (I), a calm hymn over the organ (II), a fierce scherzo with flashing piano (III), the organ's blazing C major finale (IV).

### I. Adagio – Allegro moderato

**Summary.** A short, slow introduction of sighing woodwind phrases, then a restless, nervous Allegro. The quick, repeated notes in the strings are the symphony's main theme, and they will come back in many disguises. The movement does not end: it fades away into the slow movement that follows.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: quick, nervous repeated notes in the strings, turning around a few notes like a worried thought.
- The second theme: a smoother, singing melody that brings some calm.

### II. Poco adagio

**Summary.** The organ enters for the first time, on a single soft note, and the strings sing a long, calm hymn over it. This is the slow movement of the symphony, though Saint-Saëns joined it to the first. It is warm and still, and it fades into silence at the end.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: a slow, broad melody for the strings in [[unison]], over quiet held chords on the organ.

### III. Allegro moderato – Presto

**Summary.** Part Two opens with a fierce, stamping [[scherzo]] in C minor. Twice it gives way to a brilliant *Presto* in C major, where the piano adds glittering scales. Then the energy drains away into a quiet, searching passage that prepares the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: an aggressive, stamping figure in the strings, a hardened form of the main theme.
- The *Presto*: fast, light music in C major, with rushing scales on the piano.

### IV. Maestoso – Allegro

**Summary.** A huge C major chord on the full organ opens the finale. The restless theme of the first movement has become a broad, triumphant hymn, sung by strings and organ with brass fanfares. After a busy, fugue-like passage and a brief pastoral moment, the music builds to a long, blazing ending.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: the symphony's main theme, now slow, broad and in C major.

### Threads

- **One theme, many faces.** The restless figure of the first movement returns as the stamping scherzo and finally as the finale's hymn. Saint-Saëns learned this [[thematic transformation]] from Liszt, the symphony's dedicatee.
- **Dark to light.** The symphony moves from C minor to C major, the same path as Beethoven's Fifth.
- **The organ held back.** Silent for the first ten minutes, then soft and hushed, the organ is kept for the moment the finale begins.
- **Four in two.** Saint-Saëns joined his four movements in two pairs: the first fades into the slow movement, the scherzo grows into the finale.
