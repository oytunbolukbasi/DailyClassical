### The big picture

- Liszt finished the sonata in Weimar, where he was court music director; the manuscript is dated 2 February 1853. He had spent the 1830s and 1840s as the most famous touring pianist in Europe.
- He dedicated it to Robert Schumann, in return for Schumann's *Fantasie* in C, which had been dedicated to him. When the printed copy reached Schumann's house in May 1854, Schumann had already been taken to the asylum at Endenich.
- It is one movement of about half an hour, but it holds a whole sonata inside it: a fast opening, a slow movement in the middle, a quick fugue that works like a [[scherzo]], and a finale. Almost everything grows from three or four short [[motif]]s that keep changing character.
- Hans von Bülow gave the first public performance, in Berlin in January 1857. Critics were hostile: Eduard Hanslick wrote that anyone who heard it and found it beautiful was beyond help. The story goes that the young Brahms fell asleep when Liszt played it to him in 1853. Today it is one of the summits of the piano repertoire.
- In one line: a dark question and a defiant answer (0:00), a hymn and a love song (≈ 3:00–5:00), a still, glowing slow section (≈ 12:15), a whispered fugue and a race to the top (≈ 19:35), and a quiet ending (last three minutes).

### I. Lento assai – Allegro energico – Andante sostenuto – Allegro energico – Lento assai

**Summary.** One unbroken half hour for one player. The sonata opens with a soft, sinking scale and two angry ideas, and then spends its whole length transforming them: the same few notes become a hymn, a love song, a whispered fugue and, at the very end, a farewell. The big shape is [[sonata form]], with the slow movement and the scherzo set inside it. Because it is a single track, the times below are your map.

**Main ideas**

- The falling scale: after two soft, detached notes in the bass, a slow scale sinks downward, quiet and uncertain. It returns at every turning point.
- The defiant leap: loud octaves that jump up and fall in a jagged line, *Allegro energico*.
- The hammer blow: one note repeated low in the bass, then a quick, mocking turn. Liszt called it *Hammerschlag*.
- The hymn: a broad, solemn melody in full chords, marked *Grandioso*, in D major.

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the hammer blow. It begins as a sneer in the bass, becomes a love song at ≈ 4:30, and at the end is only a soft knock under quiet chords.
2. Listen to the very first and very last minutes. The sonata ends with the sounds it began with: the falling scale and soft, low notes.

**In this recording.** Zimerman recorded the sonata in Copenhagen in February and March 1990. On the Spotify album it is a single track (track 1), followed by four of Liszt's late pieces. On the CD the track carried index marks at the start of the slow section and of the fugue, which is why those two stops are the safest guides.

### Threads

- **A few notes, many faces.** Nearly every theme in the sonata is one of the opening ideas in disguise. Liszt's name for this way of working was the transformation of themes.
- **Four in one.** Fast, slow, scherzo, finale: the shape of a classical sonata, folded into a single span with no breaks.
- **The silence after the storm.** The loudest climax, near the end, is followed not by triumph but by stillness.
