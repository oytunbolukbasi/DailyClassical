### The big picture

- Ravel wrote the quartet in Paris between December 1902 and April 1903, when he was 28. It is dedicated "to my dear teacher Gabriel Fauré", his composition teacher at the Paris Conservatoire.
- It was first played on 5 March 1904 in Paris by the Heymann Quartet, at a concert of the Société nationale de musique. Fauré was not much taken with it. Debussy was: on the eve of the premiere he wrote to Ravel urging him to change nothing.
- The plan follows Debussy's String Quartet of ten years earlier: four movements, a plucked [[scherzo]] in second place, themes that return across the work. But the sound is Ravel's own: clearer, cooler, and more tightly built.
- Critics were divided. One said it bore "an incredible resemblance" to Debussy; another called Ravel "one of the masters of tomorrow". It has remained a standard work of the chamber music repertoire.
- In one line: a gentle song in sonata form (I), a plucked dance with a slow, wistful heart (II), a dreamy night piece (III), a storm in five beats that calls back the opening (IV).

### I. Allegro moderato – Très doux

**Summary.** Ravel marks the opening *très doux*, "very sweet", and the whole movement keeps that soft, glowing tone. It is a classical [[sonata form]] with two themes. The surprise comes near the end, when the second theme returns transformed by a change in just one of the four parts.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a long, calm tune in the first violin that rises and falls in one smooth arc, while the other three move quietly in step beneath it.
- Theme 2: a sadder, more inward melody, played by the first violin and the viola together, two octaves apart.

### II. Assez vif – Très rythmé

**Summary.** A [[scherzo]] that opens with all four players plucking their strings. The rhythm is the game: the music is written in 6/8 and 3/4 at the same time, so the beat seems to shift every few bars. In the middle the dance stops for a long, slow, muted song led by the viola.

**Main ideas**

- The plucked dance: a quick, dry tune in A minor, all [[pizzicato]]. Some writers hear in it the Javanese gamelan, which Ravel heard in Paris in 1889; others hear Spain.
- The bowed melody: a smooth, singing line that floats above the plucking.
- The slow song: a wistful tune, first in the viola, with the instruments [[muted]].

### III. Très lent

**Summary.** A slow, dreamy movement with the instruments [[muted]]. It is restless rather than calm: Ravel changes the tempo again and again, and the music keeps drifting away from its key. It is linked by its themes to the first movement.

**Main ideas**

- The viola's song: a long, sorrowful line over soft chords.
- The rocking figure: quick, quiet repeated notes played over the fingerboard, under a rising melody.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the chords in the opening minute. Ravel moves them in parallel, which the rules of harmony forbid, and they sound open and old, almost like a chant.

### IV. Vif et agité

**Summary.** A finale like a squall: tremolos, sudden accents and an uneven five-beat rhythm. It is loosely a [[rondo]], with calmer episodes between the outbursts. In one quiet passage the gentle first theme of the first movement floats back, tying the whole work together.

**Main ideas**

- The storm: fast, sawing repeated notes ([[tremolo]]) in groups of five, loud and urgent.
- The long melody: a broad, singing line that rises above the agitation.
- The memory: the first movement's opening tune, very soft.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Loud, sawing repeated notes in all four instruments, in a lopsided five-beat rhythm | The opening storm, in 5/8 |
| ≈ 0:35 | The storm drops; a long, singing melody rises over a rippling accompaniment | The calmer second idea, now in a slower 5/4 |
| ≈ 1:45 | Very quiet, played over the fingerboard: the gentle tune from the very start of the quartet | The first movement's Theme 1 recalled, an example of [[cyclic form]] |
| ≈ 2:20 | The storm breaks out again | The opening material returns |
| ≈ 3:20 | All four players hammer out the melody at full strength | The climax |
| ≈ 4:05 | A brief hush, very soft | The last calm before the end |
| Last 15 seconds | A final rush and loud chords in F major | The close |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the beats in the opening: five to a bar, grouped unevenly. It is what makes the storm feel off balance.
2. When the first movement's tune returns, notice how far away it sounds, played softly over the fingerboard.

### Threads

- **Four equal voices.** No player simply accompanies for long. The viola leads the slow song in the scherzo and opens the slow movement; the cello's single change transforms the first movement's second theme.
- **The opening tune.** The first movement's Theme 1 echoes in the slow movement and returns, far away, in the finale.
- **Clarity.** Ravel takes Debussy's plan but writes with sharper outlines: clear phrases, classical forms and precise sounds, plucked, muted or played over the fingerboard.
