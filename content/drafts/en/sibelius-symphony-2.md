### The big picture

- Sibelius began the symphony in 1901 in Rapallo, on the Italian coast, where his friend Baron Axel Carpelan had raised the money for him to spend the winter. He finished it in Finland early in 1902.
- He conducted the first performance himself in Helsinki on 8 March 1902. It was a great success. He then revised it, and the revised version was first played in Stockholm on 10 November 1903, conducted by his brother-in-law Armas Järnefelt.
- Finland was then ruled by Russia, and Finnish language and culture were under pressure. Many listeners heard the grand finale as a picture of the struggle for independence, and the work was even called the "Symphony of Independence". Whether Sibelius meant it that way is still debated.
- The symphony grows from tiny cells. A three-note rising figure heard at the very start returns in many disguises, and becomes the great theme of the finale.
- In one line: a pastoral morning that keeps breaking into fragments (I), a dark, troubled ballad (II), a furious scherzo that runs without a break (III) into a triumphant finale (IV).

### I. Allegretto

**Summary.** It begins like a fresh spring morning, with a gentle pulse in the strings and a cheerful tune in the woodwinds. Then the music does something unusual: instead of building long themes, Sibelius throws out short fragments, lets them fall silent, and gradually fits them together. Only at the climax do the pieces lock into a whole.

**Main ideas**

- The pulse: the strings repeat a soft, rising three-note figure, like breathing.
- The woodwind tune: a bright, folk-like melody for oboes and clarinets.
- The horn call: a soft, warm answer from the horns.

### II. Tempo andante, ma rubato

**Summary.** A dark ballad with sudden storms. It opens with a long, plucked walk in the low strings, over which the bassoons sing a mournful tune. Twice the music builds to a violent climax, and twice it is answered by a calm, glowing melody in a far-off key. In a sketch for this music Sibelius wrote of a stranger who turns out to be Death.

**Main ideas**

- The walking bass: a long line plucked by the double basses and cellos.
- The bassoon theme: a sad, low melody, first played by two bassoons.
- The consolation: a soft, radiant melody in F-sharp major for the strings, marked "Christus" in Sibelius's sketch.

### III. Vivacissimo – Lento e suave

**Summary.** A furious, breathless [[scherzo]], with the strings racing in fast repeated notes. Twice it stops dead for a slow, tender oboe melody. After the second time the music does not return to the scherzo: it climbs, slowly and steadily, straight into the finale without a break.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: whirling, rapid notes in the strings, with sharp accents from the woodwinds.
- The oboe tune: a slow, gentle melody that begins on a single repeated note.

### IV. Finale: Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The arrival. A broad, rising theme in D major, answered by trumpet calls, bursts in at the end of the long climb. Its opposite is a long, mournful passage in which a scale keeps turning over in the low strings while the woodwinds lament. The two alternate, until the mournful music itself is turned into the final blaze.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a broad, rising melody in the strings, built from the three-note cell of the first movement, with trumpet fanfares in reply.
- The second theme: a scale figure going round and round in the violas and cellos, under a sad melody in the woodwinds.

### Threads

- **From fragments to a whole.** Sibelius builds his themes from small cells and lets them come together slowly. The rising three-note figure from the first bar becomes the big tune of the finale.
- **Darkness and light.** The storms of the slow movement and the lament of the finale are answered by the D major blaze at the end.
- **No break.** The scherzo runs straight into the finale, as in Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, so that the last two movements are heard as one long climb.
- **Italy and Finland.** The first ideas came in Rapallo; the work became, in Finland, a symbol of hope during years of Russian pressure.
