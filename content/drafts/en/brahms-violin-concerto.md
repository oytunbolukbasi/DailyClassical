### The big picture

- Brahms wrote the concerto in the summer of 1878 at Pörtschach, on a lake in southern Austria, for his friend Joseph Joachim, the leading violinist of the day. Joachim advised on the solo part and gave the premiere in Leipzig on 1 January 1879, with Brahms conducting.
- It was planned in four movements. Brahms dropped the two middle ones and replaced them with what he called a "feeble Adagio".
- The orchestra is a full partner, not a background. A joke that the piece was written "against the violin" is attributed both to the conductor Hans von Bülow and to the violinist Joseph Hellmesberger. The virtuoso Pablo de Sarasate, the story goes, refused to play it because the oboe has the only tune in the Adagio.
- Brahms wrote no [[cadenza]] for the first movement. Almost every violinist plays Joachim's. The finale's dance has a Hungarian flavour, a nod to Joachim, who was Hungarian.
- In one line: a broad, pastoral drama with a stormy entrance (I), an oboe's song (II), a Hungarian dance (III).

### I. Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** A long movement in [[sonata form]] with a [[double exposition]]: the orchestra presents the main tunes for more than two minutes before the violin plays a note. The mood is broad and pastoral, but the violin arrives like a storm.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a calm tune that climbs up and down the notes of a D major chord, first in low strings, bassoons and horns.
- The closing theme: a fierce, marching tune in the minor, with a dotted long-short rhythm.
- Theme 3: a tender melody that only the violin plays, kept back for its own exposition.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A slow song in three parts. The oboe has the melody first, over the wind section alone, and the violin only joins after more than two minutes. The middle part turns passionate and minor.

**Main ideas**

- The main melody: long, calm and singing, first on the oboe over bassoons, horns and the other winds.
- The middle section: a restless, richly decorated violin line in F-sharp minor.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Bassoons and horns, then the oboe begins a long, slow melody; only wind instruments play | The main theme. No strings yet, and no violin |

| ≈ 3:30 | The winds slip suddenly into a far-off key; the violin grows restless | The turn to the middle section |
| ≈ 3:59–5:28 | A passionate violin line in the minor, ever more decorated | The middle section, in F-sharp minor |

| Near the end | The violin holds the melody to a quiet close over soft wind chords | The [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the opening two minutes as a piece for wind band. Brahms keeps the strings silent until the violin is about to enter.

### III. Allegro giocoso, ma non troppo vivace – Poco più presto

**Summary.** A fast [[rondo]] with a Hungarian flavour. The violin opens with a bold dance tune played on two strings at once, and the movement ends with the tune turned into a swinging march.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a stamping, Hungarian-style dance, played by the violin in [[double stop]]s.
- First episode: rising scales in a dotted rhythm, answered by falling scales in the low strings.
- Second episode: a gentle, pastoral dance in three beats.

### Threads

- **A friend's concerto.** Joachim shaped the solo part, played the premiere and wrote the cadenza; the finale's Hungarian dance is a tribute to him.
- **The orchestra as partner.** The orchestra plays the first two minutes of the first movement and the oboe opens the Adagio. The violin is often accompanist as much as soloist.
- **Calm and storm.** Each movement sets a calm, singing idea against a fierce one: the pastoral opening and the stormy entrance, the oboe's song and the minor middle section, the dance and its rhapsodic interruption.
