### The big picture

- Ravel wrote the concerto between 1929 and 1931, at the same time as his Concerto for the Left Hand for the pianist Paul Wittgenstein. It was his second-to-last work.
- He said he wanted a real concerto, brilliant and showing off the soloist, without trying to be profound, and that his models were Mozart and Saint-Saëns. Two other influences can be heard: jazz, which he had enjoyed in Paris and on his American tour of 1928, and the Basque music of his mother's country. He was born in Ciboure, a Basque town on the French coast.
- He meant to play the solo part himself, but poor health made him hand it to Marguerite Long, to whom it is dedicated. She gave the first performance on 14 January 1932 at the Salle Pleyel in Paris, with Ravel conducting the Lamoureux Orchestra. Within days they set off on a tour of sixteen European cities.
- The orchestra is small, almost a chamber orchestra, with one each of most wind instruments and a busy percussion section, including a whip.
- In one line: a whip crack, a festival and the blues (I), a long, calm song (II), a mad dash with the same four chords at each end (III).

### I. Allegramente

**Summary.** A crack of the whip, and a piccolo whistles a perky tune over the piano's glittering patterns. The movement brings together five themes: the first suggests a Basque folk tune, the second Spain, and the others the blues and jazz. Where a single [[cadenza]] might be expected, Ravel writes three: first for the harp, then for the woodwinds, and finally for the piano.

**Main ideas**

- The festival tune: a perky melody for piccolo, then trumpet, over rippling piano patterns in two keys at once.
- The blues: a slow, swaying melody with "blue" notes, first sung by the piano alone.
- The jazz trumpet: a brassy, bending tune that sounds like a dance band.

1. In the piano cadenza, listen to the right hand. It plays the melody and a trill at the same time.
2. Notice the jazz colours: muted trumpet, sliding notes, the "blue" notes that slip between major and minor.

**In this recording.** Martha Argerich recorded the concerto in Berlin in 1967, with Claudio Abbado and the Berlin Philharmonic; both were early in their careers. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 4–6, after Prokofiev's Third Concerto, which was its partner on the original LP.

### II. Adagio assai

**Summary.** After the fun of the first movement, stillness. The piano plays alone for about three minutes: one long, calm melody that never repeats itself, over a steady, gently swaying accompaniment. Ravel took the slow movement of Mozart's Clarinet Quintet as his model, and said the melody nearly killed him. Then the orchestra enters, the music clouds over for a while, and the cor anglais brings back the melody while the piano decorates it.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: a long, unbroken line in the right hand, slow and calm, like a stately dance.
- The accompaniment: a steady swaying in the left hand that seems to pull against the melody's beat.

### III. Presto

**Summary.** The shortest movement and the wildest. Four sharp chords launch a whirlwind of fast notes for the piano, with shrieking clarinets and piccolo, brassy fanfares and a trombone that seems to bray like a donkey. Some hear the circus or a fairground. The same four chords end it.

**Main ideas**

- The four chords: a sharp signal from the orchestra, heard at the start and the end.
- The toccata: rapid, quiet notes running through the piano part from start to finish.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. The piano almost never stops in this movement. Listen to how soft its rapid notes can be, even at this speed.

### Threads

- **Fun first.** Ravel said he wanted to entertain, like Mozart, rather than to be profound. Only the slow movement breaks the spell.
- **Jazz and Basque.** Blues and dance-band colours sit beside a Basque-flavoured opening, a mix that is all Ravel's own.
- **A small orchestra.** With one of each wind instrument and a whip, the orchestra plays like a band of soloists, and every colour stands out.
