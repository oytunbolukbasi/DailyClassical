### The big picture

- Tchaikovsky wrote the concerto in 1878 in Clarens, on Lake Geneva in Switzerland, where he had gone to recover after the collapse of his marriage the year before. It was finished in about a month.
- His pupil, the violinist Iosif Kotek, joined him there. They played through Lalo's *Symphonie espagnole* together, which may have sparked the idea, and since Tchaikovsky was not a violinist, Kotek helped him shape the solo part. Tchaikovsky threw out his first slow movement and wrote the Canzonetta in its place; the discarded movement survives as a piece for violin and piano.
- He dedicated the concerto to the violinist Leopold Auer, but Auer would not play it, and the planned premiere was cancelled. Adolph Brodsky gave the first performance in Vienna on 4 December 1881, with Hans Richter conducting, and the dedication went to him instead.
- The critic Eduard Hanslick called the concerto long and pretentious, and the finale "odorously Russian". Auer later played and taught it, in his own edited version.
- In one line: a sweet tune that grows into fireworks (I), a sad song for muted violin (II), a whirling Russian dance (III).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** It begins quietly, almost shyly, and the soloist enters with a few searching phrases before singing the main tune. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but what you notice is how the two lyrical themes are spun out into more and more brilliant decoration. Before the themes return, the violin plays a long [[cadenza]] alone, ending on a [[trill]].

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: a gentle, hesitant phrase in the violins of the orchestra. As in the First Piano Concerto, this opening idea never returns.
- Theme 1: a sweet, lilting, [[cantabile]] tune for the solo violin, in D major.
- Theme 2: a more tender, yearning melody, high on the solo violin.

### II. Canzonetta: Andante

**Summary.** A "little song" in G minor, sad and simple. A few soft woodwind chords open it, like a hymn. Then the violin, with a mute on its bridge, sings a long, lonely melody. A brighter middle section passes, the song returns, and soft chords in the orchestra lead without a pause into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The woodwind opening: a few slow, soft chords, like a hymn.
- The song: a sad, simple melody on the muted solo violin, sweet and melancholy at once.

### III. Finale: Allegro vivacissimo

**Summary.** A Russian dance that keeps getting faster. After a loud start and a short, free passage for the violin, the soloist launches a quick, stamping tune, played low on its thickest string for a rough, earthy sound. A second, swaying tune is played over a drone in the bass, like a village band, and is varied several times. The dance wins in the end, at top speed.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a quick, stamping tune, first played on the violin's lowest string, gritty and resonant.
- The village tune: slower and swaying, over a drone, in A major.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the bass during the second theme. The low strings hold one note, like the drone of a bagpipe or a village fiddler's open string.
2. The tempo creeps up. The final pages are much faster than the opening dance.

**Cuts.** For decades many violinists, following Auer's edition, made small cuts in this finale, so its length varies from recording to recording.

### Threads

- **Song and dance.** Every movement is either a song (the lyrical themes of I, the Canzonetta) or a dance (the finale and the orchestra's grand version of the main tune).
- **The violin in many voices.** Sweet in the first theme, veiled with a mute in the Canzonetta, rough and rustic in the finale.
- **Russian colour.** What Hanslick complained of is now part of the attraction: the drone, the folk-like tunes, the rough low string and the accelerating dance of the finale.
