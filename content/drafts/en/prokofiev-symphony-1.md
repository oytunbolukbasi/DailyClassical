### The big picture

- Prokofiev started the symphony in 1916 and finished it on 10 September 1917, the year of the two Russian revolutions. He wrote much of it in the summer of 1917 in the countryside, away from the street fighting in Petrograd.
- It was an experiment: for the first time he composed without a piano, working the music out in his head. He chose a simple, classical model to make the exercise easier.
- The model was Haydn, whose music he had studied in his conducting classes. In Prokofiev's own account, he wrote as Haydn might have done had he lived in Prokofiev's day. The nickname "Classical" is his own.
- The orchestra is the size Haydn would have known: pairs of woodwind, horns and trumpets, timpani and strings. The whole symphony lasts about a quarter of an hour.
- Prokofiev himself conducted the premiere in Petrograd in April 1918, shortly before he left Russia. It became one of his most popular works.
- In one line: a fast, bright opening (I), a floating song (II), a stately dance with a stumble (III), a finale that never touches a minor chord (IV).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** A fast, light [[sonata form]] in the spirit of Haydn: two contrasting themes, a short [[development]] and a [[recapitulation]]. Prokofiev's own fingerprints are the sudden slips into unexpected keys, which the music corrects as if nothing had happened, and the comic leaps of the second theme.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: after a quick rising flourish, a bright, bouncing tune in the violins.
- Theme 2: a comic tune with enormous leaps in the violins, from very high to very low, over a tiptoeing bassoon.

1. Listen for the moments when the music seems to slip on a banana skin into a strange key, then steps straight back. That is Prokofiev winking at Haydn.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** A gentle slow movement. After a few soft chords, the first violins enter very high with a long, floating melody. The middle grows busier and louder, then the melody returns and the movement fades as it began.

- The accompaniment: soft, regular steps in the lower strings, like a slow walk.

### III. Gavotta: Non troppo allegro

**Summary.** The shortest movement, under a minute and a half: a gavotte, an old French court dance. It begins stately and polite, then swerves into far-off keys before landing safely home. A softer middle section sits over a held bass note, like a bagpipe.

**Main ideas**

- The gavotte: a dignified tune with big leaps and sudden swerves.
- The middle section: a gentle tune over a [[drone]].

### IV. Finale: Molto vivace

**Summary.** A finale at top speed, again in [[sonata form]]. Prokofiev threw away his first finale as too heavy and wrote this one so that, as he noted in his diary, it contains no minor chords at all. Only the second theme survives from the discarded version.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a whirling, scurrying tune in the strings.
- Theme 2: a cheeky, skipping tune in the woodwind, with the flute racing on top.

### Threads

- **Old forms, new tricks.** Every movement uses a form Haydn would recognise; every movement also has Prokofiev's sudden key-slips and wide leaps.
- **Small orchestra, sharp colours.** With no trombones or tuba, the woodwind solos, especially flute and bassoon, stand out clearly.
- **Neoclassical.** The symphony is often called an early example of [[neoclassicism]]: modern composers returning to the clarity and forms of the eighteenth century.
