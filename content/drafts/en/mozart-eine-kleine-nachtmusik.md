### The big picture

- Mozart entered the work in his own catalogue of compositions on 10 August 1787, in Vienna. He was in the middle of writing his opera *Don Giovanni* at the time.
- Nobody knows why he wrote it, who asked for it, or whether it was ever played in his lifetime. Most of his serenades were commissions, so it may have been one whose first performance simply went unrecorded.
- The famous name comes from that catalogue entry, which begins "Eine kleine Nacht-Musick". It means "a little night music", but Mozart was probably just noting that he had finished "a little serenade", not giving it a title.
- The same entry lists five movements, with a minuet and trio after the opening Allegro. That movement is lost; no one knows who removed it. The serenade was published only around 1827, long after Mozart's death, when his widow Constanze sold it to a publisher with a bundle of his manuscripts.
- It is written for five string players (two violins, viola, cello and double bass) but is usually played, as here, by a string orchestra.
- In one line: a brisk, sunny opening (I), a gentle song with a darker middle (II), a firm little dance (III), a quick, playful finale (IV).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The strings shoot up the notes of the home chord together, and the whole movement grows from that bright, confident gesture. It is a compact [[sonata form]]: two contrasting themes, a short development, and a return. Everything is clear and in proportion, and nothing outstays its welcome.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, the call: all the strings together, loud, leaping up the notes of the G major chord, then a quicker answer.
- Theme 2: a soft, graceful phrase that falls gently, with little turns, in D major.
- The chatter: a small tune on quick repeated notes with a trill, broken off by loud chords.

1. The first two bars are a single chord, spread out and climbing. Listen to how often the movement comes back to that shape.

On the Spotify album the serenade is tracks 1–4, followed by the *Serenata notturna*, K. 239, and the Divertimento in B-flat, K. 287.

### II. Romance: Andante

**Summary.** A slow, tender song. A [[romance]] was a gentle, song-like piece, and this one is a [[rondo]]: its main tune keeps coming back between two contrasting passages. The second of them turns to the minor and becomes restless, before the calm tune returns.

**Main ideas**

- The romance tune: a gentle melody in the first violins that always starts a little before the main beat, like a dance step.
- The light episode: quick, airy notes in the first violins over even, repeated chords.
- The minor episode: short, anxious phrases with little turns, over a fast, nervous murmur in the second violins and violas.

### III. Menuetto: Allegretto

**Summary.** A short, firm [[minuet]], the courtly dance in three beats, with a softer [[trio]] in the middle. The minuet stamps; the trio sings quietly.

**Main ideas**

- The minuet: loud, clear steps up the scale, in a strong three-beat rhythm.
- The trio: a smooth, soft melody in D major, marked *sotto voce* ("under the voice").

### IV. Rondo: Allegro

**Summary.** A quick, high-spirited finale. Mozart calls it a rondo, but it is built more like a [[sonata form]], with two themes, a development and a return. Its main tune is a cheeky little upbeat followed by repeated notes.

**Main ideas**

- The main tune: three quick rising notes, then repeated notes on one pitch; quiet and light.
- The loud answer: the whole group, firm and bright.

### Threads

- **A serenade, not a symphony.** Light music for an evening, written for five players. The ideas are short and clear, and every movement is easy to follow on a first hearing.
- **The missing minuet.** Mozart planned a second minuet after the first movement. Without it, the work has the four-movement shape of a small symphony.
- **Hidden shadows.** The minor-key middle of the Romance and the brief E-flat surprise of the finale are the only clouds, and both pass quickly.
