### The big picture

- Schubert wrote the quintet in the late summer or early autumn of 1828, alongside his last three piano sonatas, and finished it about two months before his death in November, at the age of 31. He offered it to the publisher Probst, mentioning that it would soon be rehearsed; Probst asked instead for songs and easier piano music.
- No performance in Schubert's lifetime is known. The work waited until 17 November 1850 for its first known public performance, at the Musikverein in Vienna, and was published in 1853. The manuscript is lost.
- Most string quintets of the time, including Mozart's in C major, add a second viola to the string quartet. Schubert adds a second cello. The lower half of the group becomes rich and dark, and either cello can soar into a high, singing register while the other holds the bass.
- It is long, almost an hour in this recording, and the first movement alone takes more than a third of it.
- In one line: a vast, glowing opening (I), a still, timeless song with a storm at its heart (II), a fierce hunt with a frozen trio (III), a Hungarian-flavoured dance that ends on a jarring note (IV).

### I. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** The quintet opens not with a tune but with a single long chord that swells and then turns sour. From this slow beginning grows a huge [[sonata form]], full of sudden changes of key. Its heart is the second theme: a long, tender duet for the two cellos.

**Main ideas**

- The opening chord: C major, held and swelling, then clouding over.
- Theme 2: a warm, lilting duet for the two cellos in E-flat major, over a soft accompaniment.
- The march: a firm, dotted rhythm that closes the exposition and drives the development.

1. Listen to the two cellos in the second theme. One plays the tune high, the other answers or doubles below: a sound no quartet can make.

**In this recording.** The Melos Quartett with Mstislav Rostropovich as second cellist recorded the quintet in the Tonhalle, Zurich, in September 1977. They take the repeat of the first movement's exposition (≈ 5:18), which is one reason the movement lasts over twenty minutes; the other is the slow, spacious tempo, especially in the Adagio.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A very slow movement in three parts. The outer parts, in E major, are almost motionless: three instruments sustain a quiet hymn while the first violin and the second cello add short, separate notes. In the middle a storm breaks out in F minor. When the calm returns, it carries a memory of the storm.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: long, soft chords in the second violin, viola and first cello.
- The commentary: little phrases in the first violin, and plucked notes in the second cello.
- The storm: a passionate, sobbing melody over restless, throbbing accompaniment and trills, in F minor.

### IV. Allegretto

**Summary.** A lively finale with a dance-like main theme that has a Hungarian accent. It is a [[rondo]] in which the main tune keeps coming back, and its mood keeps swinging between major and minor. The ending speeds up, and the last two notes are a famous shock.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a swinging, accented dance, half in minor, half in major.
- The second theme: a smoother, singing tune in the lower instruments.

### Threads

- **Two cellos.** The extra cello makes the low register dark and full, and frees one cello to sing high. It shapes the first movement's duet, the Adagio's plucked bass and the scherzo's huge sound.
- **A semitone away.** E major against F minor in the Adagio, C major against D-flat in the scherzo, and D-flat to C in the last two notes: the same small step returns as the work's biggest shock.
- **Late Schubert.** Like the last piano sonatas written alongside it, the quintet is long, unhurried and full of sudden turns of light. Knowing that it was written months before his death, many listeners hear it as a farewell, though there is no sign that Schubert expected to die so soon.
