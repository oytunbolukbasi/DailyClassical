### The big picture

- Rachmaninoff wrote the concerto in the summer of 1909 and finished it on 23 September, just before his first tour of the United States. He wrote it for that tour, and had so little time that he practised it on a silent keyboard during the voyage.
- He played the first performance himself in New York on 28 November 1909, with the New York Symphony Society conducted by Walter Damrosch. Two months later he played it again with Gustav Mahler conducting, and he remembered how Mahler rehearsed every detail long past the scheduled hour.
- It is dedicated to the pianist Josef Hofmann, who never played it in public. Rachmaninoff himself preferred it to his Second Concerto, which he found "uncomfortable to play".
- It has a reputation as one of the hardest piano concertos. Vladimir Horowitz, who played it for Rachmaninoff in 1927, made it famous with his recording of 1930. The 1996 film *Shine* made it famous again.
- In one line: a simple melody grows into a vast first movement with a huge solo [[cadenza]] (I), a brooding set of variations (II), a driving finale that brings back the first movement's themes and ends in triumph in D major (III).

### I. Allegro ma non tanto

**Summary.** After two bars of quiet pulsing in the orchestra, the piano plays a long, plain melody in bare octaves. Everything grows from it. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but its centre of gravity is the long solo cadenza, which builds to a thunderous climax and then turns quiet as four wind players recall the opening theme.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a simple, narrow melody, like a chant, played by the piano with both hands an octave apart.
- Theme 2: a gentler, more yearning melody, introduced by quiet exchanges between orchestra and piano.

**Things to notice**

1. Sing the opening melody to yourself. It moves by small steps and stays in a narrow range, like a chant. Listen for it in all three movements.
2. In the cadenza's quiet second part, count the four wind soloists: flute, oboe, clarinet, horn.

**In this recording.** Ashkenazy recorded all four Rachmaninoff concertos with Previn and the London Symphony Orchestra for Decca; this Third was recorded in March 1971 and first issued in 1972. Rachmaninoff wrote two versions of the first-movement cadenza; pianists choose one or the other. On the Spotify album the Third is tracks 7–9.

### II. Intermezzo: Adagio

**Summary.** A slow movement built as a set of [[variation]]s on a sad, Russian-sounding tune. The orchestra plays the tune and the first two variations alone before the piano enters. In the middle, a quick, waltz-like episode brings back the first movement's opening melody. The movement does not end: the piano bursts out and leads straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a long, sorrowful melody, first on the oboe over the strings.
- The waltz episode: quick and light, with the first movement's melody in the woodwinds.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for how long the piano waits before entering. The orchestra has the first two minutes to itself.
2. In the waltz episode, see if you can recognise the first movement's melody in the woodwinds.

### III. Finale: Alla breve

**Summary.** The finale bursts in without a break. A driving, rhythmic theme in the piano alternates with a broader one in heavy chords. Instead of a normal development there is a long, playful detour, and the first movement's themes return. Then the main themes come back, the piano builds a dazzling climax, and the second theme bursts out in D major.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: hard-driving and rhythmic, in the piano's chords.
- Theme 2: broader, a rising tune in heavy chords that becomes the final hymn.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the four-note rhythm at the very end, "dum, da-da, dum". Rachmaninoff ends his Second Concerto and Second Symphony the same way.
2. The concerto began in D minor with a plain, quiet melody. It ends in D major with everyone at full volume.

### Threads

- **One melody.** The quiet opening tune returns in the second movement's waltz and in the finale. The concerto is built like a [[cyclic form]].
- **A Russian sound.** The opening melody moves like a church chant. One scholar, Joseph Yasser, traced it to a liturgical model; Rachmaninoff said it simply wrote itself.
- **Cuts.** Rachmaninoff allowed cuts, especially in the second and third movements, and for decades most pianists took them. Today the concerto is usually played complete.
- **Piano first.** Unlike the Second Concerto, where piano and orchestra are partners, here the piano is the main attraction.
