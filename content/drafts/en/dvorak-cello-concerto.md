### The big picture

- Dvořák wrote the concerto in New York between November 1894 and February 1895, in his last season as director of the National Conservatory. He was homesick and left for Bohemia soon after.
- For years he had refused the cellist Hanuš Wihan's requests for a concerto. He thought the cello fine in the orchestra but weak as a soloist, with a nasal top and a mumbling bass. Hearing Victor Herbert's Second Cello Concerto in New York in 1894 changed his mind.
- While he worked, his sister-in-law Josefina, his first love, wrote that she was seriously ill. He quoted her favourite of his songs, "Leave Me Alone", in the slow movement. She died in May 1895, and he rewrote the end of the finale in her memory.
- He refused to let anyone add a [[cadenza]]. The finale, he wrote, should die away "like a breath" before the orchestra ends it stormily. The first performance was in London on 19 March 1896, with Leo Stern as soloist and Dvořák conducting.
- In one line: a grand, singing [[sonata form]] with a famous horn tune (I), a tender song interrupted by grief (II), a march-like [[rondo]] that slows into a farewell (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra plays both main themes before the cello enters: a quiet, brooding tune for clarinets and a long, tender melody for solo horn. Then the cello comes in with a bold flourish and takes over both themes. The movement ends in a blaze of B major.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, brooding: a low, quiet tune for two clarinets, later played *grandioso* by the full orchestra.
- Theme 2, the horn tune: a long, tender melody, first on a solo horn.

### II. Adagio, ma non troppo

**Summary.** A slow movement in G major that begins like a gentle pastoral song in the woodwinds. A sudden outburst leads to a sadder melody in the minor, taken from Dvořák's song "Leave Me Alone". After the calm returns, the cello plays a long, free passage of its own and the movement ends very softly.

**Main ideas**

- The pastoral song: a gentle melody in the woodwinds, led by the clarinet.
- The song quotation: a longing, sad melody in the minor, from "Leave Me Alone".

### III. Finale: Allegro moderato – Andante – Allegro vivo

**Summary.** The horns start a quiet march that gathers force until the cello enters with the main tune. The tune keeps returning between contrasting episodes. Near the end the music slows almost to a standstill, memories of the first movement and of the song drift past, and then the orchestra ends the concerto stormily.

**Main ideas**

- The march tune: a bold, dancing theme, first hinted at by the horns and then played in full by the cello.
- The farewell: a slow, fading passage near the end that recalls earlier themes.

### Threads

- **Homesickness.** Written in New York by a composer who longed for home. Unlike the *New World* Symphony, it shows few clear American influences.
- **A private story.** The slow movement and the end of the finale carry the memory of Josefina, through a song she loved.
- **The cello as a singer.** Dvořák, who doubted the cello as a soloist, gives it one long melody after another.
- **No cadenza.** Dvořák refused one. The nearest thing is a free, songlike passage for the cello in the slow movement.
