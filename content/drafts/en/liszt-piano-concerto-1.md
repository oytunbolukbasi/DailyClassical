### The big picture

- Liszt worked on the concerto for more than twenty years. Its main themes are in a sketchbook from around 1830, when he was about nineteen; he finished a version in 1849, kept revising it and published it in 1856.
- It was first played in Weimar on 17 February 1855, with Liszt at the piano and Hector Berlioz conducting. Liszt dedicated it to the pianist and composer Henry Litolff.
- The four movements are short and follow each other without a break. Themes from one movement return, changed, in the others, and Béla Bartók called the concerto "the first perfect realisation of cyclic sonata form".
- The triangle has a solo in the third movement, and the critic Eduard Hanslick mocked the work as a "Triangle Concerto". There is also a story, told as a joke, that Liszt and Hans von Bülow fitted the words "None of you understand this, ha-ha!" to the opening notes.
- In one line: a defiant motto and thundering octaves (I), a dreamy nocturne (II), a sparkling scherzo with triangle (III), a march that brings all the themes back (IV).

### I. Allegro maestoso

**Summary.** The strings hurl out a short, defiant motto and the piano answers at once with thundering octaves. A tender duet with the clarinet brings calm, then the drama returns. The movement does not end loudly: it fades away to the softest sound.

**Main ideas**

- The motto: a short, bold figure in the strings, answered by blazing wind and brass chords.
- The falling octaves: fast octaves on the piano, sliding down by semitones.
- The duet: a quiet, tender melody shared by the piano and a solo clarinet.

### II. Quasi adagio

**Summary.** A slow, dreamy song. The low strings introduce it, and the piano takes it up like a [[nocturne]]. After a climax and a silence, the mood breaks: the piano answers the orchestra in free, speech-like phrases, then trills while the woodwinds sing a new tune.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a slow, singing melody, first in the [[muted]] cellos and basses.
- The new tune: a gentle melody passed from one woodwind to another over the piano's trills.

### III. Allegretto vivace – Allegro animato

**Summary.** A sparkling [[scherzo]] that starts with a solo for triangle. The piano plays a light, mischievous tune with the woodwinds, until the music darkens and old themes return. The movement ends with a fierce piano passage that stops on a tense, unresolved chord.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo tune: a quick, playful melody on the piano, marked *capriccioso scherzando* (whimsical and joking).
- The return: the motto and the falling octaves from the first movement.

### IV. Allegro marziale animato

**Summary.** The finale turns the slow movement's dreamy song into a brisk march. Then themes from the whole concerto return one after another, faster and brighter, and the piano drives towards a dazzling end in E-flat major.

**Main ideas**

- The march: the slow movement's song, now quick and military, in the woodwinds.
- The falling octaves, now at breakneck speed.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A falling scale, then the slow movement's tender song as a brisk march in the winds | [[Thematic transformation]]: the same tune in a new character |

1. Listen to the last two notes. The piano has stopped, and the orchestra closes the concerto on its own.

### Threads

- **One set of themes.** The motto, the falling octaves and the slow song return across the movements, each time in a new form. This is the [[cyclic form]] Bartók admired.
- **Transformation.** The tender song of the slow movement becomes a march in the finale. Liszt changes a theme's character while keeping its notes.
- **The virtuoso.** Liszt wrote the concerto for his own hands. Its octaves and leaps show off the pianist who was the most famous performer of his age.
