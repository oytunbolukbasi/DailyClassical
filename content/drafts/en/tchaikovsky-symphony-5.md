### The big picture

- Tchaikovsky wrote the Fifth between May and August 1888, mostly at Frolovskoye, the country house where he was then living. It came ten years after the Fourth, and while writing it he kept doubting that he could still compose.
- In a notebook that spring he sketched a programme: "Total submission before fate, or, what is the same thing, the inscrutable designs of Providence". Yet in June he told a correspondent that the symphony had no programme. How much of the plan survived is not known.
- A single theme, often called the motto, is heard in all four movements: funereal at the start, a troubling ghost in the middle movements, a blazing march at the end. The keys travel the same way, from E minor to E major.
- Tchaikovsky conducted the first performance in St Petersburg on 17 November 1888. After the first few performances he decided it was a failure, "too colourful, massive, insincere". A few months later, after a concert in Hamburg, he loved it again. It is dedicated to Theodor Avé-Lallemant, head of the Hamburg Philharmonic Society.
- In one line: a dark march and a restless dance (I), a love song on the horn (II), a graceful waltz with a shadow (III), the motto's triumph (IV).

### I. Andante – Allegro con anima

**Summary.** Two clarinets, low and dark, play the motto as a slow funeral march. Then a quick, restless dance begins, and the movement becomes a struggle between that nervous energy and a broad, warm second theme. It ends as it began, in the dark: quiet, low and in E minor.

**Main ideas**

- The motto: a slow, heavy tune in the low clarinets, like a funeral march.
- Theme 1: a lilting, slightly limping dance in 6/8, first on clarinet and bassoon.
- Theme 2: a broad, yearning melody in the strings, in D major.

### II. Andante cantabile, con alcuna licenza

**Summary.** One of the most famous horn solos ever written. After a few dark chords in the low strings, the horn sings a long, tender melody; an oboe joins it. A more agitated middle section, led by the clarinet, builds to an outburst in which the motto crashes in. The love song returns, grander, and the motto interrupts it a second time.

**Main ideas**

- The horn melody: slow, soft and very long, marked *dolce con molto espressione*.
- The second tune: a shorter, rising phrase for oboe and horn.
- The middle section: a restless clarinet theme.

1. Listen for how freely the horn shapes its melody. *Con alcuna licenza* means "with some freedom": the tempo bends with the phrase.
2. The motto now sounds like a threat from outside. Each time, the love song has to recover.

### III. Valse: Allegro moderato

**Summary.** A waltz takes the place of the usual scherzo. The tune is graceful but slightly lopsided, with phrases that do not quite fit the beat. The middle is a flurry of fast, whispering notes in the strings. Near the end the motto drifts through, softly, in waltz time, like a shadow crossing a ballroom.

**Main ideas**

- The waltz: a lilting tune in the violins, with little hesitations.
- The flurry: fast, light running notes passed through the strings.
- The motto, quietly, in the woodwinds.

1. Try to count the waltz in threes. Here and there the rhythm seems to shift into twos, a [[hemiola]].
2. Listen for the motto near the end. It is so quiet it is easy to miss.

### IV. Finale: Andante maestoso – Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The motto returns at the start, now in E major and played by the strings like a hymn. Then a fierce Allegro breaks out. The finale is a hard-driven race, and at its end the motto becomes a grand march in the brass. In the last pages, the trumpets play the first movement's dance tune, now in triumph.

**Main ideas**

- The motto in the major: broad and solemn at first, triumphant at the end.
- The Allegro's main theme: a fierce, stamping tune in the strings.
- A second, more lyrical theme in the woodwinds.

1. Compare the first two minutes with the very first minute of the symphony: the same tune, now in the major and in the strings instead of the clarinets.
2. After the big pause near the end, notice how slow the march is. Tchaikovsky himself thought this ending overdone; listen and decide.

### Threads

- **The motto.** One tune through four movements: a funeral march (I), an intruder (II), a shadow in waltz time (III), a hymn and a victory march (IV). This is [[cyclic form]].
- **Minor to major.** The symphony moves from E minor to E major, the path Beethoven's Fifth made famous.
- **Doubt.** Tchaikovsky wrote it doubting himself, judged it harshly, then changed his mind. He was never sure the triumphant ending rang true.
- **Melody first.** The horn solo, the waltz and the second themes show his gift for long, singing lines, often given to a single wind instrument.
