### The big picture

- Mozart wrote it in Paris in April 1778, during his seven-month stay there. It was commissioned by the Duc de Guînes, an aristocrat and keen amateur flautist, for himself and his daughter Marie-Louise-Philippine, a harpist, who was taking composition lessons from Mozart.
- In a letter to his father Mozart said the duke played the flute extremely well and the daughter the harp "magnifique". As a composition pupil she disappointed him. The duke never paid for the concerto, and Mozart refused the half fee for the lessons offered through the housekeeper.
- It is Mozart's only concerto with a harp. In his day the harp was not yet a regular member of the orchestra, and pairing it with the flute was unusual.
- In shape it is close to the [[sinfonia concertante]], a piece for several soloists that was then the fashion in Paris. Mozart left no cadenzas for it; those by the 19th-century composer Carl Reinecke are the most often played, as here.
- In one line: a bright, courtly first movement (I), a slow song for strings and soloists (II), a dancing finale (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra opens with a bold, rising figure on the notes of C major, and sets out the themes before the soloists join. Then flute and harp take everything over, trading the melody and the accompaniment between them. It is a concerto movement in [[sonata form]], with a [[double exposition]] and a long cadenza for the two soloists together.

**Main ideas**

- The opening figure: a loud, rising call on the notes of the home chord, then quick runs.
- The duet: the flute singing a line while the harp ripples underneath in broken chords, and then the other way round.

### II. Andantino

**Summary.** A slow, songlike movement in F major. The strings set out short, gentle phrases, and the soloists answer and extend them into longer lines. The horns fall silent. Halfway through, the opening music comes back, and a quiet cadenza leads to a short close.

**Main ideas**

- The song: short, tender phrases in the strings, taken up by the flute.
- The harp's ripple: broken chords that keep the music moving.

### III. Rondeau: Allegro

**Summary.** A lively finale in the French manner: a *rondeau*, in which a dancing tune keeps coming back between new episodes. The orchestra plays it first, and at length. Then harp and flute enter, and much of the movement is a game of turns: one soloist plays while the other rests.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a bright, graceful tune for the orchestra.
- The turns: harp passages with the orchestra, then flute passages, then both together.

### Threads

- **Music for a salon.** Mozart wrote for two aristocratic amateurs and their guests, and the concerto keeps to elegant, well-mannered melody.
- **A rare pair.** Flute and harp share the solo role equally; in the long cadenzas they finish each other's phrases.
- **Paris.** The *sinfonia concertante* shape and the *rondeau* finale, with its French name, both suit the city where it was written.
