### The big picture

- Schumann married the pianist Clara Wieck in September 1840, after a long court battle with her father. In May 1841 he wrote a single movement for her, a *Phantasie* in A minor for piano and orchestra. Clara played it at the Leipzig Gewandhaus in August 1841, but no publisher wanted a one-movement piece.
- In 1845, now living in Dresden and urged on by Clara, he added a slow Intermezzo and a finale. Clara gave the first performance of the complete concerto in Dresden on 4 December 1845. A month later, on New Year's Day 1846, she played it in Leipzig with Mendelssohn conducting.
- It is not a contest between a star soloist and an orchestra. The piano and the woodwinds talk to each other like friends, and almost every theme grows out of the same tender melody heard at the start.
- Schumann liked to think of himself as two characters he had invented: Florestan, wild and passionate, and Eusebius, dreamy and gentle. Both are here: the fierce opening chords and the soft oboe tune that answers them.
- In one line: a passionate, songful fantasy (I), a shy, graceful conversation (II), a bright waltz-like finale that plays tricks with the beat (III).

### I. Allegro affettuoso

**Summary.** One sharp chord, a cascade of loud chords tumbling down the piano, and then the oboe sings the gentle, sad tune that the whole concerto is built on. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but it feels like a free fantasy: the tune keeps changing shape, in the minor, in the major, slow and dreamy, then fast and marching.

**Main ideas**

- The cascade: loud chords in a jagged rhythm, falling from the top of the piano to the bottom.
- The main theme: a soft, sighing melody in A minor, first on the oboe with the other winds, then on the piano.
- The dream: the same theme slowed down, in a warm, distant key, for piano and clarinet.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | One sharp stroke from the orchestra, then the piano alone hurls loud chords down the keyboard | The opening: the passionate side speaks first |
| ≈ 0:12 | The oboe, with the other winds, plays a soft, sighing melody | The main theme, in A minor: the gentle side answers |
| ≈ 0:40 | The piano repeats the oboe's melody on its own | The soloist takes up the theme |
| ≈ 2:30 | The same melody, but now brighter and calmer, in the clarinet with the piano weaving around it | The main theme again, in C major. In this concerto the "second theme" is the first one in a new light |
| ≈ 3:45 | The full orchestra, loud, with the cascade's jagged rhythm | The end of the [[exposition]] |
| ≈ 4:15–6:15 | Everything slows; piano and clarinet trade soft phrases of the melody, in a dreamy, faraway key | A slow interlude, *Andante espressivo*, in A-flat major. The heart of the movement |

**Things to notice**

1. Hum the oboe's tune from the start. Then listen for it in the slow interlude, the cadenza and the march at the end. It is the same few notes every time.
2. In the slow interlude, notice who has the tune. The piano often accompanies while the clarinet sings, like two people in quiet conversation.

**In this recording.** On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–3. It is followed by Grieg's Piano Concerto, also in A minor, which Lupu and Previn recorded with the same orchestra in January 1973. Grieg had heard Clara Schumann play this concerto in Leipzig in 1858, and his own owes much to it.

### II. Intermezzo. Andantino grazioso

**Summary.** A short, graceful interlude. Piano and strings swap tiny, polite phrases, as if bowing to each other. In the middle the cellos sing a broad, warm melody. At the end, the clarinet and bassoon quietly remember the first movement's theme, and the music runs straight into the finale without a break.

**Main ideas**

- The dialogue: a short, dainty four-note phrase, passed between piano and strings.
- The cello song: a long, warm melody in the cellos, with the piano rippling round it.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The piano plays a tiny, delicate phrase; the strings answer with the same phrase | The opening dialogue, in F major |
| ≈ 1:20 | The cellos sing a broad, warm melody; the piano decorates it with rising and falling figures | The middle section |
| ≈ 3:10 | The tiny phrases return | The opening dialogue again |
| ≈ 4:30 | Very quiet; the clarinet and bassoon recall the sighing theme from the first movement, first in the major, then in the minor | A memory of movement I |
| ≈ 5:00 | The music gathers itself, and the piano bursts into the finale | The movements are joined, [[attacca]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the last minute. The theme from the first movement comes back, and suddenly the door opens into the finale. The two movements are one continuous piece of music.

### III. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** A bright, dancing finale in A major. The piano's main tune is the concerto's first theme turned upside down into joy. The second theme plays a famous trick: the orchestra stretches the notes across the bar-lines, so the steady waltz beat seems to disappear.

**Main ideas**

- The main tune: a rising, joyful melody in the piano, related to the first movement's sighing theme.
- The beat trick: long, off-the-beat notes in the strings that make three beats feel like two.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The strings rush upwards and the piano bursts in with a bright, rising tune | The main theme, in A major |
| ≈ 0:45 | The full orchestra takes the tune, loud and festive | The [[tutti]] |
| ≈ 1:40 | The strings play long notes that cut across the beat; for a while you cannot tell where the bar begins | The second theme, in E major. The waltz in three seems to turn into a slow march in two: a [[hemiola]] |
| ≈ 2:30 | Fast, brilliant passagework in the piano | The end of the exposition |
| ≈ 3:15 | The main tune is passed from instrument to instrument, each entering in turn | The [[development]], with imitations of the tune |
| ≈ 4:45 | The bright, rising tune returns in the piano | The [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 6:15 | The beat trick again, now in the home key | The second theme returns, in A major |
| ≈ 7:15 | A long, rising build; a new, swaying tune appears in the oboe while the piano runs underneath | The long [[coda]] begins |
| Near the end | The piano races up and down in brilliant runs; a final crash of chords | The end, in A major |

**Things to notice**

1. During the second theme, try counting "one-two-three" with the orchestra. You will lose your place, because Schumann deliberately makes the music move in twos for a while.
2. Compare the finale's main tune with the oboe's tune at the start of the concerto. They begin the same way; one sighs, the other jumps for joy.

### Threads

- **One melody.** Almost everything grows from the oboe's opening tune: the second theme, the slow interlude, the cadenza, the marching coda and the finale's main tune.
- **Clara.** Schumann wrote it for his wife to play, and she was the soloist at its first performances. The concerto shows off tenderness more than speed.
- **Conversation.** The piano is a partner, not a lone hero. It often accompanies a clarinet or an oboe, and the woodwinds are its closest friends.
- **Two characters.** Fierce outbursts and dreamy replies take turns throughout, the two sides of Schumann he called Florestan and Eusebius.
