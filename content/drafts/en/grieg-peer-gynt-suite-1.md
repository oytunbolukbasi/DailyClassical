### The big picture

- Henrik Ibsen wrote *Peer Gynt* in 1867 as a play in verse, about a boastful Norwegian country lad who lives in his own fantasies. Peer runs off with another man's bride, wanders into the hall of the troll king, comes home in time for his mother's death, and spends years abroad as a trader and a false prophet, while Solveig, the woman who loves him, waits.
- When the play was to be staged, Ibsen asked Grieg in 1874 for [[incidental music]]. Grieg found it hard going and called the subject "terribly unmanageable". The production opened in Christiania (now Oslo) on 24 February 1876, with about 90 minutes of his music, and was a great success.
- More than ten years later Grieg chose four numbers for a concert suite, published in 1888, and a second suite followed in 1893. The four pieces are not in the play's order: the sunrise comes from Act IV, Åse's death from Act III, Anitra's dance from Act IV, and the trolls from Act II.
- "Morning" sounds like a Norwegian dawn to most listeners, but in the play the sun rises over the Moroccan desert, where Peer has been robbed and abandoned.
- In one line: sunrise (I), a mother's death (II), a dancer's charm (III), a troll chase that ends in uproar (IV).

### I. Morning Mood

**Summary.** A flute and an oboe pass a simple, rocking melody back and forth, like two birds greeting the light. The melody travels through new keys, grows warmer, and the whole orchestra blazes out as the sun breaks through. Unusually, this climax comes early; the rest of the piece is the calm of a bright morning.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: five notes rising and falling in a gentle rocking rhythm, built on the [[pentatonic scale]], first on the flute, then on the oboe.

1. The loudest moment comes in the first minute or so. Notice how the rest of the piece stays bright but calm, as if the day has simply begun.

### II. The Death of Åse

**Summary.** Åse, Peer's mother, is dying. Peer, home again, sits at her bed and pretends to drive her by sleigh to the fairy-tale castle of Soria Moria, where the king is giving a feast. By the time they reach the gate, she has died. The music is for strings alone: a slow, grieving phrase in B minor, repeated higher and louder, then sinking back into near silence.

**Main ideas**

- The lament: a slow, heavy phrase that climbs a little and falls back, in hushed strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The strings play a slow, grieving phrase, very softly | The lament, in B minor |
| ≈ 1:10 | The same phrase, higher and louder | The grief grows |
| ≈ 2:00 | The fullest, loudest moment, then the phrase begins to fall | The climax |

| Last minute | Very quiet, slow chords that almost disappear | Åse dies |

**Things to notice**

1. There are no winds, brass or drums here, only strings. Notice how much grief Grieg gets from a single phrase played again and again at different heights and volumes.

### III. Anitra's Dance

**Summary.** In the desert Peer is taken for a prophet by a Bedouin tribe, and the chief's daughter, Anitra, dances for him. She will soon run off with his money and his rings. Grieg writes a light, teasing mazurka for strings with a triangle.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a lilting, coquettish tune in the violins, in three beats, with little turns and sighs.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the triangle. It is the only instrument besides the strings, and it gives the dance its glint of jewellery.

### IV. In the Hall of the Mountain King

**Summary.** Peer has ridden into the hall of the troll king, the Dovre King, with the king's daughter, and the troll courtiers shout for his blood. A short, sneaking tune starts very quietly in the lowest instruments, climbs, gets louder and faster, and ends in uproar. In the play the trolls sing; in the suite the music is for orchestra alone.

**Main ideas**

- The trolls' tune: a short, tiptoeing melody that is repeated again and again, a little higher each time, faster and louder.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Very softly, low plucked strings and bassoons creep through a short tune | The trolls stir, in B minor |
| ≈ 0:25 | The tune moves higher, to the violins, and keeps repeating | The same tune in a higher key |
| ≈ 0:50 | Louder and faster; the brass join in | The chase begins |
| ≈ 1:25 | Very fast and very loud, with crashing cymbals and drums | Full fury |
| Last 15 seconds | Huge, hammered chords and a final crash | The uproar at its height |

**Things to notice**

1. The tune never changes: only its speed, volume and register do. It is one long [[crescendo]] and acceleration.
2. Grieg wrote to a friend that this piece reeked of ultra-Norwegianism and troll self-satisfaction, and that he could not bear to hear it, though he hoped the irony would come through. It became one of his best-known pieces.

### Threads

- **A play without words.** Each piece is a scene from Ibsen, with the actors removed. Knowing the story (a sunrise in the desert, a mother's deathbed and her imaginary sleigh ride, a dancer who robs her admirer, a troll hall) changes what you hear.
- **Order of a concert, not of the play.** Grieg arranged the scenes for contrast: calm, grief, charm, fury. The suite ends with its loudest number, as a concert piece should.
- **Simple tunes, big effects.** Every piece is built from one short melody, repeated and recoloured. Grieg's skill is in the orchestra: flute and oboe, hushed strings, a triangle, a crescendo.
