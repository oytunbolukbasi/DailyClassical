### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote the concerto in 1809, the year Napoleon's army besieged and occupied Vienna. In July he wrote to his publisher that there was "nothing but drums, cannons, men, misery of all sorts" around him. The story goes that during the bombardment he sheltered in his brother's cellar with pillows over his ears.
- It is dedicated to Archduke Rudolf, a younger brother of the Austrian emperor and Beethoven's pupil, friend and patron.
- Beethoven had been the soloist in his earlier concertos, but by now his hearing was too poor for him to play this one. Friedrich Schneider gave the premiere in Leipzig on 28 November 1811; Beethoven's pupil Carl Czerny played it in Vienna on 12 February 1812.
- The nickname "Emperor" is not Beethoven's and its origin is uncertain; it may come from his English publisher, Johann Baptist Cramer. Writers such as Donald Tovey think Beethoven, no friend of Napoleon's conquests, would have disliked it.
- In one line: a grand, martial opening movement led by the piano (I), a hushed hymn in a far-away key (II), a leaping dance that bursts in without a break (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** Instead of waiting for the orchestra to finish its introduction, the piano answers the very first chord with a sweeping solo. Three times. Only then does the orchestra begin the [[double exposition]] of a Classical concerto, with a proud, striding theme and a soft march. When the soloist returns, the two sides compete, interrupt each other and finally join forces. Beethoven even wrote out the [[cadenza]] himself.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: three huge orchestral chords, each answered by a burst of [[arpeggio]]s, [[trill]]s and scales from the piano.
- Theme 1: a proud, striding tune, martial in the orchestra, gentle in the piano.
- Theme 2: a soft march, first in the minor in the strings, then in the major in the horns.

### II. Adagio un poco mosso

**Summary.** A hushed hymn in B major, a key very far from the concerto's E-flat. [[muted]] strings play the hymn; the piano floats above them, then sings it itself. At the end the music sinks by a half-step, and the piano quietly tries out the theme of the finale before it bursts in.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: a slow, quiet melody for muted strings.
- The piano's line: a soft, falling thread of notes high above, like an improvisation.

### III. Rondo: Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** The piano leaps in with a dancing theme in six beats, and the orchestra repeats it with full force. A [[rondo]]: the theme keeps returning between other episodes, and in the middle it travels through three different keys. Near the end the music suddenly slows and quietens, leaving the piano alone with the timpani before the final rush.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a leaping, dancing tune in six beats, with a lively, off-balance rhythm.
- The timpani: a soft, steady tapping in the coda, under the piano's last fragments.

### Threads

- **The piano in command.** The soloist opens the concerto, leads the bridge into the finale and starts the rondo: this is a concerto in which the piano leads.
- **Linked movements.** The slow movement does not end; it turns into the finale, with the piano trying out the new theme first.
- **Far-away keys.** The slow movement is in B major, and in the first movement the piano plays the march in B minor: Beethoven keeps stepping just a half-step away from home.
