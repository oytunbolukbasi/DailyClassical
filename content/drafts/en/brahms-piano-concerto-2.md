### The big picture

- Brahms began the concerto in 1878 and finished it in the summer of 1881 at Pressbaum, near Vienna, shortly after returning from a journey to Italy. It came 22 years after his First Piano Concerto.
- He played the solo part himself at the premiere in Budapest on 9 November 1881. It was an immediate success, and he went on to perform it in many cities. He dedicated it to his old teacher, Eduard Marxsen.
- Brahms liked to understate. He told a friend he had written "a very small piano concerto with a very small and pretty scherzo". In fact it has four movements instead of the usual three and lasts about 50 minutes.
- Piano and orchestra work together more than they compete. The piano is fiendishly hard, but it often accompanies, and in the slow movement the main melody belongs to a solo cello.
- In one line: a broad, noble opening that grows into a storm (I), a fierce [[scherzo]] (II), a cello song and a still, hushed dream (III), a light, graceful dance (IV).

### I. Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** A solo horn opens the concerto with a calm call, and the piano answers. Almost at once the piano breaks into a loud solo passage, and only then does the orchestra start its own presentation of the themes. The movement is a concerto [[sonata form]] on a very large scale, with long stretches of struggle before the horn's peace returns.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, the horn call: a calm, rising phrase that ends with a gentle turn.
- Theme 2: a sad, expressive melody in the violins, in a minor key.

1. The horn call comes back at every turning point: at the start, at the beginning of the development and at the recapitulation. Each time the piano's answer is different.

### II. Allegro appassionato

**Summary.** The "very small and pretty scherzo" is a storm. The piano drives a fierce theme in D minor, and a sweeping string melody answers it. In the middle the music turns to a bright D major and a striding, march-like tune that the piano and orchestra finally shout together.

**Main ideas**

- The storm: a driving, restless theme for the piano, beginning on a strong upbeat.
- The yearning tune: a sweeping melody for violins and violas in unison.
- The [[trio]] tune: a bright, striding melody in D major.

### III. Andante

**Summary.** The slow movement opens with a song for solo cello, not for piano. When the piano finally enters, it seems to improvise around the melody. A stormy passage breaks out and passes, and in the middle the music almost stops in a hushed, distant key. Then the cello returns with the song.

**Main ideas**

- The cello song: a long, slow, tender melody. Brahms later turned it into a song, *Immer leiser wird mein Schlummer* ("My slumber grows ever lighter").
- The dream: a hushed melody for two clarinets over the piano's sparse chords, in F-sharp major.

### IV. Allegretto grazioso

**Summary.** After all the drama, a light, smiling [[rondo]]. The piano's graceful tune keeps returning between episodes, one of them a sad melody with a Hungarian lilt. At the end the tempo quickens and the tune skips into a new rhythm.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo tune: a light, graceful melody for piano over plucked strings.
- The Hungarian tune: a swaying, melancholy melody in A minor, shared between woodwinds and strings.

### Threads

- **Partners.** The horn opens the concerto, a cello sings the slow movement, and the piano often accompanies. This is a concerto for piano and orchestra together, almost a symphony with piano.
- **From struggle to ease.** Two big, stormy movements are followed by a song and a dance. The weight of the concerto lies in its first half; the second half relaxes.
- **Understatement.** Brahms called it very small. It is one of the longest and hardest concertos in the repertoire.
