### The big picture

- Alexander Borodin was a professor of chemistry first and a composer in his spare time. He was also a keen amateur cellist, and the cello opens two of the four movements here with the main melody.
- He wrote the quartet quickly in the summer of 1881, while staying at the country estate of his friend Nikolai Lodyzhensky, south-east of Moscow. It is dedicated to his wife, Ekaterina, a pianist he had met in Heidelberg, where he worked as a young chemist.
- Borodin's biographer Serge Dianin suggested that the quartet was a present for the twentieth anniversary of that meeting, and that it recalls it in music. Borodin left no programme, so this is an interpretation, not a fact.
- In 1953 the Broadway musical *Kismet* turned Borodin's melodies into songs, including the second theme of this quartet's scherzo ("Baubles, Bangles and Beads"). The show won a Tony Award in 1954, and Borodin, long dead, was among those honoured.
- In one line: a warm, singing conversation led by the cello (I), a quick, shimmering scherzo with a waltz tune (II), a love song at night (III), a question and an answer that become a race (IV).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The cello opens with a long, songful melody high in its range, and the first violin answers. The whole movement is lyrical: instead of dramatic clashes, Borodin gets contrast from changing keys and from lines that weave around each other. It is in [[sonata form]].

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a warm, gently rising melody on the cello, high up, answered by the violin.
- Theme 2: a tender tune on the first violin, in A major, with a middle part where violin and cello trade phrases.
- The closing theme: livelier, marked *Animato*.

### II. Scherzo: Allegro

**Summary.** A fast, light scherzo, placed second rather than third, as Borodin also did in his Second Symphony. A quick, glittering figure on the first violin alternates with a lilting tune that later became a Broadway song. Unusually for a scherzo, it is in sonata form, and it ends in a whisper.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a quick, falling run on the first violin, with a little falling figure in the viola underneath.
- Theme 2: a lilting, waltz-like tune built on rising steps, the tune of "Baubles, Bangles and Beads".

### III. Notturno: Andante

**Summary.** The quartet's most famous movement. The cello sings a long, tender song, then the first violin takes it. A more agitated middle section interrupts the calm. When the melody returns, cello and violin play it in [[canon]], one following the other, like two voices in conversation.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: long, warm and slow, first on the cello, over soft, gently pulsing chords.
- The middle section: quicker, more restless figures and a rising phrase.

### IV. Finale: Andante – Vivace

**Summary.** It starts slowly, with a question and an answer: the two violins ask, viola and cello reply. Then the question speeds up and becomes a quick, busy theme passed from player to player. Borodin's skill with [[counterpoint]] is on show: lines enter one after another and weave together, and the movement races to a broad, bright end in D major.

**Main ideas**

- The question: a short, rising phrase on the two violins.
- The answer: a falling reply from viola and cello.
- The Vivace theme: the question at speed, entering in turn on viola, second violin and first violin.

### Threads

- **The cello.** Borodin played it himself, and it opens both the first movement and the Notturno with the main melody.
- **Lyrical, not dramatic.** Contrast comes from changing keys and woven lines more than from conflict.
- **Pairs.** Question and answer in the finale, the canon in the Notturno, the violin answering the cello in the first movement: the quartet is full of conversations for two.
- **A love letter?** The dedication to Ekaterina is a fact; the story of the first meeting is Dianin's reading. Listen and decide.
