### The big picture

- Shostakovich wrote the quartet in three days, 12 to 14 July 1960, at Gohrisch, near Dresden. He had gone to East Germany to write music for a film about the bombing of Dresden in the Second World War.
- It came shortly after he had joined the Communist Party, which he did reluctantly. The printed score is dedicated "to the victims of fascism and war". To his friend Isaak Glikman he wrote, bitterly, that he had written it in memory of its author. Friends said he thought of it as his own epitaph.
- Every movement is built on his musical signature, [[DSCH]]: the notes D, E-flat, C, B, his initials in German spelling. The quartet is also full of quotations from his own earlier works, like a life passing before his eyes.
- The five movements are played without a break: three slow movements frame two fast ones.
- The story goes that when the Borodin Quartet played it to him at his home in Moscow, he buried his head in his hands and wept, and the four players quietly packed up and left.
- In one line: a lament (I), a fury (II), a ghostly waltz (III), knocks and memories (IV), a farewell (V).

### I. Largo

**Summary.** Four slow notes in the cello, Shostakovich's signature, taken up by each instrument in turn. A grey, still lament, with fragments of his earlier music drifting through it.

**Main ideas**

- [[DSCH]]: four notes, close together, turning back on themselves. They run through the whole quartet.
- The movement quotes his First Symphony, written when he was 19, and his Fifth.

**Summary.** A sudden explosion. All four instruments attack at top speed and full volume. In the middle, a wild, dance-like tune from his Second Piano Trio, a work written in 1944 that draws on Jewish folk music.

### III. Allegretto

**Summary.** A waltz, but a thin and ghostly one. The first violin plays the four-note signature as a dance tune, high and brittle, while the lower instruments thump out the beat.

### IV. Largo

**Summary.** Three loud, violent chords interrupt a held note, again and again, like knocking at a door. In between, two quotations: a Russian revolutionary funeral song, and a love song from his own opera *Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District*, the work that had brought down Stalin's anger on him in 1936.

### V. Largo

**Summary.** The last movement mirrors the first: the four notes enter one instrument at a time, slower and greyer than before. The quartet ends as it began, in near silence.

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the first and last minutes of the quartet. The same four notes open and close it, first as a question, at the end as a farewell.

**In this recording.** The Pavel Haas Quartet, from Prague, recorded the quartet in 2019. The album was named Recording of the Year at the Classic Prague Awards.

### Threads

- **A signature.** The four notes of DSCH appear in every movement, in every mood.
- **Memories.** Quotations from his symphonies, the Piano Trio, the Cello Concerto and *Lady Macbeth* pass by like scenes from a life.
- **Four voices.** Listen for who leads: the cello opens and closes the quartet, the first violin carries the dances, and at the climaxes all four play as one.
