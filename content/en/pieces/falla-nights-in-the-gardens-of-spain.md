### The big picture

- Falla began the music in Paris in 1909 as a set of nocturnes for solo piano. The pianist Ricardo Viñes suggested turning them into a work for piano and orchestra. When the First World War broke out Falla returned to Spain, and he finished it in 1915. It is dedicated to Viñes.
- The first performance was at the Teatro Real in Madrid on 9 April 1916, with José Cubiles at the piano and Enrique Fernández Arbós conducting the Orquesta Sinfónica de Madrid.
- Falla called it "symphonic impressions for piano and orchestra", not a concerto. The piano part is brilliant but rarely dominates: it is woven into the orchestra, often imitating the strummed and plucked sounds of a guitar. The colours owe much to Debussy and Ravel, Falla's friends in Paris.
- The tunes are not folk songs, but they are built from the rhythms, scales and ornaments of Andalusian music. Falla wrote that the work "has no pretensions to being descriptive; it is merely expressive", and that "melancholy and mystery" have their part, as well as the sounds of festivals and dances.
- In one line: a night in the gardens of the Alhambra (I), a dance heard from far away (II), and a fiesta in the hills of Córdoba that fades into the dark (III).

### I. En el Generalife

**Summary.** The Generalife is the summer garden of the Moorish kings, on the hill beside the Alhambra in Granada, full of fountains, cypresses and jasmine. The music begins in a hush, with rustling strings and a melody that turns around a few notes. The piano enters with quick, glittering figures, and the music slowly gathers into a passionate climax before sinking back into the night.

**Main ideas**

- The night theme: a narrow, winding melody that circles a few notes, in the woodwinds and strings over a soft, rustling accompaniment.
- The piano's figures: rapid repeated notes and ripples that sound like a guitar or like water.
- The climax: the same winding melody, broadened and loud, with the piano in heavy chords.

1. Listen to how seldom the piano plays a tune of its own. Mostly it adds colour: shimmering repeated notes, rippling figures, strummed chords. That is why Falla would not call the work a concerto.

This version, with the London Philharmonic and the Spanish conductor Rafael Frühbeck de Burgos, was made in July 1983 at Walthamstow Assembly Hall in London. On the Spotify album the three movements are tracks 1–3.

### II. Danza lejana

**Summary.** A dance heard from far away, in a garden that Falla does not name. It begins softly, with a swaying rhythm, and the piano takes up the dance tune with Andalusian turns and flourishes. The dance comes closer and grows more insistent, and then, without a break, it runs straight into the last movement.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a lilting, swaying rhythm, first in the orchestra, soft and distant.
- The piano's tune: ornamented with quick turns and trills, in the manner of Spanish song.

### III. En los jardines de la Sierra de Córdoba

**Summary.** A fiesta in the gardens of the hills outside Córdoba. Loud, rhythmic outbursts from the whole orchestra alternate with long, singing passages for the piano and strings, some of them as plaintive as flamenco song. After the last climax the celebration does not end with a bang: the music fades into a calm, quiet close.

**Main ideas**

- The fiesta: hammered rhythms and strummed chords in piano and orchestra.
- The lament: a long, ornamented melody that wails and turns like *cante jondo*, the "deep song" of Andalusia.
- The close: slow, tender music for piano and strings.

### Threads

- **Gardens, not maps.** Falla insisted the music expresses rather than describes: the scent and mood of gardens at night, not a story or a tour.
- **The guitar inside the piano.** In every movement the piano imitates the guitar: plucked repeated notes, strummed chords, rolling strokes. The real guitar never appears.
- **Granada.** The first movement looks at the Generalife from afar; a few years later, in 1921, Falla went to live in Granada, below the Alhambra, and stayed for almost twenty years.
