### The big picture

- Mahler began the symphony in the summer of 1899 at Bad Aussee and finished it in August 1900 at Maiernigg, on the Wörthersee, during his summer breaks from running the Vienna Court Opera.
- It grew backwards from a song. In 1892 Mahler had set "Das himmlische Leben" ("The Heavenly Life"), a poem from the folk anthology *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* in which a child describes heaven as a feast. He called the song the "tapering spire" of the symphony. Its tunes are glimpsed in the first three movements, and a soprano sings it whole in the finale.
- It is shorter than the two symphonies before it and uses his smallest orchestra, with no trombones or tuba. Mahler gave the movements no titles and published no programme.
- The premiere, in Munich on 25 November 1901 with Mahler conducting, puzzled and angered much of the audience, and critics in Berlin and Vienna were harsh. After the Second World War it became one of his most popular works.
- In one line: a sleigh-bell stroll that turns turbulent (I), Death fiddling a dance (II), a long, serene set of variations that opens onto heaven (III), a child's song of heavenly feasting (IV).

### I. Bedächtig, nicht eilen

**Summary.** Sleigh bells and flutes jingle for three bars, and the violins slide into a graceful, old-fashioned tune that has often been compared to Schubert. The opening sounds innocent, but the [[development]] grows crowded, strange and loud, until a trumpet calls everyone to order. The tune then returns as if nothing had happened.

**Main ideas**

- The bells: sleigh bells and flutes in a jingling, slightly hesitant rhythm. They return in the finale.
- Theme 1, violins: a graceful, bowing melody in G major.
- Theme 2, cellos: a broad, singing tune, marked "broadly sung".

1. Listen to the trumpet call after the climax. Mahler later used it to open his Fifth Symphony.
2. The recapitulation does not start at the beginning of the tune but in the middle of a phrase, as if the music had simply been interrupted and picked up again.

### II. In gemächlicher Bewegung, ohne Hast

**Summary.** Mahler once called this [[scherzo]] "Freund Hein spielt auf": Friend Hein, a folk name for Death, strikes up a tune. The leader of the violins plays a solo on a violin tuned a whole tone higher, which gives a harsh, rasping sound, like a village fiddler's. The tune is eerie rather than terrifying. Between its appearances come two gentle [[Ländler]]-like trios.

**Main ideas**

- Death's fiddle: a crooked, scraping tune on a solo violin tuned up a tone, a technique called [[scordatura]].
- The horn call: a short phrase that introduces the fiddle each time.
- The trios: lazy, cheerful country dances in F major, warmer and softer.

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the solo violin in this movement with the orchestra's violins. The retuned instrument sounds tighter and coarser, which is exactly the effect Mahler wanted.

### III. Ruhevoll (Poco adagio)

**Summary.** The heart of the symphony, and its longest movement. The cellos sing a calm, rocking melody over plucked basses, and a sad, lamenting theme for the oboe answers. Mahler alternates the two in a series of [[variation]]s. Late in the movement the variations speed up, faster and faster, then stop. At the very end, without warning, the whole orchestra blazes out in E major, the gates of heaven opening, and the music dies away.

**Main ideas**

- Theme A: a peaceful, cradle-like melody in the cellos over slowly plucked basses.
- Theme B: a lament for the oboe, in the minor, which rises to anguished climaxes.
- The bell-like bass: a slow, tolling figure in the low strings that is almost always present.

**Things to notice**

1. The E major outburst near the end comes out of near silence and soon sinks back into it. It is brief, yet the whole movement seems to lead to it.
2. Mahler said the movement came to him from the image of a tombstone carved with the figure of the dead person, arms folded, in eternal sleep.

### IV. Sehr behaglich

**Summary.** A soprano sings "Das himmlische Leben", the song from which the symphony grew: heaven described by a child, as a place of dancing, feasting and saints in the kitchen. Mahler asks her to sing "with childlike, cheerful expression". Between the verses, the sleigh bells of the first movement rush in. The last verse moves to E major, soft and mysterious, and the symphony ends in a whisper.

**Main ideas**

- The song tune: a simple, skipping melody, first heard on the clarinet.
- The refrain: the sleigh bells of the first movement, now wild and hurried.
- The chorale: a slow, hymn-like phrase at the end of each verse, "Saint Peter in heaven looks on".

1. The text is naïve, but the music is not: listen for the second verse, where the joyful heaven includes a lamb led to its death, and the music turns uneasy.
2. The final verse sings that "all things awake to joy". The music then grows quieter and quieter instead of louder.

### Threads

- **A song at the end.** The finale's song existed eight years before the symphony. The sleigh bells, the trumpet's tune in the E major blaze of the slow movement, and many melodic shapes all anticipate it, so the symphony seems to find its way to a song it already knew.
- **Innocence with shadows.** Sleigh bells and a child's heaven, but also Death's fiddle, the oboe's lament and a lamb led to the slaughter. Mahler keeps both in view.
- **Ending soft and in another key.** The symphony starts in G major and ends in E major, very quietly. Instead of a triumph, it closes with a child's vision fading away.
