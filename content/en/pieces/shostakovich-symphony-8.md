### The big picture

- Shostakovich wrote the symphony between 2 July and 9 September 1943, mostly at the Composers' Union retreat at Ivanovo, north-east of Moscow. The Red Army had won at Stalingrad that winter and was now pushing west. A victory symphony was expected.
- Instead he wrote an hour of music that is mostly grief and violence, and that ends very quietly. In public he summed up its idea as "life is beautiful"; his friend Isaak Glikman called it his most tragic work.
- Evgeny Mravinsky, to whom it is dedicated, conducted the USSR State Symphony Orchestra in the first performances in Moscow on 3 and 4 November 1943. The response was cool. Officials missed a triumphant ending, it was attacked at a meeting of composers in 1944, and after the 1948 campaign against "formalism" it was in effect banned until 1956.
- In the West it was briefly called the "Stalingrad" Symphony, a title Shostakovich never gave it.
- In one line: a long lament and an outcry (I), a grotesque march (II), a machine (III), a funeral [[passacaglia]] (IV), a pastoral finale that fades into silence (V). The last three are played without a break.

### I. Adagio – Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** The longest movement, more than a third of the symphony. It begins slowly, with long, searching melodies. Gradually it speeds up and breaks out into music of terrible violence. After the climax, a single cor anglais sings a long, lonely solo, and the movement ends almost in silence.

**Main ideas**

- The opening motto: a jagged, loud rhythm in the low strings, answered by the violins.
- Theme 1: a long, quiet violin melody over a limping, off-beat accompaniment.
- Theme 2: a violin melody in five beats to the bar, over an uneven pulse.

### II. Allegretto

**Summary.** A short, loud, swaggering march with an edge of mockery. Shostakovich called it "a march with elements of a scherzo". The orchestra's extremes, the shrill piccolo and the growling contrabassoon, are in the spotlight.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 0:00 | A loud, heavy, swaggering march tune; brass and shrill woodwinds jeer | A grotesque march |

### III. Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** A [[toccata]]: one line of even, hammering notes that never stops, like a machine. Over it the orchestra shrieks and stamps. The conductor Kurt Sanderling, who knew Shostakovich, heard it as the crushing of the individual. It crashes straight into the next movement.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The violas, loud, hammer out even notes, all the same length; the other strings stab chords | The machine starts |
| ≈ 0:35 | The violins take over the hammering; a two-note wail cuts across | The same [[ostinato]], higher |
| ≈ 1:30 | All the violins hammer together, then the woodwinds in [[unison]] | The machine grows |
| ≈ 2:40 | A solo trumpet plays a brash, galloping tune over the snare drum and the strings | The middle section: a fanfare from a circus or a parade ground |
| ≈ 4:30 | The whole orchestra, drums and cymbals, louder and louder | The biggest climax in the symphony |
| Last seconds | The climax does not end; it breaks straight into the Largo | No pause |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the hammering notes. They hardly ever stop, and they never change their length.

### IV. Largo

**Summary.** A [[passacaglia]], a set of variations over a slow bass line repeated again and again. The bass line grows out of the opening of the symphony. After the explosion of the toccata, this is the stillness of mourning.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The whole orchestra, very loud, still carrying the weight of the toccata; then the sound collapses | The bass theme, stated at full force |
| ≈ 1:00 | Cellos and basses, quiet and slow, repeat the same long line | The [[ground bass]], which is heard eleven times |
| ≈ 2:30 | A horn sings a long, sad melody above the bass | A lament over the repeating line |
| ≈ 4:00–7:30 | Lonely solos high above: a piccolo, very high and thin, and a clarinet, over flutes fluttering in place | Desolation. Nothing moves forward |
| Last minute | Very quiet; the music slips straight into the finale | No pause |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the slow bass line under everything. It never changes; only the music above it does.

### V. Allegretto

**Summary.** C major arrives, but not as a victory. The finale is a pastoral, a gentle, rambling set of episodes. In the middle the violence of the first movement breaks in once more. Then the music thins out and ends in a long, soft major chord, with a question still hanging in the air.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A solo bassoon, quiet, plays a lilting tune; the violins take it up | The main theme, almost a country dance |
| ≈ 1:30 | A solo flute over plucked string chords that make three beats sound like two | A first episode |
| ≈ 2:00 | The bassoon's tune returns in the violins, then in the cellos | The main theme comes back, as in a [[rondo]] |
| ≈ 5:30 | The instruments enter one after another with the same tune | A [[fugato]]; the music begins to gather strength |
| ≈ 8:00 | A huge, crashing climax, the cry of the first movement once more | The first movement's catastrophe returns |
| ≈ 9:30 | Quiet again; solo instruments recall earlier themes | The aftermath |
| ≈ 12:30 | The violins hold a soft C major chord; below it, the lower strings pluck a short three-note figure | The [[coda]]. The figure is the beginning of a theme that never continues |
| Last 30 seconds | The chord fades away | The end, *pianissimo* |

**Things to notice**

1. The symphony ends in C major, but very softly. Compare it with the blazing end of the Seventh: one year later, Shostakovich gave his listeners no victory celebration.

**In this recording.** Kirill Petrenko, chief conductor of the Berlin Philharmonic, conducted this performance on 13 November 2020, when Berlin's concert halls were closed because of the pandemic. It was streamed live from the empty Philharmonie and released in 2023. Petrenko has called the symphony an "incredible psychological drama".

### Threads

- **One seed.** Almost everything grows out of the opening bars: the passacaglia's bass line comes from there, and the first movement's cry returns in the finale.
- **The war without victory.** The first movement's violence, the march, the machine of the toccata: none of it leads to triumph. The answer is a lament, and a quiet C major.
- **Single voices.** After each catastrophe a single instrument speaks: the cor anglais in the first movement, the horn and piccolo in the Largo, the bassoon at the start of the finale.
