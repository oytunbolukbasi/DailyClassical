### The big picture

- Beethoven finished it in October 1826 at Gneixendorf, his brother Johann's country estate on the Danube, where he had gone with his nephew Karl. It was the last major work he completed. Only a new finale for an earlier quartet, Op. 130, came after it. He died in March 1827.
- After the long, unusual quartets that came before it, this one goes back to four movements and modest length, closer to Haydn and Mozart. It is the shortest of Beethoven's late quartets.
- Above the slow opening of the finale Beethoven wrote "Muss es sein?" ("Must it be?"), and above the quick answer "Es muss sein!" ("It must be!"). The phrase began as a joke: a music lover who wanted the quartet Op. 130 played in his home was told he must pay for it, and Beethoven wrote a comic canon on the words "It must be! Out with the purse!". A later story about Beethoven's housekeeper asking for her wages was invented by his biographer Anton Schindler.
- He dedicated it to Johann Nepomuk Wolfmayer, a Viennese cloth merchant and a long-standing admirer. It was first played in public on 23 March 1828, at a concert in Beethoven's memory.
- In one line: a light, witty opening (I), a rough, wild scherzo (II), a slow song of peace (III), a question and its answer (IV).

### I. Allegretto

**Summary.** A light, conversational movement in [[sonata form]]. Short scraps of tune are tossed from one instrument to another, more like a conversation than a melody with accompaniment.

**Main ideas**

- The opening figure: a short, questioning phrase in the viola, answered by a little flourish in the first violin.
- The unison: all four together in octaves, a few firm steps.
- Theme 2: brighter, with quick, rippling triplets.

### II. Vivace

**Summary.** A short, fast [[scherzo]] with a stubborn, off-beat rhythm. In the middle section the music climbs up through three keys, and then the three lower instruments repeat the same small figure, very loudly, for about fifty bars, while the first violin leaps wildly above them.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: a simple tune that keeps tripping over the beat, the instruments seemingly out of step with each other.
- The trio: fast running notes in the first violin, climbing from key to key.
- The stuck figure: one short, loud figure hammered again and again by the lower three.

### III. Lento assai, cantante e tranquillo

**Summary.** A slow, hushed song in D-flat major. In his sketches Beethoven called it a "sweet song of rest, or song of peace". It is a theme with four [[variation]]s, but the tune hardly changes; what changes is what surrounds it.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a slow, quiet hymn played low by all four instruments.
- The dark variation: slower, in the minor, with broken, halting phrases.
- The cello's turn: the theme in the cello, almost unchanged, with the first violin imitating it a step behind.

### IV. Grave, ma non troppo tratto – Allegro

**Summary.** Beethoven headed the finale *Der schwer gefasste Entschluss*, "The difficult decision". A slow, heavy question in F minor ("Must it be?") is answered by a quick, cheerful tune in F major ("It must be!"). The question returns once, louder, but the answer wins, and the quartet ends in good humour.

**Main ideas**

- The question: three slow notes in the low instruments, in the minor, "Muss es sein?".
- The answer: the same three notes turned upside down and made quick and bright, "Es muss sein!".
- Theme 2: a light, tiptoeing tune, almost a march for fairies.

### Threads

- **Back to the beginning.** After the vast late quartets, Beethoven returns to four short movements and the conversational style of Haydn and Mozart.
- **Four voices.** The viola opens the first movement, the lower three drive the scherzo, the cello sings in the slow movement, and in the finale everyone takes part in the question and answer.
- **A last word.** The question and answer were Beethoven's joke, but his final completed work ends with "It must be!". In Milan Kundera's novel *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* the hero borrows the phrase.
