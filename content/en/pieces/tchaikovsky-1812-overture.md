### The big picture

- In 1880 Nikolay Rubinstein asked Tchaikovsky for a festival piece for the All-Russian Arts and Industrial Exhibition, planned for Moscow in 1881. The overture recalls 1812, the year Napoleon invaded Russia, took Moscow and then lost almost his whole army in the retreat. The Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, built in Moscow to give thanks for that victory, was nearly finished.
- Tchaikovsky did not want the job. He wrote it in the autumn of 1880, alongside the *Serenade for Strings*, and told his patron Nadezhda von Meck that the overture would be "very loud and noisy", written "without any warm and loving feelings", and so probably of little artistic worth.
- After the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in March 1881 the exhibition was put off by a year. The official premiere was in Moscow, at the exhibition, on 20 August 1882, conducted by Ippolit Altani. It was a success from the start, and Tchaikovsky conducted it more often than any other work of his.
- The overture tells the story with borrowed tunes: an Orthodox hymn for the Russian people at prayer, the *Marseillaise* for the French army, a Russian folk song, and at the end the tsarist anthem "God Save the Tsar". Neither anthem quite fits 1812: Napoleon had dropped the *Marseillaise* as France's anthem, and "God Save the Tsar" was written only in 1833.
- The score asks for bells and for cannon shots. Tchaikovsky's note says the "cannon" is the theatre's instrument for a cannon shot, a large drum. Real cannons are a later tradition, used in many recordings, including this one.
- In one line: prayer, the call to arms, the battle between the two anthems, the retreat in winter and the bells of victory.

### I. Largo – Allegro giusto

**Summary.** The overture begins very quietly, with a hymn for a few strings alone, and ends with some of the loudest music Tchaikovsky wrote: full orchestra, bells, brass and cannons. In between, a call to arms, a long battle in which the *Marseillaise* seems to be winning, a Russian song and a folk dance, and a rushing retreat. Then the opening hymn returns in triumph.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: "O Lord, save thy people", the Orthodox troparion of the Holy Cross, played by four cellos and two violas.
- The *Marseillaise*: its first notes, as a short, jaunty call in the brass, the sign of the French army.
- The lyrical theme: a broad, warm melody for the strings, taken from a duet in Tchaikovsky's own abandoned opera *The Voyevoda*.
- The folk song: "By the Gates", a short, quick Russian dance tune in the woodwinds, which Tchaikovsky had arranged for piano duet in 1868.
- "God Save the Tsar": the anthem of Tchaikovsky's Russia, blared out by the brass at the end.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the opening. Only six string players play the hymn, the sound of a small choir in a church. Remember it: the same hymn returns at the end with the full orchestra and the bells.
2. Follow the *Marseillaise*. Each time it comes back it is louder and closer, until it is swept away by the long falling scale.

**In this recording.** Telarc's 1979 album was an early digital recording, and it became famous for its cannons: real cannon shots, credited on the album to the Fifth Virginia Regiment. They are recorded at a very high level, and the album came with a warning about loudspeakers. It is worth starting at a moderate volume.

### Threads

- **Music to order.** Tchaikovsky wrote the overture for an exhibition, without enthusiasm, and expected little of it. It became one of his most performed works, and he kept conducting it.
- **Tunes that tell the story.** Almost every theme is a borrowed tune with a meaning: the hymn for faith, the *Marseillaise* for the enemy, a folk song for the Russian people, the anthem for the tsar.
- **Bells and cannons.** Tchaikovsky asked for large bells, "struck in the manner of celebratory ringing", and for a theatre drum to stand in for the cannon. The hymn of the opening and the bells of the ending frame the whole battle as a prayer and its answer.
