### The big picture

- In 1872 Saint-Saëns set a short poem by Henri Cazalis as a song for voice and piano. In the poem, Death strikes a tomb with his heel at midnight, plays a dance tune on his violin, and the skeletons dance in their shrouds until the cock crows. The poem's title is "Égalité, Fraternité": in the dance, the lord and the peasant are the same.
- In 1874 he turned the song into a [[symphonic poem]] for orchestra. The voice disappears, and Death's violin becomes a real solo violin. The first performance was in Paris on 24 January 1875, conducted by Édouard Colonne.
- The orchestra includes a xylophone, still a rarity in a symphony orchestra at the time, for the rattle of dry bones. Saint-Saëns borrowed the idea again in "Fossils" from *The Carnival of the Animals*.
- The whole piece is a waltz, in three beats, in G minor. Two tunes carry it: a jerky one, first on the flute, and a sliding one on the violin.
- In one line: midnight, Death tunes up, the dance starts quietly, grows wild, and stops dead at dawn.

### I. Mouvement modéré de valse

**Summary.** One continuous movement, a story told in order. The harp strikes midnight, the solo violin tunes up with a harsh, out-of-tune chord, and two waltz tunes start the dance. They pass round the orchestra, are piled up against each other and grow louder, with a mocking quotation of an old funeral chant halfway through. At the height of the frenzy an oboe crows, the dance breaks off, and the dead slip back into their graves.

**Main ideas**

- The first waltz tune: short, jerky phrases that keep falling, first on the flute, later rattled out by the xylophone.
- The second waltz tune: a long, smooth line sliding down a scale, played by the solo violin over soft string chords.
- Death's violin: the soloist's top string is tuned down a semitone, so the open strings sound A and E-flat together, a clashing interval old theorists called "the devil in music". The retuning is called [[scordatura]].

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the solo violin's first chord. It sounds wrong on purpose: the instrument has been retuned so that Death's fiddle grates.
2. The two waltz tunes start far apart, on the flute and the violin. Notice how, near the end, they sound at the same time.

**In this recording.** Charles Dutoit recorded the piece with the Philharmonia Orchestra in Kingsway Hall, London, in 1980, for an album of Saint-Saëns's four symphonic poems. On the Spotify album it is track 1, followed by *Phaéton* and *Le rouet d'Omphale*.

### Threads

- **A song without words.** The piece follows Cazalis's poem line by line: the twelve strokes, the heel on the tomb, the violin, the skeletons, the cock. The voice is gone, but the story is all there.
- **One idea, many colours.** Saint-Saëns builds seven minutes from two short waltz tunes. What changes is the orchestra: flute, violin, xylophone, a crowd of strings, then everyone at once.
- **Death made light.** The subject is grim, but the tone is ironic: a waltz, a cheeky Dies irae, a rooster. The fear is part of the fun.
