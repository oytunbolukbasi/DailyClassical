### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the symphony in 1772, aged forty, as music director to Prince Nikolaus Esterházy. The prince's court spent its summers at Eszterháza, his new palace in the Hungarian countryside.
- Haydn told the story himself in old age, to his biographers Griesinger and Dies. That year the stay at Eszterháza went on longer than expected, and most of the musicians had left their wives at home in Eisenstadt. Instead of asking the prince outright, Haydn put the request into the music.
- In the last pages of the finale the players stop one after another, snuff out the candle on their music stand and leave, until only two muted violins are left: Haydn himself and his leader, Luigi Tomasini. The prince seems to have understood. According to the story, the court left for Eisenstadt the next day.
- The key, F-sharp minor, was very unusual. This seems to be the only known symphony of the 18th century written in it, and Haydn had to order special slides so that the horns could play in it.
- In one line: a fierce, driving storm (I), a slow song for muted violins (II), a quiet, hesitant minuet (III), a fast finale that breaks off and turns into the slow farewell (IV).

### I. Allegro assai

**Summary.** A storm from the first bar. The violins plunge down through the notes of the minor chord while the second violins throb off the beat beneath them. There is no gentle second theme where one would expect it; instead, near the end of the middle section, the music suddenly stops for a calm new tune that is never heard again.

**Main ideas**

- The plunging theme: the first violins race down the notes of the F-sharp minor chord, over restless, off-beat notes in the second violins and long chords in the winds.
- The new tune: a quiet, graceful melody in the strings, in D major, almost like a minuet, which appears only once.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A long, gentle song in A major, played by violins with mutes. Its calm keeps clouding over: the music slips into the minor and back, and slow, aching harmonies hang over from one bar to the next. Some writers hear in it the musicians' longing for home.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a soft, swaying melody for the [[muted]] violins, with a small figure of repeated notes that sounds like a hiccup.
- The shadows: passages that turn from major to minor and back.
- The aching chords: notes held over into the next bar, so that the harmony clashes for a moment before it resolves.

### IV. Finale: Presto – Adagio

**Summary.** The finale starts as a fast, fierce movement in F-sharp minor. When it seems about to end, it breaks off on an open chord, and a second slow movement begins in A major. One after another the players have a few last notes and leave, and the music grows thinner until two muted violins finish alone, very softly, in F-sharp major.

**Main ideas**

- The Presto: fast, driving music in the minor, with the violins racing.
- The farewell: a calm, gentle tune in three, in A major, played first by the whole orchestra.
- The departures: little solos for the leaving players, in this order: first oboe and second horn, then the bassoon, second oboe and first horn, double bass, cello, the violins of the orchestra, the viola.

**In this recording.** Doráti and the Philharmonia Hungarica made the first widely issued recording of all of Haydn's symphonies for Decca, between 1969 and 1972. The orchestra had been founded in 1956 near Vienna by Hungarian musicians who had fled their country.

### Threads

- **Music with a message.** Haydn did not ask the prince for leave; he let the orchestra show it. The idea only works in the finale, but the whole symphony has a restless, homesick tone.
- **Endings that do not end.** The minuet's sections stop on weak beats, the Presto breaks off, and the last chord is the quietest of all.
- **Storm and calm.** The fierce F-sharp minor of the first movement and the Presto frames two quiet movements, and the symphony ends in the major, very softly.
