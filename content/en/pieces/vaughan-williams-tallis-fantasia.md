### The big picture

- The theme is a hymn tune by Thomas Tallis, one of nine he wrote in 1567 for Archbishop Matthew Parker's book of psalms in English. It sets Psalm 2 and is in the Phrygian mode, an old scale that sounds neither major nor minor.
- Vaughan Williams found it while he was editing *The English Hymnal* (1906), where he set it to another hymn text. Four years later the Three Choirs Festival asked him for a new work, and he built this fantasia on it.
- He conducted the first performance himself, with the London Symphony Orchestra, in Gloucester Cathedral on 6 September 1910. The second half of the concert was Elgar's *The Dream of Gerontius*. The cathedral organist called the new piece "a queer, mad work by an odd fellow from Chelsea", but the press was generally warm. He revised it in 1913 and 1919, making it shorter each time.
- It is written for strings only, divided into three groups: a full string orchestra, a second, smaller orchestra of nine players, and a string quartet. The groups answer each other like choirs in a cathedral, and the four quartet players step out as soloists.
- In one line: the old tune revealed, phrase by phrase; echoes between the groups; a solo viola and violin; a great climax; and the tune again, high and calm.

### I. Largo sostenuto

**Summary.** One continuous span of slow music. A hushed introduction hints at the tune; then the whole tune is sung by the middle strings, rising to a passionate climax. In four episodes Vaughan Williams breaks it apart: the two orchestras echo each other, a solo viola and the quartet take over, a swaying figure builds to the biggest climax of all and fades. At the end the tune returns, with the solo violin and viola singing above a soft cushion of strings, and the piece settles on a quiet major chord.

**Main ideas**

- Tallis's tune: four slow phrases, each rising and falling within a narrow range, with the old, slightly dark colour of the Phrygian mode.
- The sway: a short, rocking figure of chords that returns between the phrases like a refrain.
- The soloists: the viola and the violin of the string quartet, who carry the most personal versions of the tune.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the space. The three groups answer each other, and the second orchestra, smaller and softer, often sounds as if it were further away. In a resonant church the effect is strongest.
2. Follow the solo viola in the middle. It is the moment the old hymn turns into something personal, as if one person in the congregation began to sing alone.

**In this recording.** Barbirolli recorded the Fantasia on 17 May 1962 in the Temple Church in London, a resonant medieval church; the rest of the original LP, *English String Music*, with Elgar's *Introduction and Allegro* and *Serenade*, was recorded a week earlier at Kingsway Hall. The string quartet is the Allegri Quartet. On this Spotify album, a 2022 remastering, it is track 1.

### Threads

- **Old and new.** A tune from 1567, harmonies that could only have been written in the 20th century. The *Times* critic at the first performance wrote that one was never quite sure whether one was listening to something very old or very new.
- **A building made of sound.** Three groups of strings, separated and then joined, imitate the way choirs answer each other across a cathedral.
- **Before *The Lark Ascending*.** The Fantasia was Vaughan Williams's breakthrough, at 37, four years before *The Lark Ascending*: the same composer, looking back to Tudor church music here and to the English countryside there.
