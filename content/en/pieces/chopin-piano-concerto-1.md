### The big picture

- Chopin wrote it in Warsaw in 1830, when he was twenty. It was actually the second of his two piano concertos. It is called No. 1 only because it was published first.
- He played it on 11 October 1830 at the National Theatre in Warsaw, in one of his farewell concerts before going abroad; a local paper counted about 700 listeners. Three weeks later he left Poland, and he never returned. Before the year was out, the November Uprising against Russian rule had begun.
- It is a young pianist's concerto, written for himself to play. The orchestra mostly frames and supports the piano, and the piano does nearly all the talking: long, ornamented melodies like an opera singer's, and sparkling runs.
- Chopin told his friend Tytus Woyciechowski that the slow movement was "a kind of reverie in the moonlight on a beautiful spring evening". The finale borrows the rhythm of the krakowiak, a lively dance from Kraków.
- In one line: a long, proud and songful Allegro (I), a moonlit romance (II), a Polish dance (III).

### I. Allegro maestoso

**Summary.** The orchestra plays alone for more than four minutes, setting out the themes. Then the piano bursts in and takes over: it sings the same tunes with long, decorated lines and links them with glittering runs. The movement is long and in [[sonata form]], but it feels like a series of songs for the piano.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, proud: a firm, stately tune in E minor, first played loud by the whole orchestra.
- Theme 2, longing: a soft, singing melody in the minor, first on the strings.
- Theme 3, warm: a gentle, rocking melody in E major, the brightest moment of the movement.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the piano decorates a slow melody. It adds trills, turns and little rushes of notes, like a singer ornamenting an aria.
2. The orchestra hardly ever plays while the piano sings. It waits, accompanies softly, and comes in loud only between the piano's sections.

**In this recording.** Zimerman recorded the concertos in 1999, the 150th anniversary of Chopin's death, with the Polish Festival Orchestra, which he directs from the piano. His tempos are unusually broad, with time for every phrase to breathe: the first movement lasts more than 23 minutes, against 19 in Argerich's 1968 recording, recommended above.

### II. Romance. Larghetto

**Summary.** A slow, dreamy song for the piano over [[muted]] strings, close to the piano [[nocturne]]s for which Chopin became famous. It is the "moonlit reverie" of his letter: calm, tender, a little sad. A restless passage in the middle passes, and the melody returns, more decorated than before.

**Main ideas**

- The romance: a slow, singing melody in E major, richly decorated by the piano.
- The middle section: quicker, rustling figures in the piano, more troubled.

### III. Rondo. Vivace

**Summary.** A bright, dancing [[rondo]] in E major. The main tune bounces, with accents that land off the beat, the stamping rhythm of the [[krakowiak]], a dance from Kraków. The piano races through glittering runs and ends the concerto in a blaze.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo tune: a quick, playful melody in E major, with little accents on the weak beats.
- The octave tune: a second tune with both hands playing the same notes, and a heavy, stamping rhythm.

### Threads

- **The piano as a singer.** Chopin's piano melodies are decorated the way an opera singer decorates an aria: trills, turns and long, flowing runs.
- **Goodbye to Warsaw.** It was written and first played in Warsaw, weeks before Chopin left Poland forever.
- **Polish dance.** The finale's krakowiak is one of the Polish dance rhythms that run through Chopin's music, with his mazurkas and polonaises.
- **Major and minor.** The first movement keeps turning from E minor to E major. The last two movements stay in the major, and the concerto ends in bright E major.
