### The big picture

- In June 1884 the Philharmonic Society of London, which had once commissioned Beethoven's Ninth, made Dvořák an honorary member and asked him for a new symphony. He began it on 13 December 1884 and finished it on 17 March 1885.
- He had recently heard Brahms's new Third Symphony and admired it. He aimed for a work on the scale of Brahms and Beethoven, and wrote that it must be capable of stirring the world.
- Under the main theme he wrote that it came to him at the station, as the festive train from Pest arrived in 1884: hundreds of Hungarians and Hungarian Czechs coming to Prague's National Theatre, a visit with a strong patriotic charge. How much that mood shaped the music is still debated.
- It is darker than almost anything else he wrote, and it leaves out the Slavonic folk tunes he was known for. He conducted the premiere in London on 22 April 1885. His publisher Simrock printed it as "Symphony No. 2", after long arguments about the fee and about printing his first name in Czech.
- In one line: a stormy opening that ends in a whisper (I), a slow prayer with sudden outbursts (II), a fierce, swirling dance (III), a hard-won victory in D major (IV).

### I. Allegro maestoso

**Summary.** It begins very quietly, with a low, held note and a rumble of timpani like distant thunder, under a dark tune in the violas and cellos. Two worlds then struggle through the movement: that brooding theme and a gentle, warm second tune. The storm builds to a huge climax near the end, then dies away, and the movement closes as quietly as it began.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a low, creeping tune in the violas and cellos that stays within a few notes, then suddenly rises and breaks off on a tense chord. This is the "train" theme.
- Theme 2: a soft, consoling melody in B-flat major for flutes and clarinets.

### II. Poco adagio

**Summary.** A slow movement that starts like a prayer and keeps being shaken by stronger feelings. Woodwinds open with a quiet, hymn-like phrase; flowing melodies follow, then a pastoral horn tune leads to a powerful climax. It ends in peace, with the woodwinds fading away.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a calm, hymn-like phrase on the clarinet, with oboe and bassoon weaving around it.
- The main theme: a gentle melody for flute and oboe.
- The horn tune: a warm, country-like melody at the centre of the movement.

### III. Scherzo: Vivace – Poco meno mosso

**Summary.** A fast, swirling dance in D minor with the spirit of a Czech [[furiant]]: the beat seems to switch between two big pulses and three, so the rhythm keeps tugging against itself. A slower, sunlit middle section in G major gives a rest before the dance returns and spins into a long, fierce ending.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: cellos and bassoons play the main tune while the violins add a second line against it, with cross-accents that blur the beat.
- The middle section: brighter and slower, with a tune that the instruments take up one after another, as in a [[canon]].

### IV. Finale: Allegro

**Summary.** The finale begins in the shadows and fights its way to the light. A tense theme with a rising leap drives the music forward; a folk-like cello melody in A major offers warmth; and after a stormy development the symphony breaks through, at the very end, into broad D major chords.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a gripping tune that starts with a leap up an octave, first in clarinets and horns, then in the whole orchestra.
- Theme 2: a warm, singing melody in the cellos, like a folk song, in A major.
- The closing tune: a firm, marching idea in the violins, used heavily in the development.

**In this recording.** Szell, born in Budapest, recorded the symphony with the Cleveland Orchestra in 1960. The first LP still called it "Symphony No. 2", the number Simrock had given it, with a note that it is now known as No. 7.

### Threads

- **Darkness to light.** The first movement ends in a whisper and the scherzo in fury; only the last bars of the finale reach D major.
- **A symphony for the world.** Dvořák wrote it for London and wanted it to stand beside Brahms and Beethoven; the tight construction, with no wasted notes, is part of that ambition.
- **Czech pride.** Folk tunes are mostly left out, but the furiant rhythm of the scherzo, the folk-like cello song in the finale and the patriotic train in the first movement keep Bohemia close.
