### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it in 1821–22, aged fifty-one. It is the last of his thirty-two piano sonatas and the last of three he planned together (Opp. 109, 110 and 111). He dedicated it to his pupil and patron Archduke Rudolf.
- It has only two movements: a stormy Allegro in C minor and a long, slow set of [[variation]]s in C major. His secretary Anton Schindler, an unreliable witness, claimed that Beethoven, asked why there was no third movement, answered that he had not had time. The sketches suggest he dropped the idea of a third movement himself.
- Contemporaries largely ignored it. Leading pianists took it up only in the second half of the 19th century.
- In Thomas Mann's novel *Doctor Faustus* a music teacher gives a whole lecture on why Op. 111 needs no third movement: after this ending, he says, there could be no return.
- In one line: struggle (I), and a slow song that is varied until it seems to dissolve into light (II).

### I. Maestoso – Allegro con brio ed appassionato

**Summary.** A slow, grim introduction of hammered chords and wide leaps, then a fierce, fast movement in [[sonata form]], driven by a short, angry theme that keeps turning into counterpoint. At the very end the storm dies away into a quiet C major chord.

**Main ideas**

- The Maestoso: loud, harsh chords in a jagged dotted rhythm, with the left hand leaping a long way down; then low trills rumbling in the bass.
- Theme 1: a short, fierce figure, first in bare octaves low in the bass, then piled up in several voices like a [[fugue]].
- Theme 2: brief and gentle, in A-flat major; the music slows almost to a stop.

1. Listen to how short the gentle second theme is. It barely gets started before the storm takes over again.
2. The last chord is major, and quiet. It already points to the second movement.

### II. Arietta. Adagio molto semplice e cantabile

**Summary.** A slow, simple tune ("arietta" means little song), then a chain of [[variation]]s. The beat stays the same throughout, but the notes get faster in each variation, until the music becomes rhythmic, almost dance-like. Then it turns quiet, rises to the top of the keyboard, and ends in a long, shimmering haze of [[trill]]s.

**Main ideas**

- The Arietta: a slow, calm melody in C major, in short phrases, each half played twice.
- The quickening: in each of the first three variations the notes are subdivided further, so the same pulse feels faster and more excited.
- The trills: long, held trills that make the high notes shimmer, as if the sound were suspended.

1. Tap the slow beat at the start and keep tapping. In variation 3 the beat is the same; only the notes inside it have become faster.
2. In the coda, listen to the right hand. One finger sustains a trill while others still play the melody.

### Threads

- **Two worlds.** Minor and major, fast and slow, struggle and calm: the two movements are as opposite as Beethoven could make them.
- **The whole keyboard.** The first movement digs into the lowest notes of the piano; the second ends at its very top.
- **Counting time.** The theme's slow pulse never changes, yet the music speeds up and slows down by changing how finely each beat is divided.
- **The last sonata.** After Op. 111 Beethoven wrote more piano music, the Diabelli Variations and the Bagatelles, but never another piano sonata.
