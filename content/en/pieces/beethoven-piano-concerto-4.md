### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it in 1805–06, in his mid-thirties, in the same years as the Fourth Symphony and the Violin Concerto. He dedicated it to his pupil and patron Archduke Rudolf.
- It was first heard privately in March 1807, at the Vienna palace of Prince Lobkowitz. Beethoven played the solo part at the public premiere on 22 December 1808, in a marathon concert that also brought the first performances of the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies. It was his last appearance as soloist with an orchestra.
- The opening broke the rules: instead of a long orchestral introduction, the piano begins alone, softly. Early critics found that strange. After Beethoven's death the concerto was rarely played until Felix Mendelssohn revived it in 1836.
- In the slow movement loud, stern strings and a quiet, pleading piano answer each other. In 1859 the writer Adolf Bernhard Marx compared it to Orpheus taming the Furies at the gates of the underworld. The image is his, not Beethoven's, but it has stuck.
- In one line: a gentle, spacious opening (I), a short dialogue between stern strings and a pleading piano (II), a lively dance that follows without a break (III).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The piano begins alone with a few soft chords. The orchestra answers in a surprising, far-off key, then plays its own long introduction. When the piano returns, it takes the themes and spins them out in long, flowing passages. Late in the movement comes a [[cadenza]] for the piano alone.

**Main ideas**

- The opening theme: soft, repeated chords in the piano, calm and questioning, ending on an open chord.
- The orchestra's reply: the same theme, but in B major, a key far from G, which makes it sound like a sudden change of light.
- The flowing piano writing: running notes, [[trill]]s and wide [[arpeggio]]s that decorate the themes.

### II. Andante con moto

**Summary.** A short movement for strings and piano only. The strings play loud, short, stern phrases all together; the piano answers each time softly, in slow chords like a prayer. Gradually the strings grow quieter, until the piano is left almost alone.

**Main ideas**

- The strings: loud, short, detached notes, all playing the same line in [[unison]].
- The piano: soft, slow, singing chords, answering each phrase.
- The climax: a passage for the piano alone, with [[trill]]s, rising to a cry.

### III. Rondo. Vivace

**Summary.** A lively, dancing [[rondo]]. The strings begin very quietly with a march-like tune, in the "wrong" key of C major, before the music finds its way to G. Trumpets and timpani, silent until now, join in at the first loud outburst. Near the end comes a short cadenza and a fast, joyful close.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a quick, march-like tune, first very soft in the strings, then taken up by the piano.
- Theme 2: a smoother, singing melody.
- The full orchestra: trumpets and drums, heard here for the first time in the concerto.

### Threads

- **Soft beginnings.** The piano opens the concerto softly and alone, the slow movement is won by the quiet voice, and even the finale starts in a whisper.
- **Unexpected keys.** The orchestra's first entry in B major, the slow movement in E minor, and the finale's first notes in C major all start away from G and find their way back.
- **Who leads.** The piano begins the concerto, the strings lead the slow movement until the piano wins them over, and in the finale the two share the lead.
- **Saved for last.** Trumpets and timpani are kept silent until the finale.
