### The big picture

- Dvořák wrote the Serenade in May 1875, between the 3rd and the 14th: about twelve days. He was thirty-three, two years married, and had just won an Austrian state grant for the first time.
- A [[serenade]] was originally light evening music, often played outdoors. Dvořák's is for string orchestra alone, in five short movements; all but the finale have a simple song-like shape with a contrasting middle.
- It was first played in Prague on 10 December 1876, at the Žofín Palace, conducted by Adolf Čech. It was such a success that it was programmed again at once. Leoš Janáček conducted it in Brno the next spring, and in August 1877 it was the work with which Dvořák made his first documented appearance as a conductor.
- At the end of the finale, the opening tune of the first movement comes back, so the whole work closes where it began.
- In one line: a gentle song (I), a wistful waltz (II), a playful chase (III), a tender slow movement (IV), a lively village dance that looks back before it ends (V).

### I. Moderato

**Summary.** A calm, singing opening. Over a steady pulse in the violas, the second violins and cellos share a warm melody, and the first violins soon float above it. A livelier middle section with a skipping rhythm briefly changes the mood before the song returns.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a gentle, lyrical melody passed between second violins and cellos, in E major.
- The dance: a bouncier tune in G major, built on a dotted, skipping rhythm.

- The waltz: a lilting, wistful tune in C-sharp minor.
- The second tune: a more flowing melody in a major key.
- The middle section: a smoother, more continuous dance in D-flat major.

### III. Scherzo: Vivace

**Summary.** A quick, playful [[scherzo]] in F major. Its main tune is a [[canon]]: one group of strings starts it, and another follows close behind with the same notes, as if chasing it. A broader, singing section gives a moment of calm, but the chase keeps coming back.

**Main ideas**

- The chase: a bright, bouncing tune that one group plays and another imitates a moment later.
- The song: a broad, lyrical melody in a contrasting middle section.

**Summary.** The quiet heart of the Serenade: a slow, tender song in A major. It begins very softly, grows warmer, passes through a more restless middle section and returns to its first calm.

**Main ideas**

### V. Finale: Allegro vivace

**Summary.** A lively finale with the energy of a Bohemian village dance, full of accents on the "wrong" beats. It brings three tunes, recalls the Larghetto, then the opening song of the first movement, and ends with a quick dash to three firm E major chords.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a lively, dotted tune after a short opening of falling figures.
- Theme 2: a tune passed between violins and cellos over running notes in the violas.
- Theme 3: a busy, energetic tune, with violins and cellos imitating each other.

**In this recording.** Marriner and the Academy recorded the Serenade in May 1970 in London's Kingsway Hall, and the first LP paired it with Grieg's Holberg Suite.

### Threads

- **A happy spring.** A state grant, a young marriage and a growing reputation: the Serenade has hardly a dark moment, and even its minor-key waltz ends in the major.
- **Song and dance.** Every movement except the Larghetto dances: a gentle song with a skipping middle, a waltz, a chase, a village dance.
- **Coming full circle.** The finale recalls the slow movement and then the opening tune, so the work ends where it began.
