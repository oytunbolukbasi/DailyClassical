### The big picture

- Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941. Shostakovich was in Leningrad, his home city, and wrote the first three movements there over the summer and early autumn, as German armies surrounded the city and the bombing began. In September he spoke on Leningrad radio, telling listeners he had just finished two movements of a new symphony. In October he was evacuated, and he finished the work in Kuybyshev (today Samara) in December 1941.
- The first performance was in Kuybyshev on 5 March 1942, under Samuil Samosud. The score was copied onto microfilm and taken via Tehran to the West; Arturo Toscanini conducted it on American radio on 19 July 1942. Within months it was a symbol of resistance across the Allied world.
- In besieged Leningrad it was played on 9 August 1942. The Radio Orchestra under Karl Eliasberg had shrunk to about fifteen players; the rest were found around the city or sent from army bands, and the musicians got extra food. The concert was broadcast by radio and loudspeakers, and Soviet guns shelled the German positions beforehand to keep them quiet. A story often told says the date was the one Hitler had chosen to celebrate the city's fall with a banquet at the Astoria Hotel.
- What the symphony is about has been argued ever since. Officially it was the war against fascism. Some of Shostakovich's friends later said the famous march had been sketched before the invasion, and the memoir *Testimony*, published by Solomon Volkov in 1979 and disputed ever since, has him say the symphony is also about the Leningrad that Stalin had destroyed. He gave the movements titles, "War", "Reminiscences", "Home Expanses" and "Victory", and then withdrew them.
- In one line: peace, then an invasion (I), a gentle memory (II), a lament for the homeland (III), a long climb to victory (IV).

### I. Allegretto

**Summary.** The longest movement, and the most famous. Two calm themes paint a world at peace. Then a snare drum starts tapping far away, and a trivial little march tune is repeated, louder and louder, until it crushes everything. What follows the march is the part Shostakovich himself called the heart of the movement: a requiem.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a broad, striding melody in the strings, all playing together.
- Theme 2: a gentle violin song over a soft, swaying accompaniment.
- The march, known as the "invasion" theme: a jaunty, simple tune over a snare-drum rhythm that never changes. Its second half echoes a song from Lehár's operetta *The Merry Widow*.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the snare drum in the invasion episode. It plays the same rhythm for more than ten minutes without a break, while everything above it grows.
2. Shostakovich said he did not want to depict battle, with planes and tanks; he wanted to convey "the context of grim events". The march is not frightening at first. It becomes frightening by being repeated.

**In this recording.** Vasily Petrenko was born in Leningrad in 1976. He recorded the symphony in Liverpool in June 2012, as part of his complete cycle of Shostakovich's symphonies for Naxos. His first movement is broad, almost half an hour long.

### III. Adagio

**Summary.** The emotional centre of the symphony, first called "Home Expanses". Solemn wind chords alternate with a long, speaking melody in the violins. A fierce, fast middle section interrupts, and then the solemn music returns and leads straight into the finale.

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the flute's solo at ≈ 5:00 with the bassoon's requiem in the first movement. Shostakovich gives the most personal music in this huge orchestra to single wind players.

### IV. Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** The finale begins in near silence and has to fight its way to victory. A fast, driving struggle, a slow and heavy lament, and then one long, steady build to the end, where the very first theme of the symphony returns in a blaze of C major.

**Things to notice**

1. The tune at ≈ 17:00 is Theme 1 from the very start of the symphony. Eighty minutes later it returns, and the question is whether it sounds like the same music.

### Threads

- **Marches and machines.** The invasion march in the first movement, the hammering middle of the second, the driving figures in the Adagio and the finale: repeated rhythms keep taking over.
- **Single voices.** In between the masses of sound, single players carry the grief: the bassoon's requiem, the oboe in the scherzo, the flute in the Adagio.
- **A symbol and a puzzle.** In 1942 the symphony was a public weapon, played in a starving city and broadcast to the world. What Shostakovich meant by it in private is still debated.
