### The big picture

- *The Firebird* was Stravinsky's first ballet, written in 1909–10 for Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes. Diaghilev wanted a ballet on a Russian subject for the 1910 Paris season. He first asked other composers, then gave the commission to Stravinsky, who was 27 and almost unknown.
- The premiere at the Paris Opéra on 25 June 1910, with choreography by Michel Fokine and Tamara Karsavina as the Firebird, was an immediate success and made Stravinsky famous overnight. Three years later he would shock the same city with *The Rite of Spring*.
- The story comes from Russian fairy tales. Prince Ivan strays into the magic garden of the immortal sorcerer Kashchei, catches the Firebird and lets her go in return for one of her feathers. He falls in love with a captive princess. When Kashchei's monsters seize him, he waves the feather; the Firebird makes them dance until they drop, sings them to sleep, and shows Ivan the egg that holds Kashchei's soul. Ivan breaks it, the spell is lifted, and everyone celebrates.
- The full ballet needs a huge orchestra and lasts about 45 minutes. In 1919, living in Switzerland, Stravinsky made a shorter concert suite for a smaller orchestra. It is the version heard most often today.
- In one line: a dark enchanted garden (I), the Firebird's flight (II), the princesses' round dance (III), the monsters' frenzy (IV), a lullaby (V) and a sunrise of celebration (VI).

### I. Introduction

**Summary.** Night in Kashchei's enchanted garden, where his enemies stand turned to stone. Low strings mutter a slow, dark phrase; above them the violins slide in eerie, glassy sounds. Strange colours flicker in the woodwinds. It is music of mystery rather than melody, and it begins so quietly that it is almost not there.

**Main ideas**

- The magic phrase: a few slow notes creeping up and down in the cellos and basses, the musical sign of the supernatural.
- Glassy string slides: violins playing [[harmonics]] while sliding along the string, one of the effects with which Stravinsky said he tried to outdo his teacher Rimsky-Korsakov.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the opening. Richard Strauss told Stravinsky he should have begun with a crash to astonish the audience; Stravinsky chose almost silence instead.

**In this recording.** Robert Shaw, best known as a choral conductor, was music director of the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra when he made this recording in June 1978. It was one of the first orchestral recordings made digitally, for the Cleveland label Telarc. On the Spotify album the suite is tracks 1–6.

### II. The Firebird and its Dance – Variation of the Firebird

**Summary.** The Firebird darts into the garden, a creature of light and speed. Brilliant, fluttering figures shoot through the woodwinds and strings, never settling. This is a dance without a tune: all wings, flashes and glitter.

**Main ideas**

- The Firebird's flight: fast, fluttering figures in flutes, clarinets and violins, with sudden accents and trills.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Rapid, glittering flurries in the woodwinds and strings | The Firebird appears |
| ≈ 0:20 | Fast, flickering figures leaping from instrument to instrument | Her dance, the Variation |
| ≈ 1:10 | The flurries grow more brilliant, with sharp accents | The wings beat faster |
| Last 15 seconds | A last shimmer, and the music vanishes | The Firebird is caught |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to follow a single line here. It is almost impossible: Stravinsky makes the orchestra sound like feathers catching the light.

### III. The Princesses' Khorovod

**Summary.** Thirteen princesses held captive by Kashchei come out into the garden and dance a khorovod, a slow Russian round dance. Prince Ivan has fallen in love with one of them. The melody, gentle and a little melancholy, is a Russian folk song that Rimsky-Korsakov, Stravinsky's teacher, had also used.

**Main ideas**

- The round-dance melody: a calm, folk-like tune, first on the oboe, later in the strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | Flutes and a harp play softly, then an oboe begins a gentle, folk-like tune | The princesses' round dance |
| ≈ 1:00 | Other woodwinds answer the oboe; the tune is passed around | The dance circles on |

**Things to notice**

1. This is the only movement built on a real tune you could sing. Notice how calm it is next to the magic music around it.

### IV. Infernal Dance of King Kashchei

**Summary.** Kashchei's monsters have seized Ivan. He waves the Firebird's feather, and the Firebird casts a spell: the whole court must dance, faster and faster, until they collapse. After a sudden, violent crash, the music hammers out jagged, off-beat rhythms; a long, swaying melody in the strings sweeps through the middle; brass slide and roar towards the end.

**Main ideas**

- The jagged dance: short, stabbing phrases in the low brass and bassoons, with accents in the wrong places.
- The swaying melody: a broad, lyrical line in the strings, carried along by the frantic rhythm.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the accents. They keep landing off the beat, so the dance seems to stumble forward, never comfortable.

### V. Berceuse

**Summary.** The Firebird sings the exhausted monsters to sleep. Over a gently rocking harp and muted strings, a bassoon plays a long, sad, lulling melody. Then the music sinks into a hushed shimmer of strings that leads, without a break, into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The lullaby: a slow, drooping melody on the bassoon, later taken up by the oboe and strings.
- The rocking accompaniment: harp and violas swaying back and forth.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A harp rocks gently; a bassoon plays a slow, sad melody | The Firebird's lullaby |
| ≈ 1:00 | An oboe takes the melody, high and tender | The monsters fall asleep |

| ≈ 2:30 | The bassoon returns with the opening melody | The lullaby again |
| Last 40 seconds | A very quiet, trembling shimmer in the strings, almost still | Darkness; the music runs straight on into the finale |

**Things to notice**

1. The bassoon usually plays low, comic or gruff parts. Here Stravinsky gives it a lullaby, and the effect is strange and beautiful.

### VI. Finale

**Summary.** Ivan has broken the egg; Kashchei is dead, the spell is lifted, the stone figures come back to life. A solo horn plays a simple, folk-like melody very softly. The melody passes through the orchestra, grows louder and louder, and finally blazes out in the brass in a broad, uneven rhythm, ending in a long, radiant chord.

**Main ideas**

- The celebration tune: a short, simple melody, first on the horn, then built up through the whole orchestra.
- The final transformation: the same tune in long, uneven beats in the brass, over ringing chords.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A solo horn plays a simple, gentle melody over quiet, shimmering strings | Dawn: the spell is broken |
| ≈ 0:30 | The tune passes to the strings and woodwinds, each time a little fuller | Kashchei's prisoners come back to life |
| ≈ 1:15 | The whole orchestra plays the tune, loud and bright | The celebration begins |
| ≈ 2:00 | The brass hammer the tune in long, uneven beats | The final hymn |
| Last 20 seconds | A last brilliant, sustained chord | Triumph |

**Things to notice**

1. It is the same short tune from the horn's first notes to the last bars. Only the volume, the instruments and the rhythm change.

### Threads

- **Two worlds in sound.** Stravinsky gives the human characters simple, folk-like melodies, and the magic world strange, sliding, chromatic sounds. You can hear which world you are in.
- **The pupil's farewell.** The rich, colourful orchestra comes from his teacher Rimsky-Korsakov, but the jagged rhythms of the Infernal Dance already point to *The Rite of Spring*.
- **From darkness to light.** The suite begins with almost nothing in the low strings and ends with the whole orchestra blazing, in the same journey as the story.
