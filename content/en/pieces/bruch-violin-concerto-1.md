### The big picture

- Bruch finished the first version in 1866, while he was music director in Koblenz. It was first played on 24 April 1866 by the violinist Otto von Königslow, with Bruch conducting.
- He then revised it thoroughly with the help of the great violinist Joseph Joachim. Joachim gave the first performance of the final version in Bremen on 7 January 1868.
- The first movement is not a full sonata movement but a *Vorspiel*, a prelude, that leads straight into the slow movement. The Adagio is the heart of the concerto.
- Bruch sold the score outright to his publisher, Simrock, for a small lump sum, so its huge success earned him little. He grew tired of violinists who played only this concerto and ignored his others. Left poor by the First World War, he sent the manuscript to America to be sold; he died in October 1920 without receiving any money for it.
- In one line: a dramatic prelude with free solo passages (I), a long, glowing song (II), a fiery dance in double stops (III).

### I. Vorspiel: Allegro moderato

**Summary.** A prelude, not a conventional first movement. The woodwinds ask a quiet question and the violin answers alone, freely, like a singer improvising. Then come a passionate main theme and a gentler second one, and at the end the opening question and answer return. Instead of finishing, the movement flows straight into the Adagio.

**Main ideas**

- The question: a soft drum roll, then a short, solemn phrase in the woodwinds and horns.
- The answer: the solo violin alone, rising freely from its lowest note, like a short [[cadenza]].
- The main theme: a passionate, surging melody for the violin, in G minor.
- The second theme: slower and more tender.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A very soft drum roll; woodwinds and horns play a short, solemn phrase | The question, *pianissimo* |
| ≈ 0:20 | The violin alone answers, climbing from its lowest note to a long high note | The first free solo passage |
| ≈ 0:45 | The question and the answer again, the answer now wider | The introduction repeated |

| ≈ 6:30 | The woodwind question and the violin's answer return, now more brilliant | The opening frame comes back |

**Things to notice**

1. The movement ends where it began, with the woodwind question and the violin's free answer. Compare the two versions.
2. Listen to the last seconds: the movement never really stops. A single held note carries you into the Adagio.

**In this recording.** Mutter recorded the concerto with Karajan and the Berliner Philharmoniker in September 1980, when she was seventeen. On the Spotify album the Bruch is tracks 4–6, after Mendelssohn's concerto. Because the first movement runs straight into the Adagio, the break between tracks 4 and 5 falls in continuous music.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** The heart of the concerto: a slow, warm song in E-flat major. The violin sings almost without a break, while the orchestra keeps moving underneath, so the music always seems to flow. Two melodies alternate and grow, rising to a passionate climax before the movement fades in peace.

**Main ideas**

- The first melody: a long, expressive line that rises slowly and keeps unfolding.
- The second melody: broader and darker, a contrast to the first.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |

| ≈ 1:30 | The melody climbs and swells; the woodwinds answer | The first melody unfolds |
| ≈ 2:45 | A broader, darker melody on the violin | The second melody |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen under the violin. The orchestra hardly ever stops moving, even when the melody is at its slowest.

### III. Finale: Allegro energico

**Summary.** A fiery dance. After a quiet, tense build-up in the orchestra, the violin bursts in with a theme played in [[double stop]]s, two notes at once. A broad, singing second melody keeps cutting in, but the dance wins and the concerto ends in a rush.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a vigorous, leaping theme in G major, played in double stops.
- The broad melody: a slower, warm, singing tune.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Quiet, tense chords in the violas, then the violins; the dance is hinted at and grows | The introduction, *pianissimo* |
| ≈ 0:20 | The solo violin bursts in loudly with a leaping dance, playing two notes at a time | The main theme, in double stops |
| ≈ 0:50 | The whole orchestra takes up the dance | A [[tutti]] |
| ≈ 1:30 | The orchestra plays a broad, warm melody; then the violin takes it | The second theme |
| ≈ 2:30–4:00 | The dance returns and is worked through new keys | The development |
| ≈ 4:00 | The leaping theme in double stops again | The main theme returns |
| ≈ 5:00 | The broad melody once more, now in G major | The second theme returns |
| Last 70 seconds | Faster and faster, climbing higher; two short, grand chords | *Presto*: the [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the violin's first entry. You hear two notes at once, which is what makes the theme sound so full and rough.

### Threads

- **A prelude and a song.** The first movement exists to lead into the Adagio; the two belong together and run without a break.
- **The violin as singer.** In his day Bruch was known above all for choral music, and the solo part is written like a long vocal line.
- **Free and strict.** The free, improvised-sounding solo passages of the first movement set off the regular rhythm of the dance at the end.
