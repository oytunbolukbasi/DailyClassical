### The big picture

- Schubert worked on it in the summer of 1825, travelling through Upper Austria and the Alps, and had it fully scored by 1826. For a long time it was wrongly dated to 1828, his last year.
- In October 1826 he sent it to Vienna's Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde. Its amateur orchestra tried it through in 1827 and set it aside as too long and difficult. It was never properly performed in his lifetime.
- In 1838, ten years after his death, Robert Schumann was shown the manuscript in Vienna by Schubert's brother Ferdinand. Mendelssohn conducted the first full performance in Leipzig on 21 March 1839, and Schumann praised its "heavenly length".
- "The Great" first told it apart from Schubert's shorter Symphony No. 6, also in C major. It is the same work sometimes numbered 7 or 8.
- In one line: a horn call that grows into a journey (I), a march that breaks down (II), a huge dance (III), a whirlwind (IV).

### I. Andante – Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** A slow introduction that begins with two horns alone, then a fast movement in [[sonata form]] driven by short, dotted rhythms. The horns' opening tune comes back at the very end, played by the whole orchestra.

**Main ideas**

- The horn tune: a calm, eight-bar melody for two horns in [[unison]], with no accompaniment.
- Theme 1: a sharp, dotted figure in the strings, answered by the woodwinds in triplets.
- Theme 2: a quiet, skipping tune for oboes and bassoons, in E minor instead of the expected G major.

### II. Andante con moto

**Summary.** A march in A minor with a lyrical second theme, each heard twice. The second time, the march builds to a crisis: loud, clashing chords, then complete silence.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a march tune for the oboe, with a jaunty dotted rhythm, over walking strings.
- Theme 2: a warm, consoling melody in the strings, in F major.

### IV. Finale. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** A long, relentless finale in [[sonata form]], built from galloping triplets and a theme of four repeated notes. Near its middle, a quiet tune close to Beethoven's "Ode to Joy" slips past.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a fanfare-like call for the full orchestra, with galloping triplets in the strings.
- Theme 2: four repeated notes in the horns, then a dancing tune in the woodwinds over busy strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the four repeated notes from ≈ 1:45 to the end. They begin as a soft horn signal and finish as the climax of the whole symphony.
2. The story goes that in London in 1844 the violinists laughed at the second theme (≈ 1:45) in rehearsal, and the orchestra refused to play the symphony.

### Threads

- **Trombones as singers.** Schubert gives the trombones melodies, quiet ones as well as loud.
- **Rhythm as engine.** Dotted figures, triplets and repeated notes drive all four movements.
- **Beethoven's shadow.** Schubert worked on it soon after Beethoven's Ninth (1824), and the finale quotes it, briefly and almost in passing.
