### The big picture

- Schumann wrote the symphony in 1841 in Leipzig, the year after he married the pianist Clara Wieck, straight after his *Spring* Symphony. He is said to have given the score to Clara on her birthday, 13 September 1841. It was first played at the Leipzig Gewandhaus on 6 December 1841, conducted by the orchestra's leader, Ferdinand David, but it made little impression and Schumann put it aside.
- Ten years later, in 1851, as music director in Düsseldorf, he revised it thoroughly: he made the orchestra fuller, tightened the links between the movements and changed the tempo marks from Italian to German. He conducted the new version in Düsseldorf, and it was published in 1853. Because two more symphonies had appeared in between, it became his No. 4.
- The four movements are played without a break, and they share their themes: a melody from the slow introduction returns in the slow movement, a violin solo from the slow movement becomes the trio of the scherzo, and the finale grows out of the first movement's main theme. Schumann had thought of calling the work a "symphonic fantasy".
- Johannes Brahms preferred the lighter original of 1841 and had it published in 1891, against Clara's objections. Most orchestras play the 1851 version, as in this recording.
- In one line: darkness and a restless struggle (I), a sad song with a violin's garland (II), a stamping dance and a gentle trio (III), and a slow awakening into a bright, fast finale in D major (IV).

### I. Ziemlich langsam – Lebhaft

**Summary.** A slow, dark introduction ("rather slow") gathers speed and breaks into a fast movement ("lively") built almost entirely on one short, restless figure. Instead of returning to its opening as a classical [[sonata form]] would, the movement presses on, finds new themes in the middle and ends in D major. It leads straight into the second movement.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction theme: a long, winding melody in the violins, rising and falling in small steps, over a held bass note.
- The main figure of the *Lebhaft*: a quick, circling run of notes in the violins, repeated again and again.
- Two new themes in the middle: a loud, stabbing call with heavy chords, and a broad, singing tune.

### II. Romanze: Ziemlich langsam

**Summary.** A short, sad song. An oboe and a solo cello sing it together, an unusual pairing. In the middle the winding melody of the first movement's introduction returns, and a solo violin spins a delicate garland of fast notes around it.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a simple, melancholy tune for oboe and solo cello together, over plucked strings.
- The garland: a solo violin's ornament of fast, light notes, in D major.

### III. Scherzo: Lebhaft

**Summary.** A heavy, stamping [[scherzo]] in D minor, with strong accents on every beat. The [[trio]] in the middle is its opposite: soft and flowing, and made from the violin garland of the slow movement. The trio comes twice, and the second time it fades away into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: a loud, determined tune for the whole orchestra, rising in strides.
- The trio: a quiet, gliding melody in B-flat major, the garland from the Romanze.

### IV. Langsam – Lebhaft

**Summary.** A slow, mysterious introduction in which the main theme of the first movement is heard again, slowed down, as if waking up. Brass chords grow louder, the tempo picks up, and the finale bursts out in D major with the same theme, now fast and bright. The symphony ends faster and faster.

**Main ideas**

- The awakening: soft, shivering strings and a slow version of the first movement's racing figure; brass chords answer it.
- The finale theme: the first movement's figure turned into a bright, energetic tune in D major, with a jumping, dotted rhythm.
- A second theme: lighter and more dancing.

**In this recording.** Wolfgang Sawallisch recorded all four Schumann symphonies with the Staatskapelle Dresden, one of the oldest orchestras in the world, in the warm acoustic of Dresden's Lukaskirche in 1972. EMI later reissued the set among its "Great Recordings of the Century".

### Threads

- **One symphony, one stream.** The four movements follow each other without a pause, and the themes keep returning in new forms. Building a whole symphony from a few shared ideas in this way is called [[cyclic form]]; for its time the symphony was unusually tightly knit.
- **Two versions.** The light, transparent score of 1841 and the fuller, more solid one of 1851 are both played today. Clara Schumann preferred the revision; Brahms preferred the original.
- **Clara.** The symphony belongs to Schumann's first year of marriage, the year he turned from piano music and songs to the orchestra.
