### The big picture

- Aranjuez, south of Madrid, is a royal palace among gardens, fountains and avenues of trees by the river Tagus, the spring residence of the Spanish kings. Rodrigo said the concerto should capture "the fragrance of magnolias, the singing of birds, and the gushing of fountains" there.
- Rodrigo had been almost completely blind since the age of three. He was a pianist and did not play the guitar. He wrote the concerto in Paris in early 1939, at the end of the Spanish Civil War, and dedicated it to the guitarist Regino Sainz de la Maza, who gave the premiere in Barcelona on 9 November 1940.
- The guitar is a quiet instrument, so the orchestra is small: pairs of woodwind, horns and trumpets, and strings, with no trombones and no percussion. Rodrigo often thins the orchestra to a few instruments so the guitar can always be heard.
- The slow movement became one of the best-known melodies of the 20th century; Miles Davis and Gil Evans recorded it on *Sketches of Spain* (1960). For years the Rodrigos said nothing about its inspiration. Late in life his wife, the Istanbul-born pianist Victoria Kamhi, wrote that it recalled the happy days of their honeymoon and Rodrigo's grief when she lost their first pregnancy.
- In one line: a strummed Spanish dance that never stops (I), a long, sad song for cor anglais and guitar (II), a light courtly dance in shifting beats (III).

### I. Allegro con spirito

**Summary.** The guitar starts alone, strumming a dance rhythm that runs, in Rodrigo's words, at a "relentless pace" through the whole movement. Two themes come and go over it, shared between the guitar and the orchestra. The movement ends as quietly as it began.

**Main ideas**

- The rhythm: strummed chords in six quick beats, heard now as two groups of three, now as three groups of two. It is the [[hemiola]] of Spanish dances such as the fandango.
- Theme 1: a bright, short tune for oboe and violins.
- Theme 2: a tune for the guitar that suddenly slips up a step, from D major into E-flat.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Clap along with the opening strums. The beat keeps changing its grouping, from two big beats to three, but the speed never changes.
2. Notice how often the orchestra simply stops when the guitar plays. Rodrigo clears the space so a quiet instrument can be heard.

**In this recording.** Narciso Yepes made his Madrid debut with this concerto on 16 December 1947, under Ataúlfo Argenta. The success of that concert, and his tours and early recordings with Argenta, did much to make the work famous. He recorded it again in April 1979 with the Philharmonia Orchestra and Luis Antonio García Navarro at Watford Town Hall. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–3, followed by *Fantasía para un gentilhombre*.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** Over soft guitar chords, the cor anglais sings a slow, sad melody in B minor; the guitar answers with the same tune, decorated. In Rodrigo's description the movement is a dialogue between the guitar and solo wind instruments. Tension builds slowly to a long guitar [[cadenza]], after which the whole orchestra plays the melody at full strength. It ends softly, turning to the major.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: long and slow, built from small steps, with a lingering, Spanish turn; first on the cor anglais.
- The guitar's decorations: quick ornaments, runs and [[trill]]s wound around the tune.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft guitar chords; at once a cor anglais begins a slow, sad melody | The main melody, in B minor. The cor anglais leads, the guitar accompanies |
| ≈ 1:00 | The guitar takes up the same melody, adding quick decorations; then the cor anglais and guitar share the second half | The guitar's version of the tune |
| ≈ 2:15–3:30 | Short phrases pass between the guitar and single wind instruments: oboe, bassoon, horn | The dialogue Rodrigo described |
| ≈ 3:30–5:00 | A trill in the guitar unsettles the music; the strings swell, then fall back | The first build-up of tension |
| ≈ 5:00–8:30 | The guitar alone: first the whole melody, then a long solo of runs, trills and strummed chords that grows ever louder | The great [[cadenza]] |
| ≈ 8:30–9:30 | The full orchestra plays the melody with great force, strings and wind together | The climax: the melody *molto appassionato* |
| Last minute | Calm returns; the guitar plays soft, rising [[arpeggio]]s; the strings close in the major | A brief, hushed ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the cor anglais at the start with the orchestra at the climax. It is the same melody, first almost spoken, then cried out.
2. In the cadenza, listen for the moment the guitar changes from plucked runs to strummed chords. That is where the climax begins to gather.

### III. Allegro gentile

**Summary.** A light, graceful dance. Rodrigo said it "recalls a courtly dance" in which bars of two and three beats alternate. The guitar's opening tune is the basis of the whole movement, passed back and forth with the orchestra and varied each time. It ends suddenly and softly.

**Main ideas**

- The dance tune: short, neat, a little old-fashioned, first in the guitar, which starts it in the "wrong" key of B major.
- The beat: one bar of three, then three bars of two, so each phrase has nine beats; later the pattern gets more irregular.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The guitar alone plays a short, neat dance tune | The main theme, starting in B major |
| ≈ 0:10 | The orchestra repeats the tune, brighter, in the home key | The orchestra's answer, in D major |
| ≈ 0:20–1:30 | Guitar and orchestra pass the tune back and forth; the steps of the dance trip and stumble | The beat alternates between groups of two and three |
| ≈ 1:30–2:30 | New, more wistful phrases in the guitar and woodwind, then louder chords from the whole orchestra | A contrasting episode |
| ≈ 2:30 | The dance tune again, in the guitar | The theme returns |
| ≈ 3:00–4:30 | Further variations, with fuller orchestral passages; the metre becomes more irregular | The tune is varied again |
| Last 15 seconds | The music thins out; a few dry, soft notes | A sudden, quiet end |

**Things to notice**

1. Try counting the beats of the opening tune: three, then two, two, two. The tune limps on purpose, like a dancer making a courtly bow.
2. The whole concerto ends softly, not with a loud flourish. Notice how short the final gesture is.

### Threads

- **A guitar garden.** Rodrigo wanted to evoke the gardens of Aranjuez: the fountains, the birds, the scent of magnolias. The painting is a view of the same gardens.
- **Rhythms of two and three.** Both fast movements keep switching between groups of two and three beats, an old habit of Spanish dance.
- **Room for a quiet soloist.** A small orchestra without trombones or percussion, and long passages in which only a few instruments play: the guitar is never drowned.
