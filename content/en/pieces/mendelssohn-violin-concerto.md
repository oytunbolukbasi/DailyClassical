### The big picture

- Mendelssohn wrote it for Ferdinand David, the leader of his orchestra in Leipzig, the Gewandhaus. They had been born a year apart in the same house in Hamburg. In July 1838 Mendelssohn wrote to him that a concerto in E minor was running through his head and that its beginning gave him no peace.
- It took six years. The score is dated 16 September 1844, and all along Mendelssohn kept asking David how to make the solo part work on the violin.
- David played the premiere in Leipzig on 13 March 1845. Mendelssohn was ill, so the Danish composer Niels Gade conducted.
- It broke three habits. The violin enters almost at once, with no long orchestral introduction. The [[cadenza]] is written out by the composer and comes in the middle of the first movement, not near the end. And the three movements are joined without pauses, [[attacca]], so nobody applauds between them.
- In one line: a passionate, urgent song (I), a calm song without words (II), a light, sparkling dance after a fanfare (III).

### I. Allegro molto appassionato

**Summary.** After a bar and a half of soft, nervous strings, the violin starts singing a passionate melody high up. It is a movement in [[sonata form]], urgent and restless, with one moment of complete calm: a quiet hymn in the woodwinds over a single low note from the violin.

**Main ideas**

- The opening melody: long, yearning and high, in E minor, on the solo violin over murmuring strings.
- The hymn: a quiet, slow tune in G major for clarinets and flutes, while the violin holds its lowest note underneath.
- The bouncing arpeggios: quick chords bounced across all four strings of the violin, the sound that opens the recapitulation.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the low note under the woodwind hymn (≈ 3:15). The violin plays just one note for a long time, while others have the tune.
2. In the cadenza, wait for the bouncing chords. They do not stop when the orchestra comes back. The soloist is suddenly the accompanist.

**In this recording.** Mutter was 17. Karajan and the Berlin Philharmonic take broad tempos, so the first movement lasts almost fourteen minutes. Heifetz, in the alternative recording, plays it in eleven.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow movement that sounds like one of Mendelssohn's *Songs without Words*. The violin sings a long, simple melody over a gentle, rocking accompaniment. In the middle the music darkens and grows anxious, then the melody comes back.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a long, calm melody in C major, sung by the violin in its middle register.
- The middle section: a darker, troubled passage in the minor, with trembling strings.

### III. Allegretto non troppo – Allegro molto vivace

**Summary.** A short, wistful bridge, then a fanfare, and the finale takes off: quick, light and sparkling, like Mendelssohn's fairy music for *A Midsummer Night's Dream*. The violin skips and flickers, and the orchestra answers with a bold march.

**Main ideas**

- The bridge: a few bars of quiet, wistful music for the violin and strings, still in the minor.
- The fanfare: trumpets, horns and timpani announce the finale.
- The main theme: quick, light, staccato notes on the violin, in a bright E major.
- The march: a bold, strong tune for the orchestra.

### Threads

- **No pauses.** A held bassoon note links the first two movements, and a short bridge links the second to the third. The whole concerto is meant to be heard in one sweep.
- **The soloist from the start.** The violin enters in the second bar and hardly ever stops. There is no long wait while the orchestra plays the themes first.
- **A friend's advice.** Mendelssohn was a pianist and conductor; Ferdinand David, a violinist, helped him shape the solo part over six years.
- **Lightness.** Even the passionate first movement moves quickly, and the finale flickers like his fairy music. Little in it is heavy.
