### The big picture

- Schumann wrote the quintet in a few weeks in September and October 1842, in what is called his "year of chamber music". Until then he had written almost nothing but piano music and songs; that year brought three string quartets, this quintet and a piano quartet.
- He dedicated it to his wife Clara, the great pianist. She was to play it at its first private performance in December 1842, but fell ill, and Felix Mendelssohn sight-read the difficult piano part instead. His suggestions led Schumann to revise the inner movements, adding a second [[trio]] to the scherzo.
- Clara played it at the first public performance, at the Leipzig Gewandhaus on 8 January 1843, and called it "splendid, full of vigour and freshness". She performed it often for the rest of her life.
- Its success made piano and string quartet a favourite combination: Brahms, Dvořák and many others followed. The piano sometimes converses with the strings and sometimes faces them like a soloist with an orchestra.
- In one line: an exuberant opening (I), a funeral march with a storm inside (II), a scherzo of rushing scales (III), a finale that ends by combining its own theme with the first movement's (IV).

### I. Allegro brillante

**Summary.** All five players launch a bold theme of huge upward leaps. Then the music melts into one of Schumann's most tender melodies, a duet for cello and viola. The movement is in [[sonata form]]; in its middle the piano races in quick figures while the strings hammer out the opening theme.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, all five: a confident tune in long notes that leaps upwards in wide steps.
- Theme 2, cello and viola: a soft, yearning melody, passed from one to the other.

### II. In modo d'una marcia. Un poco largamente

**Summary.** A funeral march in C minor, quiet and halting, with a melody in the first violin over the piano's dragging steps. It alternates with a warm, singing song and, at the centre, a wild, agitated storm in the piano. The march keeps coming back, each time a little changed. Ingmar Bergman used it as the main theme of his film *Fanny and Alexander* (1982).

**Main ideas**

- The march: a sombre, halting tune in the first violin, over hushed, limping chords in the piano.
- The song: a long, sweet melody for violin and cello in C major.
- The storm: fast, surging piano figures under urgent string chords; it is the march theme in disguise.

**Things to notice**

2. Compare the march before and after the storm. The second time, the piano no longer drags but ripples underneath, and the violin and cello add lines of their own.

### III. Scherzo: Molto vivace

**Summary.** The [[scherzo]] is built almost entirely on scales, rushing up and down. It has two trios: the first a gentle [[canon]] between violin and viola, the second, added after Mendelssohn's advice, a fierce, unbroken stream of quick notes.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: loud, bounding scales in a skipping rhythm, up and down the instruments.
- Trio I: a soft, lyrical tune that the violin and viola play one after the other.
- Trio II: a racing, heavily accented line in a different, two-beat metre.

**Listening stops**

### IV. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** The finale starts in the wrong key, in G minor, with a stamping, minor-key theme, and stays away from the home key for a long time. Only near the middle does E-flat major arrive. Then, in the closing minutes, Schumann combines the finale's theme with the first movement's opening theme in a grand double [[fugato]].

**Main ideas**

- Theme A: a stamping, determined tune in the minor.
- Theme B: a brighter, bouncing idea in the major.
- Theme C: a calm, lyrical melody in a distant key.
- The return: the first movement's opening theme, in long notes, combined with Theme A.

**Listening stops**

1. Near the end, listen for the first movement's leaping theme in long notes. When you recognise it, the whole quintet comes full circle.

### Threads

- **Piano and quartet.** Schumann alternates conversation among five equals with passages where the piano faces the four strings like a soloist facing an orchestra.
- **Coming full circle.** The finale brings back the first movement's theme, an example of [[cyclic form]].
- **Clara.** The quintet was written for and dedicated to Clara Schumann, and she kept it in her concerts for decades.
