### The big picture

- Ulvi Cemal Erkin (1906–1972) was one of the "Turkish Five", the first generation of composers of the Turkish Republic. All five trained in Europe and set out to join its forms with the melodies and rhythms of Turkish music. He wrote the Violin Concerto in 1946–47, after his dance rhapsody *Köçekçe* and his First Symphony.
- A premiere planned for a festival in Vienna in 1947 did not take place. The concerto was first heard on 2 April 1948, on the opening night of Ankara's new opera house, with the Presidential Symphony Orchestra and the violinist Licco Amar. Born in Budapest, Amar had led Paul Hindemith's string quartet in Germany and had taught in Ankara since leaving Nazi Germany.
- The plan is the classical one: a first movement in [[sonata form]] with a [[cadenza]], a slow movement in three parts and a [[rondo]] to finish. The Turkish colour is in the melodies and rhythms. The first theme leans on a Turkish [[makam]], the second sounds more Anatolian still, and the finale is a horon, the fast dance of the Black Sea coast, with a passage in the spirit of a [[taksim]].
- It is a soloist's concerto, with long lines to sing and passages that test the player's technique. The slow movement stands apart: its lines interweave rather than sing over an accompaniment, and one analyst hears the Baroque in it.
- In one line: a serious, broad first movement with a cadenza at its heart (I), a song over a slow, plucked tread (II), a fast Black Sea dance (III).

### I. Allegro giusto

**Summary.** The longest movement, about half the concerto. After a roll on the timpani, violin and orchestra set out a serious, broad theme; a second theme with a more Turkish turn follows. In the middle the violin plays a long cadenza alone, and the movement ends with a weighty, decisive [[coda]]. Its form is close to a textbook [[sonata form]].

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, earnest: a broad melody for violin and orchestra that climbs on rising scales. Its repeated, step-by-step phrases lean on the Kürdi [[makam]].
- Theme 2, Anatolian: a contrasting tune whose melody and rhythm are closer to Turkish folk music, shared between the violin and the woodwind.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for where the cadenza falls. In most concertos it comes near the end; here it sits in the middle of the movement, and the orchestra still has a long way to go after it.
2. Compare the two themes. The first could almost come from a European concerto of the 1930s; the second sounds closer to Anatolia.

On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 2–4, between *Köçekçe* and Erkin's Second Symphony.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A slow movement in three parts: a long melody, a middle section that climbs in steps, and the melody again. Under the strings' steady, thoughtful theme the basses pluck a slow, even tread that hardly stops. The violin does not simply sing over an accompaniment: its line is woven in with the others, and Metin Munzur of the Ankara State Conservatory hears something of the Baroque in it.

**Main ideas**

- The tread: plucked notes in the basses, walking at an even pace ([[pizzicato]]).
- The theme: a slow, grave melody in the strings, later taken up by the violin, with a melancholy that the woodwind deepen.

1. Follow the plucked basses. They keep the slow pulse almost from the first bar to the last.
2. Notice how often the violin is one voice among several, rather than the singer with an accompaniment.

### III. Allegro con fuoco

**Summary.** "Fast, with fire." The finale is a horon, the quick, driving dance of the Black Sea coast, set as a [[rondo]]: the dance tune keeps coming back between contrasting episodes. It is the most openly Turkish of the three movements. The violin plays crisp [[double stop]]s against a pounding orchestra, and before the end a free, unmeasured passage recalls the [[taksim]] of Turkish violinists.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a fast, insistent dance tune, with short, repeated figures.
- The episodes: contrasting tunes between the returns of the refrain, some built from short phrases repeated at new pitches.

**Things to notice**

1. Count how many times the dance tune comes back. Each return is a little landmark in the rondo.
2. In the free violin passage near the end, the pulse disappears. In Turkish music a taksim is an improvisation played before or within a piece; here it is written out, but it is meant to sound improvised.

### Threads

- **Two traditions.** The form of every movement is European: sonata form, a three-part slow movement, a rondo. The colour comes from Turkish music: a makam in the first theme, an Anatolian second theme, a horon and a taksim in the finale.
- **The violin's long road.** The concerto gives the soloist room to sing and to dazzle. Licco Amar played it first, in Ankara in 1948; ten years later the Turkish violinist Ayla Erduran played it in Brussels, with Erkin conducting.
- **Ankara.** Erkin taught in Ankara for more than forty years, and the concerto was heard first on the night the city's opera house opened.
