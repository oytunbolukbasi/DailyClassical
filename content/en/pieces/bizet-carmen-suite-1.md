### The big picture

- *Carmen*, Bizet's last opera, opened at the Opéra-Comique in Paris on 3 March 1875. It is set in Seville around 1820: Carmen, a Roma woman who works in the cigarette factory, seduces the soldier Don José, leaves him for the bullfighter Escamillo, and is stabbed by José outside the bullring.
- The audience was shocked by the story and the reviews were mostly hostile. Bizet died three months later, on 3 June 1875, aged 36, and never knew that within ten years *Carmen* would be played all over the world.
- The suite is not by Bizet. After his death his friend Ernest Guiraud assembled concert suites from the opera for its publisher, Choudens; a later German edition by Fritz Hoffmann, with almost the same pieces, is the one usually played. Suite No. 1 takes mostly the orchestral preludes and [[entr'acte]]s, the music the orchestra plays while the curtain is down.
- Bizet never went to Spain. He took Spanish dance rhythms and colours from books of songs and from the Spanish fashion in Paris, and made from them music that sounds Spanish to the whole world.
- In one line: fate and a dance (I), a night in the mountains (II), Carmen's tease (III), a soldier's song (IV), the bullfighters' parade (V).

### I. Prélude – Aragonaise

**Summary.** Two pieces in one track. The Prélude is the dark second half of the opera's overture: the theme of Carmen and her fate, heard over trembling strings, which breaks off on an unresolved chord. Straight after it comes the Aragonaise, the prelude to the last act, a dance from Aragon with a guitar-like strumming and an oboe tune, played while the crowd gathers outside the bullring.

**Main ideas**

- The fate theme: a slow, winding tune with a sinister bend in it, played low by cellos, clarinet, bassoon and cornet over a string [[tremolo]].
- The Aragonaise: hard, stamping chords, then a lilting oboe melody over plucked strings, harp and tambourine.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment the Prélude stops. It does not end: it breaks off on a chord that needs an answer, the same tension that hangs over the whole opera.

**In this recording.** Charles Dutoit and the Montreal Symphony Orchestra recorded both *Carmen* suites and both *L'Arlésienne* suites in the church of Saint-Eustache, near Montreal, and Decca released them on one disc in 1988. On the Spotify album Suite No. 1 is tracks 9–13; Dutoit's disc puts the Prélude and the Aragonaise together on one track.

### II. Intermezzo

**Summary.** The prelude to Act III, where the smugglers, with José among them, are camped at night in the mountains. A flute sings a calm, long melody over a harp, and the other woodwinds join one by one. It is the gentlest music in the opera, a moment of peace before the story turns dark.

**Main ideas**

- The flute melody: long, calm phrases over rippling harp chords, later taken up by the clarinet and other woodwinds.

### III. Séguedille

**Summary.** In Act I Carmen, arrested after a fight in the cigarette factory, has her hands tied and is left in José's charge. She sings him a seguidilla, an Andalusian dance-song, about the tavern of her friend Lillas Pastia, where she will dance tonight with her lover, whoever he may be. José gives in and lets her escape. In the suite her voice is given to the instruments.

**Main ideas**

- The seguidilla tune: light and teasing, in a quick three, with little hesitations as if Carmen were smiling over her shoulder.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Plucked strings set a light, quick three-beat rhythm; a woodwind plays a teasing, swaying tune | Carmen's song, without words |
| ≈ 0:40 | The tune moves to the strings and grows fuller | Carmen presses her case |
| ≈ 1:20 | The opening tune again, then a quickening | The seduction succeeds |
| Last 15 seconds | A quick, bright flourish and two short chords | Carmen gets her way |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the tune keeps pausing and starting again. In the opera, these are the moments where Carmen watches to see whether José is listening.

### IV. Les dragons d'Alcala

**Summary.** The prelude to Act II. José has gone to prison for letting Carmen escape, and this little march is the song he sings on his way to meet her again: "Halte-là! Qui va là? Dragon d'Alcala!", the challenge and reply of a soldier of the Alcalá dragoons. Two bassoons play it, side by side, light and a little comic.

**Main ideas**

- The soldier's song: a short, jaunty march in two bassoons, played crisply and quietly over plucked strings and a soft side drum.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

### V. Les toréadors

**Summary.** The first, brilliant part of the opera's overture, with its music for the bullfighters' procession in the last act. A loud, glittering march with cymbals and triangle frames a famous tune in the middle: the refrain of the toreador Escamillo's song.

**Main ideas**

- The march: fast and bright, with crashing cymbals and the whole orchestra in full voice.
- The toreador's refrain: a swaggering melody in the strings, first soft, then loud.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The whole orchestra bursts out with a fast, glittering march, cymbals and triangle ringing | The bullfighters' procession |
| ≈ 0:30 | A lighter, quieter strain, then the march again | A short contrast |

**Things to notice**

1. You probably know the middle tune. In the opera Escamillo sings it in the tavern in Act II, boasting about the bullring, the crowd and the dark eyes that watch the toreador as he fights.

### Threads

- **An opera without voices.** Most of this music is what the orchestra plays while the stage waits: preludes and entr'actes. Heard together, they still tell the story, from the fate theme to the bullring.
- **Fate and festivity.** The suite opens with the dark theme of fate and ends with the bright parade. In the opera the two are joined: the overture runs straight from the parade into the fate theme.
- **A Paris Spain.** Spanish rhythms, guitar-like plucking, tambourines and castanet-like patter, all imagined by a composer who never crossed the Pyrenees.
