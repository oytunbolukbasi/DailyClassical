### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it between the end of 1825 and the summer of 1826, aged fifty-five and deaf. It was the last of three quartets written one after another, Op. 132, Op. 130 and this one. According to his friend the violinist Karl Holz, Beethoven later called it the greatest of his quartets.
- It has seven movements, played without a break. When Holz asked whether the players could stop to tune, Beethoven allowed only a short pause after the fifth movement.
- On the copy he sent to his publisher, Schott, Beethoven wrote as a joke that the quartet was "put together from pilferings of this and that". When the worried publisher asked, he replied that it was in fact brand new.
- He dedicated it to Baron Joseph von Stutterheim, the officer who took his nephew Karl into the army after Karl's attempted suicide in the summer of 1826. Beethoven never heard it played: it was printed after his death in 1827. Holz later recalled that in November 1828, days before his own death, Franz Schubert asked to hear it.
- In one line: a slow fugue (I), a light dance (II), a short bridge (III), a theme with variations (IV), a quick scherzo (V), a brief lament (VI), a fierce finale (VII).

### I. Adagio ma non troppo e molto espressivo

**Summary.** The quartet begins with a slow, quiet [[fugue]]: one sad tune, played first by a single violin, then taken up by each instrument in turn and woven against itself. Richard Wagner called it the most melancholy music ever written. It stays soft almost to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The fugue tune: four long notes that rise, then fall, with a held, aching third note. The same four-note shape runs through Beethoven's other late quartets.
- The texture: four lines moving slowly over one another, as in old church music.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The first violin alone, very slow and quiet: a few long notes that rise and sink | The fugue tune, in C-sharp minor |
| ≈ 0:15–0:50 | The same tune enters in the second violin, then the viola, then the cello; the lines pile up | The four voices of the fugue enter one by one |
| ≈ 3:35 | After a long quiet stretch, the first loud passage, all four together | The fugue reaches a far-off key and swells for a moment |
| ≈ 6:30 | Very low in the cello, the tune in long, stretched notes | The fugue tune in slow motion, called augmentation |
| Near the end | Soft again; the music comes to rest on one bare note, C-sharp, played in octaves | The bare note leads into the next movement, a half step higher |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow one instrument, for example the cello, and listen to how long it waits before it plays the tune.
2. The last note is a bare C-sharp. The next movement begins one step up, on D, as if the music has simply lifted.

### II. Allegro molto vivace

**Summary.** A light, quick dance in D major, in a lilting six-beat rhythm, after the stillness of the fugue. It is built almost entirely from one folk-like tune, which keeps returning between sudden loud outbursts.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a simple, rocking melody in the first violin, very soft at first.
- The outbursts: short, loud chords that break in and are gone again.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Very soft and light: a rocking tune in the first violin, in a skipping rhythm | The dance tune, in D major |
| ≈ 0:16 | Sudden loud chords, then soft again | The first outburst |
| ≈ 1:05 | The tune comes back, passed between the instruments | The tune returns |
| ≈ 2:40 | Loud, then very loud; then a quick fade | The last outbursts and a soft ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the mood with the fugue. The key has moved only one note higher, but the music sounds like a different world.

### III. Allegro moderato – Adagio

**Summary.** Not much more than half a minute long: two loud chords, a few bars of free, speaking music in the first violin, and a slow cadence that opens the door to the next movement.

**Main ideas**

- Two firm chords, like a call to attention.
- The violin's phrases: free in rhythm, like a singer's [[recitative]].

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Two loud chords, all four together | A sudden change of mood |
| ≈ 0:10 | The first violin alone, quick then slow, like someone speaking; the others answer softly | A short recitative, leading to the Adagio |
| Last seconds | A soft, unfinished chord | It leads straight into the variations |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how short this is. Beethoven gave it a number of its own, but it works as a bridge.

### IV. Andante ma non troppo e molto cantabile

**Summary.** The heart of the quartet and its longest movement: a gentle tune in A major, followed by six [[variation]]s and a long [[coda]]. Each variation keeps the outline of the tune but changes its speed, rhythm and mood.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a simple, singing tune in short steps, shared between the two violins, which answer each other phrase by phrase.
- The variations: a march, a playful duet, a slow and rocking one, a light one, a hymn.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A simple, gentle tune; the two violins take turns with its short phrases | The theme |
| ≈ 1:30 | The tune is decorated: the lower instruments trade fragments of it in a jumpy, dotted rhythm | Variation 1 |
| ≈ 2:55 | A little faster, in four firm beats, almost a march | Variation 2 (Più mosso) |
| ≈ 4:05 | Lighter and teasing: two instruments imitate each other, one a beat behind the other | Variation 3 (Andante moderato e lusinghiero, "coaxing") |
| ≈ 5:40 | Slower, in a rocking rhythm; the four lines are closely woven | Variation 4 (Adagio) |
| ≈ 8:05 | Quick and light; the tune is reduced to its outline | Variation 5 (Allegretto) |
| ≈ 8:50 | Very slow and soft, like a hymn; the cello keeps interrupting with a short trembling figure | Variation 6 (Adagio, in nine beats to the bar) |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to hum the tune at the start. Then listen for how much of it you can still find in each variation. In some it is clear, in others only its shape remains.
2. In variation 6, listen to the cello. Its little shudders disturb the calm of the hymn.

### V. Presto

**Summary.** A fast, bright [[scherzo]] in E major, almost childlike in its tune. The cello starts it with a short call followed by silence. Its quieter middle part comes round twice, and near the end all four players bow close to the bridge, which makes a glassy, whispering sound.

**Main ideas**

- The call: a few quick notes in the cello, then a full stop.
- The scherzo tune: a quick, simple melody like a nursery rhyme, tossed between the instruments.
- The middle part: smoother and more singing, in A major.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

### VI. Adagio quasi un poco andante

**Summary.** A short, sad song in G-sharp minor, two minutes long. The viola begins alone; the others join with a slow, quiet melody. It does not end: it stops on a chord that leads straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The viola's opening: a slow, dark phrase that turns the first notes of the opening fugue upside down.
- The melody: a quiet lament in the upper instruments.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The viola alone, slow and low | The opening phrase |
| ≈ 0:15 | The others join; a slow, sorrowful melody in the violins | The lament |
| Near the end | The music stops on an unresolved chord | It leads straight into the finale |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the very first notes of the viola and think back to the fugue that opened the quartet. Beethoven has turned its first notes upside down.

### VII. Allegro

**Summary.** A fierce, driving finale back in C-sharp minor, the only movement of the seven in [[sonata form]]. It is full of hard, galloping rhythms, but a soaring, lyrical theme brings moments of relief. It ends with three loud chords in C-sharp major.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: loud, hard notes in octaves with a jagged, galloping rhythm.
- The line in long notes: over a galloping repeated note, the first violin sings a line whose shape recalls the fugue of the first movement.
- Theme 2: a soaring, lyrical tune in the first violin.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Loud and hard: all four in octaves, in a jagged, galloping rhythm | Theme 1, in C-sharp minor |
| ≈ 0:20 | Quieter; a galloping repeated note in the viola, and a long-note line above it | The fugue's shape returns in the finale |
| ≈ 0:55 | Softer and singing: a long, soaring tune in the first violin | Theme 2 |
| ≈ 1:20 | Loud again; the galloping rhythm is thrown from instrument to instrument | The [[development]] |
| ≈ 2:40 | The hard opening theme, very loud | The [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 3:35 | The soaring tune once more | Theme 2, now in D major |
| ≈ 4:25 | Very quiet, then building again | The [[coda]], long and restless |
| Last seconds | Three loud chords | The end, in C-sharp major |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the long-note line near ≈ 0:20. It is the shape of the opening fugue, now inside the fastest music of the quartet.
2. The very end turns from minor to major, but the last chords sound hard won, not happy.

### Threads

- **No breaks.** The seven movements follow one another without stopping. Three of them (III, VI, and in a way II) are short links. The big ones are the fugue, the variations, the scherzo and the finale.
- **One shape.** The fugue tune at the start comes back: turned upside down by the viola in VI, and inside the fast music of VII.
- **Four voices.** In the fugue they are equals. In the variations the violins share the tune; in the scherzo the cello sets everything off.
- **Keys that climb.** C-sharp minor, then D major one step up, then a journey through other keys before the finale returns home.
