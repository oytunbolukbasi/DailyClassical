### The big picture

- In 1899 Finland was a grand duchy of the Russian Empire, and that February the emperor issued a manifesto that cut back its self-government. Censorship of the press grew. In November the Finnish newspapers organised the Press Celebrations in Helsinki, a covert patriotic protest.
- The evening included a series of tableaux from Finnish history, from the time of the Kalevala onwards. Sibelius wrote the music. The last tableau was called "Finland Awakes".
- He reworked that final number into a separate piece, first performed in Helsinki on 2 July 1900 by the Helsinki Philharmonic Society under Robert Kajanus. The name *Finlandia*, suggested by Sibelius's friend Axel Carpelan, came into use soon after. Under Russian rule the piece is said to have been played at times under harmless titles such as "Impromptu".
- The quiet hymn in the middle is often taken for a folk tune, but it is Sibelius's own. In 1941 the poet Veikko Antero Koskenniemi set words to it, and as the "Finlandia Hymn" it is sung in Finland almost as a second national anthem. With other words it is also a church hymn in many countries, for example "Be Still, My Soul".
- In one line: dark, menacing brass (the oppression), a call to struggle, a confident fast march, a hymn of hope, and a blazing end.

### I. Finlandia

**Summary.** A [[symphonic poem]] in one movement, built like a story: from darkness, through struggle, to light. It opens with heavy, groaning chords in the brass, answered by plaintive woodwinds. A hammering rhythm calls to battle, a confident fast section follows, and then everything stops for a quiet hymn. The fast music returns and drives to a triumphant ending.

**Main ideas**

- The brass chords: low, heavy and dark, in an unclear key, like a weight pressing down.
- The call: a short, stamping rhythm in the brass over rolling timpani.
- The hymn: a calm, noble melody in long notes, first in the woodwinds, then in the strings.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment the hymn begins. After so much noise, the orchestra drops to a hush, and the tune sounds like voices singing together.
2. Compare the brass at the start and at the end. The same instruments that sounded threatening now sound triumphant.

**In this recording.** Karajan recorded *Finlandia* with the Berlin Philharmonic for Deutsche Grammophon in 1964, and again, digitally, twenty years later. This is the 1984 remake: broad and slow, about nine and a half minutes, where many performances take about eight.

### Threads

- **Music as protest.** *Finlandia* was born at a protest against censorship and was heard as a national rallying cry; under Russian rule it reportedly sometimes had to hide behind other titles.
- **From darkness to light.** The piece moves from heavy minor-key brass to a major-key hymn and a triumphant close, the same journey in eight or nine minutes that some symphonies take an hour to make.
- **A tune that left its piece.** The hymn now lives on its own, as a Finnish patriotic song and as a church hymn around the world.
