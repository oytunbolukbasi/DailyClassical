### The big picture

- Mozart finished it in Salzburg; the heading of his manuscript is dated 20 December 1775. He was nineteen and concertmaster of the Archbishop's court orchestra. It is the last of his five violin concertos, four of them written within a few months of that year.
- Nobody knows for whom it was written. Mozart was a fine violinist and played his own concertos; so did Antonio Brunetti, the Salzburg court's leading violinist. The manuscript is now in the Library of Congress in Washington.
- The nickname is not Mozart's. It comes from the finale, a graceful [[minuet]] interrupted by a loud, stamping episode in the "Turkish" style. This was Europe's idea of the music of the Janissaries, the Ottoman army's bands, as it was imitated in Austria and elsewhere at the time. It is not real Ottoman music.
- The orchestra is small: two oboes, two horns and strings. Mozart left no [[cadenza|cadenzas]] for these concertos, so soloists play their own or someone else's.
- In one line: a bright opening that the soloist interrupts with a slow song (I), a long, tender Adagio (II), a minuet with a "Turkish" march in the middle (III).

### I. Allegro aperto

**Summary.** The orchestra opens with a bright, confident tune. Then the soloist enters in a way found in no other Mozart concerto: not with the tune, but with six bars of slow, singing music, as if a second movement had begun by mistake. The quick music returns, and the violin plays a new melody of its own over the orchestra's opening theme.

**Main ideas**

- The orchestra's theme: a quiet, rising broken chord in the violins over a pulsing accompaniment, which grows bright and loud.
- The violin's Adagio: a slow, sweet melody, marked *dolce*, over a soft murmur in the strings.
- The violin's Allegro tune: a quick, springing line that floats above the orchestra's theme, two melodies at once.
- The second theme: a playful, chattering idea in the violin, in the brighter key of E major.

2. The slow Adagio entry is never heard again. It stays a single, unexplained moment of calm.

**In this recording.** Anne-Sophie Mutter was fourteen at these sessions, and it was her first recording. Karajan had heard her in 1976 and invited her to play Mozart with the Berlin Philharmonic in Salzburg the following spring. The tempos are broad: the slow movement alone takes almost eleven minutes.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A long, tender song in E major, the longest movement of the three. The violin sings over a soft accompaniment, and the orchestra answers between its phrases. A short darker stretch in the middle passes, the opening song returns, and a cadenza leads to a quiet close.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a slow, gently rocking melody, first played by the orchestra and then taken up by the violin, high and sustained.
- The second idea: a calmer, more flowing phrase in a new key.
- The middle: the same material clouded by minor harmonies.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the orchestra keeps answering the violin, like a second voice in a duet.

**A second slow movement.** In 1776 Mozart wrote a separate Adagio in E major for violin and orchestra, K. 261, for Brunetti. It is often said to have replaced this movement, which Brunetti reportedly found "too studied", but scholars treat that link as a likely guess, not a fact.

### III. Rondeau. Tempo di menuetto

**Summary.** A [[rondo]] built on an elegant minuet tune, which returns between contrasting episodes. In the middle the music suddenly changes metre, key and character: a loud, stamping march in A minor, in the "Turkish" style. Then the minuet comes back as if nothing had happened.

**Main ideas**

- The minuet: a graceful, unhurried tune in three beats, the refrain of the rondo.
- The episodes: the violin's own lively passages between the returns of the minuet.
- The "Turkish" march: fast, in two beats, in A minor; loud passages played by everyone on the same notes, short figures repeated over and over, and the cellos and basses striking their strings with the wood of the bow, like a drum.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the change at ≈ 4:30: three graceful beats in a bar become two hard ones, and major becomes minor.
2. There are no drums or cymbals in this orchestra. The "percussion" is made by the strings themselves.

**What "Turkish" meant.** Europe had known the Ottoman military bands, the *mehter*, for a long time, through wars and embassies, and in the 18th century European armies copied their bass drum and cymbals. In Austria "Turkish" music became a fashion. What composers imitated was only an outline: a march rhythm, loud unison tunes, repeated figures and the sound of drums. They did not use Ottoman modes, the *makam*, or Ottoman instruments such as the shrill *zurna*. Mozart returned to the style later, in *The Abduction from the Seraglio* (1782) and in the *alla turca* finale of his Piano Sonata in A major.

### Threads

- **Surprises inside good manners.** Each outer movement sets up polite expectations and then breaks them: a slow entry for the soloist in I, a "Turkish" march inside a minuet in III.
- **The violin as a singer.** Mozart, then busy with Italian opera, gives the violin long singing lines: the six-bar Adagio in I and the whole of II.
- **Quiet endings.** The finale does not end with a flourish; the minuet simply fades out.
