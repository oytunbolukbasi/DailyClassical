### The big picture

- A [[requiem]] is the Catholic Mass for the dead, named after its first words, "Requiem aeternam" ("eternal rest"). Most composers make its centre the *[[Dies irae]]*, a long poem about the day of judgement, full of trumpets and fire. Fauré leaves it out, keeping only its last, gentle prayer, the Pie Jesu. Instead he adds two texts sung at the graveside: the Libera me and the In paradisum, in which angels lead the dead into paradise.
- Fauré was choirmaster at the Madeleine, one of the grandest churches in Paris. A first version in five movements was sung there on 16 January 1888, at the funeral of an architect, Joseph Lesoufaché, with Fauré conducting. Women could not sing in the Madeleine's choir, so boys sang the high parts; the Pie Jesu was sung by a ten-year-old, Louis Aubert, who later studied composition with Fauré. The priest is said to have told Fauré afterwards that the church did not need "these novelties".
- Fauré added the Offertoire in 1889–90 and brought in a Libera me he had written in 1877. In 1899–1900 the score was enlarged for full orchestra so that it could be performed in concert halls; this version was first heard at the Trocadéro in Paris on 12 July 1900, during the World's Fair. The 1900 score is the one heard here; nobody knows how much of its orchestration is by Fauré himself.
- The Requiem was written for no one in particular: "for pleasure, if I may call it that", Fauré said. His father had died in 1885 and his mother at the end of 1887, but he never linked the work to them. To an interviewer he explained that someone had called it a lullaby of death, and that this was how he saw death: "as a happy deliverance". It was sung at his own funeral at the Madeleine in 1924.
- In one line: a hushed plea for rest (I), a prayer with a baritone at its centre (II), a floating "Holy" (III), a child's prayer (IV), a lamb, and a light (V), the only fear of judgement (VI), and the angels (VII).

### I. Introït et Kyrie

**Summary.** The choir prays for eternal rest, almost on a single chord. The tempo then eases, and a plain, flowing melody passes from the tenors to the sopranos and on into the Kyrie, the plea for mercy. One brief outburst and one urgent cry stand out against a movement that is mostly very quiet.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: soft, solemn chords for the whole choir, the voices barely moving.
- The tenors' melody: a simple line, close to plainchant, over gently moving strings. It becomes the tune of the Kyrie.

### II. Offertoire

**Summary.** The prayer that the souls of the dead may be freed from the pit and the darkness. It begins as a quiet [[canon]] for altos and tenors, one voice shadowing the other. In the middle a baritone sings alone, then the choir returns, and the movement ends on a soft, radiant "Amen" in B major.

**Main ideas**

- The canon: a narrow melody in B minor, sung by the altos and answered a moment later by the tenors.
- Hostias: the baritone solo, which begins on a single repeated note.
- The Amen: a turn from minor to major at the very end.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 0:20 | The altos sing a hushed, narrow melody; the tenors follow with the same tune a beat behind | "O Domine Jesu Christe, rex gloriae" ("O Lord Jesus Christ, King of glory"), in [[canon]] |
| ≈ 1:15 | The same pattern starts again a step higher; the basses join, more urgently | The prayer to deliver the dead "from the deep lake" |
| ≈ 2:25 | Short, soft phrases broken by silences | "Ne cadant in obscurum" ("let them not fall into the darkness") |
| ≈ 3:20 | The choir falls silent; the baritone sings alone, first on one repeated note | "Hostias et preces" ("sacrifices and prayers we offer you") |
| ≈ 4:15 | The baritone's line opens out into a warmer melody | "Fac eas, Domine, de morte transire ad vitam" ("Lord, let them pass from death to life") |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment the music turns to the major at the "Amen". It is a small change, and it changes the whole movement.

### III. Sanctus

**Summary.** The song of the angels, "Holy, holy, holy". Many composers set it with blazing brass; Fauré makes it float. Harp, rippling, and a high violin line above it, while the sopranos and the men answer each other with a three-note phrase. Only the "Hosanna" brings a short burst of horns and full voices.

**Main ideas**

- The three-note phrase: a gentle rise and fall, passed between the sopranos and the men.
- The violin line: a slow melody above everything else, often played by a solo violin.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Rippling harp and a high, rising violin line | The angels' music, in E-flat major |
| ≈ 0:10 | The sopranos sing softly on three notes; the tenors and basses answer with the same phrase | "Sanctus" ("Holy"), sung like a call and its echo |
| ≈ 1:10 | The phrases widen and the sound grows | "Pleni sunt caeli et terra gloria tua" ("heaven and earth are full of your glory") |
| ≈ 2:10 | Horns ring out with firm, loud chords; the men sing out | "Hosanna in excelsis" ("Hosanna in the highest") |
| ≈ 2:50 | The sopranos answer more softly; the harp ripples return | The brightness fades |
| Near the end | The violin line floats upwards; the choir sings "Sanctus" once more, very softly | The movement closes in E-flat major |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the high violin line instead of the voices. It hardly ever stops, and Fauré once called these violins "angelic".

### IV. Pie Jesu

**Summary.** "Gentle Lord Jesus, give them rest; give them everlasting rest." One line of text, sung three times by a single high voice over soft organ and strings. It is the last verse of the *[[Dies irae]]*, the only part of that poem Fauré kept.

**Main ideas**

- The solo line: simple and slow, close to plainchant, ending each time on a phrase that rocks between two notes.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft chords in the organ and strings | A short introduction in B-flat major |
| ≈ 0:10 | A single high voice sings a calm, slow phrase | "Pie Jesu Domine, dona eis requiem" ("gentle Lord Jesus, give them rest") |
| ≈ 0:45 | The same words again, the melody reaching a little higher | The second call |
| ≈ 1:20 | The line widens, then settles, rocking between two notes | "Sempiternam requiem" ("everlasting rest") |
| ≈ 2:20 | The opening phrase returns, now sinking lower | The third and last call |
| Last 20 seconds | The voice falls silent; the organ and strings close very softly | The end of the prayer |

**Things to notice**

1. The voice is never covered: the orchestra only holds soft chords underneath. Listen for how few notes Fauré needs.

**In this recording.** The soloist is a boy treble, Alain Clément, as at the first performances in the Madeleine. In concert halls Fauré himself preferred a woman's voice for this solo.

### V. Agnus Dei

**Summary.** "Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world, grant them rest." A long, flowing tune in the strings carries the tenors' melody. The harmony darkens for a moment, then a soprano note opens into "Lux aeterna", eternal light. At the end, the chords of the very opening of the Requiem come back, and the Agnus Dei tune closes it in D major.

**Main ideas**

- The string tune: broad and expansive, under and around the tenors.
- The tenors' melody: rising and falling gently, sung twice.
- The return: the "Requiem aeternam" chords of the first movement.

**Listening stops**

### VI. Libera me

**Summary.** "Deliver me, Lord, from eternal death on that dreadful day." The only place in the work where judgement day is named. A baritone sings over a steady, heartbeat-like rhythm in the low strings; the choir trembles, then breaks out with horns for "Dies illa, dies irae", "that day, the day of wrath". Fauré had written this music in 1877, as a separate piece.

**Main ideas**

- The pulse: a soft, regular figure in the basses, like footsteps or a heartbeat, under the baritone's song.
- The baritone's melody: wide and urgent, with sudden leaps.
- The day of wrath: the loudest music in the Requiem, with horn calls.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A steady, quiet pulse in the low strings; the baritone begins | "Libera me, Domine, de morte aeterna" ("deliver me, Lord, from eternal death") |
| ≈ 1:50 | The choir enters softly, in plain chords, the voices close together | "Tremens factus sum ego" ("I am made to tremble") |
| ≈ 2:35 | Loud chords and horn calls; the music speeds up | "Dies illa, dies irae" ("that day, the day of wrath") |
| ≈ 3:05 | Suddenly soft again, then a swell and a fading prayer | "Requiem aeternam dona eis" ("eternal rest grant them") |
| ≈ 3:40 | The whole choir sings the baritone's melody together on one line, strongly | The opening "Libera me" in [[unison]] |
| ≈ 5:15 | The baritone, then the choir, repeat the words quietly | The plea fades |

1. Keep an ear on the low pulse. It barely stops, even when the choir is shouting, and it is still there in the last bars.

### VII. In paradisum

**Summary.** "May the angels lead you into paradise." Words sung as the body is carried out of the church. The organ plays a light, rippling figure that never stops; above it the sopranos sing a long, rising melody, and the rest of the choir joins only for a few words. The Requiem ends in D major, quietly, high in the air.

**Main ideas**

- The organ's ripple: a fast, soft [[ostinato]] of broken chords, like bells far away.
- The sopranos' melody: slow, rising, very high.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The organ starts a soft, fast rippling figure, high up | The [[ostinato]] that runs through the whole movement |
| ≈ 0:10 | The sopranos enter with a long, rising melody | "In paradisum deducant te angeli" ("may the angels lead you into paradise") |
| ≈ 1:35 | The rest of the choir joins softly in rich harmony | "Jerusalem", the heavenly city |
| ≈ 2:00 | The sopranos alone again, with a new phrase | "Chorus angelorum te suscipiat" ("may a choir of angels receive you") |
| ≈ 3:25 | The full choir gathers on the final words, very softly | "Aeternam habeas requiem" ("may you have eternal rest") |
| Last 30 seconds | A long, quiet D major chord, the organ still rippling, then silence | The end of the Requiem |

**Things to notice**

1. Notice where the Requiem ends: in D major, the bright twin of the D minor in which it began.

### Threads

- **No terror.** Fauré left out the *Dies irae* and kept judgement day for a single passage in the Libera me. Everything else is about rest, light and paradise.
- **From D minor to D major.** The work opens on hushed D minor chords and ends in D major among the angels; the opening chords come back in the Agnus Dei to mark the way.
- **Church and concert.** Fauré wrote it for the Madeleine, with boys' voices and a small orchestra, then saw it grow for the concert hall. Both versions are performed today, and this recording uses the larger one.
