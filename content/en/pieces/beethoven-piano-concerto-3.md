### The big picture

- Beethoven noted the first idea in 1796, on a concert tour that took him to Berlin, and probably hoped to play the concerto in Vienna in April 1800. It was not ready. The premiere came on 5 April 1803 at the Theater an der Wien, with Beethoven as soloist, in a concert that also introduced his Second Symphony and the oratorio *Christ on the Mount of Olives*.
- Even then the solo part was not fully written down. His page-turner, Ignaz von Seyfried, later recalled pages that were almost empty apart from a few scribbled signs: Beethoven played most of it from memory.
- It is Beethoven's only piano concerto in a minor key, and its C minor drama owes something to Mozart's Concerto K. 491 in the same key.
- One of the very first ideas he wrote down was "kettledrum at the cadenza". It became the quiet, mysterious ending of the first movement, where the timpani and the piano talk softly together.
- In one line: a contest between piano and orchestra (I), a slow song in a far-away key (II), a restless dance that breaks into C major (III).

### I. Allegro con brio

**Summary.** The orchestra plays for more than three minutes before the piano says a word. Then the soloist storms in and goes through the same themes again, its own way: the Classical [[double exposition]]. After the [[cadenza]] comes the moment Beethoven planned first of all, a soft dialogue of piano and kettledrum.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: quiet strings in unison climb the notes of the C minor chord, then a short knocking rhythm answers. The knock returns all through the movement.
- Theme 2: a gentle, singing melody in the major, in violins and clarinets.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how long the orchestra plays before the piano's first note. The piano's three scales sound like a door thrown open.
2. After the cadenza, follow the timpani. It plays the knocking rhythm of the first bars, now almost a whisper.

**In this recording.** Richter and Kurt Sanderling had already played this concerto together in Moscow in 1952, and Sanderling recorded it again in 1994 with Mitsuko Uchida (see the other recommendations). On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 4–6, after Mozart's D minor Concerto.

### II. Largo

**Summary.** After C minor, Beethoven jumps to E major, a key very far away. The effect is like stepping into another room. The piano begins alone with a slow, hymn-like melody in soft chords; the orchestra answers.

**Main ideas**

- The Largo melody: slow, quiet chords in the piano, with the tune on top.
- The middle section: the piano's flowing [[arpeggio]]s under a duet of flute and bassoon.

### III. Rondo. Allegro

**Summary.** A [[rondo]]: the piano's nervous C minor tune keeps coming back, with brighter episodes in between. Near the end the music switches to a quick, lilting six-beat dance, and C minor turns into C major.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo tune: quick and restless, with a leaning, accented note right at its start.
- Episode 1: a carefree tune in the major, first in E-flat major, later in C major.
- Episode 2: a calm clarinet melody, followed by a passage in which the strings enter one after another.

### Threads

- **A drum from 1796.** The first idea Beethoven wrote down was a kettledrum after the cadenza. Its knocking rhythm runs through the first movement and gets the last, quietest word.
- **Minor to major.** The first movement ends firmly in C minor; the finale breaks out into a C major dash.
- **A far-away key.** The Largo's E major is a world apart, and the finale's leaning note keeps a thread back to it.
