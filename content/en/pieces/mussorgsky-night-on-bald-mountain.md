### The big picture

- An old legend says that witches gather for a sabbath on St John's Night, 23–24 June, on the Bald Mountain (Lysa Hora) near Kyiv. Mussorgsky finished his first version of this music on 23 June 1867, St John's Eve itself, after twelve days of work.
- His mentor Mily Balakirev criticised it harshly. Mussorgsky put it aside, and that version was never played in his lifetime.
- He reused the music twice: in 1872 for a collective opera-ballet, *Mlada*, which was never finished, and in 1880 as a peasant boy's dream in his opera *The Fair at Sorochyntsi*. This last version ended quietly, at dawn.
- Mussorgsky died in 1881. His friend Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov turned the 1880 version into the orchestral piece we usually hear and conducted its first performance in St Petersburg in October 1886. Disney's *Fantasia* (1940) used Leopold Stokowski's arrangement, based on Rimsky's.
- In one line: voices from under the earth, the black god and his worshippers, a wild sabbath, a church bell, and daybreak.

### I. Night on Bald Mountain

**Summary.** A piece of [[programme music]] with a story printed in the score: subterranean voices, the appearance of the spirits of darkness and of Chernobog, the "black god", their glorification of him, and the witches' sabbath. At its height a distant village church bell rings and the spirits scatter. Then morning comes. Almost all of it is loud and fast; the last few minutes are slow and calm.

**Main ideas**

- The whirlwind: rushing strings and shrieking woodwinds, swirling up and down.
- Chernobog: a heavy, stamping theme in trombones and tuba.
- The sabbath dance: short, jerky phrases tossed between woodwinds and strings, faster and wilder each time.
- Daybreak: a calm, songlike melody for solo clarinet, then flute, over soft strings and harp.

**Things to notice**

1. Count the bell strokes. After each one, listen to what happens to the music.
2. The dawn melody is played by single instruments, one at a time. After eight minutes of full orchestra, the change of colour is the change from night to day.

**In this recording.** Fritz Reiner recorded this piece with the Chicago Symphony Orchestra in March 1959, in Orchestra Hall, for an LP of Russian showpieces called *Festival*. On the Spotify album it is track 17, after Reiner's 1957 recording of *Pictures at an Exhibition*.

### Threads

- **Two composers in one piece.** The themes, the bell and the dawn ending come from Mussorgsky's 1880 version; the orchestration and much of the shape are Rimsky-Korsakov's. Mussorgsky's first version of 1867, published only in 1968, has no bell and no dawn: it ends with the sabbath.
- **Night to day.** The piece moves from D minor to D major, from the sabbath to the morning, and from the full orchestra to a single clarinet.
- **A Russian legend.** Like much of Mussorgsky's music, it comes from Russian and Ukrainian folk tales, here by way of Gogol, whose story gave *The Fair at Sorochyntsi* its plot.
