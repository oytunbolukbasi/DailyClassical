### The big picture

- Bach wrote this concerto out in Leipzig around 1738, as the fifth of a set of six concertos for harpsichord and strings in his own hand (the manuscript is now in the Berlin State Library). Why he gathered them is not known. One idea links them to the Collegium Musicum, the society of students and musicians he directed, which gave weekly concerts at Zimmermann's coffee house; another to a visit to the court at Dresden in 1738.
- Like most of the set, it is an arrangement of older music. The outer movements probably come from a lost violin concerto in G minor: in several places in the manuscript Bach first wrote the notes a step too high, as if copying from a G minor original, and then corrected them. Violinists today play a reconstruction of that concerto.
- The slow movement has its own history. It is also the opening sinfonia of the cantata *Ich steh mit einem Fuß im Grabe* (I stand with one foot in the grave), BWV 156, first performed in 1727 or 1729, where an oboe sings the melody in F major. It probably began as the slow movement of a lost oboe concerto, and its first bars almost match the opening of a flute concerto by Bach's friend Georg Philipp Telemann. It has been heard on its own in films, among them Woody Allen's *Hannah and Her Sisters* (1986).
- Concertos for a keyboard soloist were new. Until then the harpsichord had mostly played the [[continuo]], filling in the harmony underneath. Here it steps forward: the right hand takes the old violin or oboe part, and in the full-band passages the harpsichord plays along with everyone else.
- In one line: a restless dialogue in F minor (I), a slow song over plucked strings (II), a quick, driving dance (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** Bach wrote no tempo here; players take it as a moderate Allegro. The strings and harpsichord open together with a firm, short theme, the [[ritornello]], and almost at once the harpsichord breaks away to repeat the last figure on its own, like an echo. For the rest of the movement the band keeps a plain two-beat pulse while the harpsichord runs in [[triplet]]s, three notes to each beat.

**Main ideas**

- The ritornello: a short, firm theme in F minor for the strings, with the harpsichord playing along.
- The solo line: an almost unbroken stream of triplets in the harpsichord's right hand, the kind of figure a violin plays.

### II. Largo

**Summary.** The strings pluck soft chords in [[pizzicato]] and the harpsichord sings one long melody above them, slow and richly ornamented. A harpsichord cannot hold a note the way an oboe or a voice can, so Bach keeps the line alive with trills, turns and little runs. At the very end the music turns away from its home key and stops on an open chord, leading straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a slow melody that starts on a long note and then unwinds in ornaments, high in the harpsichord.
- The accompaniment: plucked chords in the strings, with the harpsichord's left hand on the bass.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the harpsichord makes a slow tune sing: every long note is followed by quick ornaments that keep the sound from dying away.
2. Notice the last chord. It does not close the movement; it leaves a question that the Presto answers.

### III. Presto

**Summary.** A fast finale in three short beats to the bar, back in F minor. The band's [[ritornello]] alternates with solo passages full of quick runs and leaping broken chords. The first movement's echo game comes back the other way round: now the band repeats a figure while the harpsichord is silent.

**Main ideas**

- The ritornello: a quick, insistent theme in three, for the whole band with the harpsichord.
- The solo: fast runs and wide, leaping broken chords in the harpsichord, which on a violin would have been chords of two notes played together.

1. Compare the echoes with the first movement. There the harpsichord answered the band; here the band answers the harpsichord.

### Threads

- **Borrowed music.** A lost violin concerto, a lost oboe concerto, a church cantata and a theme by Telemann all stand behind this short piece. Bach was his own arranger, and in the Largo he made the old solo line richer for the harpsichord.
- **Echoes.** In the first movement the harpsichord repeats the band's figures; in the finale the band repeats the harpsichord's.
- **A plucked instrument that sings.** The harpsichord's strings are plucked and its notes fade at once. In the Largo, over strings that are also plucked, Bach makes it sing with ornaments instead of held notes.
