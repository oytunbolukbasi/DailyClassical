### The big picture

- In October 1940 Bartók left Hungary, then drawing closer to Nazi Germany, and settled in New York with his wife, the pianist Ditta Pásztory. America was hard for him: little money, few concerts, and from 1943 an illness that was later identified as leukaemia. He had almost stopped composing.
- Two Hungarian musicians in America, the violinist Joseph Szigeti and the conductor Fritz Reiner, asked Serge Koussevitzky of the Boston Symphony Orchestra to help. In 1943 Koussevitzky visited Bartók in hospital and offered him $1,000 for an orchestral work in memory of his wife, Natalie.
- Bartók wrote it in about eight weeks, from 15 August to 8 October 1943, at a health resort at Saranac Lake in upstate New York. Koussevitzky conducted the premiere in Boston on 1 December 1944 and, Bartók reported, called it the best orchestral piece of the last 25 years. In February 1945 Bartók wrote a new ending for the finale.
- Why "concerto"? In his own note Bartók said the work treats single instruments or groups "in a concertant or soloistic manner". Every section of the orchestra gets its turn in the spotlight. He also described its course: from the sternness of the first movement and the "lugubrious death-song" of the third to the life-affirming finale.
- In one line: a dark introduction and a fierce allegro (I), a game of pairs (II), a night lament at the centre (III), a song interrupted by a parody (IV), a whirling dance that ends in triumph (V). The five movements mirror each other around the third.

### I. Introduzione

**Summary.** A slow, mysterious introduction grows out of a theme rising in fourths in the low strings. It speeds up into a fierce, fast movement in [[sonata form]]: a leaping main theme, a bold call on the trombone and a gentle, folk-like tune on the oboe. In the middle the brass build the trombone's call into a [[fugato]].

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a slow, hushed line for cellos and basses that climbs in wide steps (fourths), answered by shimmering upper strings.
- The main theme: a fast, springing tune in the violins, also built on leaps of a fourth.
- The trombone call: a short, bold fanfare, again made of fourths.
- The second theme: a quiet oboe tune that rocks back and forth like a folk song.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the interval of a fourth: in the slow opening, in the main theme and in the trombone call. One small shape holds the movement together.
2. In the brass fugato, count the entries: each player begins the same call a little after the previous one.

**In this recording.** Fritz Reiner had known Bartók since 1905 and was reportedly one of the two musicians who asked Koussevitzky to commission this work. He made its first recording in 1946 with the Pittsburgh Symphony; this one, with the Chicago Symphony Orchestra, followed in October 1955. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–5.

### II. Giuoco delle coppie

**Summary.** "The game of pairs." A side drum taps out a rhythm alone. Then five pairs of wind instruments take turns, each pair playing in parallel at its own fixed distance: bassoons, oboes, clarinets, flutes, then [[muted]] trumpets. A solemn brass [[chorale]] stands in the middle, and then the pairs come back, more richly accompanied.

**Main ideas**

- The side drum: a dry rhythm that opens and closes the movement.
- The pairs: each pair plays its own short, dancing tune, always moving together at the same interval.
- The chorale: a slow hymn for the brass.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 2:35–3:30 | Slow, solemn chords in the brass | The brass [[chorale]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the distance between the two instruments of each pair. Sixths and thirds sound sweet; sevenths and seconds sound tart.
2. The side drum both opens and closes the movement, like a frame around a picture.

### III. Elegia

**Summary.** The heart of the work, and what Bartók called its death-song. Mysterious ripples in the woodwind and harp surround slow, grieving melodies taken from the first movement. It is an example of Bartók's [[night music]]: soft, strange sounds like the noises of a night outdoors.

**Main ideas**

- The night sounds: rippling woodwind, harp and quiet string figures.
- The laments: broad, passionate melodies in the strings, drawn from the slow opening of the first movement.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Low strings, dark and hushed, recall the work's opening | The first movement's introduction remembered |
| ≈ 0:40–2:00 | Rippling flutes and clarinets, harp, and a lonely oboe line | [[Night music]] |
| ≈ 2:15 | The strings break out in a passionate, crying melody | The first lament |
| ≈ 3:00–4:00 | The music swells to a loud climax, then sinks back | The first climax |
| ≈ 4:30 | Violas, then the strings, sing another broad melody | The second lament |
| ≈ 5:30 | Another anguished climax for the whole orchestra | The second climax |
| Last 1:15 | The rippling night sounds return, and the movement fades | The night music closes the movement |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the background as much as the melody. The ripples and murmurs in woodwind and harp are the "night" around the lament.
2. The laments do not come from nowhere. They grow out of the slow opening of the whole concerto.

### IV. Intermezzo interrotto

**Summary.** "Interrupted intermezzo." A quirky oboe tune alternates with a warm, nostalgic melody in the violas. Then a vulgar, jaunty tune breaks in and is mocked with jeering trills and sliding trombones. It is widely heard as a parody of a march from Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony, then famous in America; the tune also recalls Lehár's operetta *The Merry Widow*. Afterwards the nostalgic melody returns.

**Main ideas**

- The oboe tune: lively and off-balance, its metre keeps changing.
- The viola melody: broad and tender, based on a Hungarian song in praise of the homeland.
- The interruption: a cheap, catchy tune, laughed at by the orchestra.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The oboe plays a skipping tune with an uneven step | The first idea |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the laughter: fast trills in the woodwind and sliding notes in the brass.
2. Compare the warm viola melody with the tune that interrupts it. One sounds like a memory of home, the other like a street song.

### V. Finale

**Summary.** After a call on the horns, the strings set off in a whirling [[perpetuum mobile]] of village dance rhythms. A trumpet tune becomes the subject of a large fugue, and after a quiet, mysterious passage the dance returns. The movement ends with the brass in triumph and a final rush upwards: the "life-assertion" Bartók wrote of.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: a short, weighty opening (*Pesante*).
- The whirl: fast, unbroken running notes in the strings, like a frantic folk dance.
- The trumpet tune: a bright melody that becomes a fugue subject.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Horns play a short, heavy call | The introduction, *Pesante* |
| ≈ 0:10 | The strings start a whirling run of fast notes that hardly stops | *Presto*: a [[perpetuum mobile]] |
| ≈ 1:20 | Stamping dance rhythms, with the whole orchestra joining in | The dance gathers |
| ≈ 2:00 | Trumpets play a bright, strutting tune | The fugue subject |
| ≈ 3:10–4:30 | The tune is passed from instrument to instrument, piling up voice on voice | The [[fugue]] |
| ≈ 4:30–5:20 | The music becomes quiet and mysterious, with soft rustling figures | A hush before the return |
| ≈ 5:20 | The whirling strings return | The dance comes back |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the strings at the start. They play almost without a break for minutes on end.
2. Compare the end with the first movement's opening. The concerto began in darkness, with low strings alone; it ends in full light, with the whole orchestra.

### Threads

- **From night to day.** Bartók himself described the plan: from the sternness of the first movement through the death-song of the third to the life-affirming finale.
- **A mirror.** The five movements are arranged symmetrically: two large outer movements, two lighter scherzo-like movements, and the Elegia at the centre.
- **Everyone a soloist.** Each section has its moment: the brass in the fugato, the wind pairs in the second movement, the violas with the Hungarian song, the strings in the finale's whirl.
- **Hungary far away.** Folk-like tunes and dance rhythms run through the work, written in America by a composer who had spent decades collecting the music of Hungarian villages.
