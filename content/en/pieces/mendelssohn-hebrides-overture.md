### The big picture

- In the summer of 1829 Mendelssohn, aged twenty, toured Scotland on foot with his friend Karl Klingemann. On 7 August they reached Tobermory on the Isle of Mull, and that evening he wrote home to show "how extraordinarily the Hebrides have affected me". In the letter he sketched what had come into his head there: the first twenty-one bars of the overture, already with notes on which instruments should play.
- The next day the two friends sailed to the small island of Staffa to see Fingal's Cave, a sea cave lined with black basalt columns. Mendelssohn was too seasick to write; Klingemann described a cave like the inside of an enormous organ, full of the roar of the waves. So the famous opening was written before Mendelssohn ever saw the cave.
- He finished a first version in Rome on 16 December 1830, calling it *The Lonely Island*, then revised it heavily. The new version was first played in London on 14 May 1832, and it was published in 1833–35 under several titles: *The Hebrides* on the parts, *Fingal's Cave* on the score.
- It is a concert overture: a single piece for the concert hall, not the prelude to a play or an opera. It tells no story but paints a mood, and some count it among the first tone poems. Wagner called Mendelssohn "a landscape painter of the first order".
- In one line: a rolling sea, a broad song for the cellos, storms and calm, and a quiet ending on the waves.

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** One movement in [[sonata form]]. A short, rocking phrase, low in the orchestra, is repeated again and again like waves; a broad, singing second theme in the cellos and bassoons answers it. The middle grows stormy, the themes return, a fast burst near the end whips up the sea once more, and the overture ends quietly, as if the island were disappearing from view.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a short phrase that rises and falls, played softly in the violas, cellos and bassoons and repeated on different notes, like waves rolling in.
- Theme 2: a long, warm melody in D major, first in the cellos and bassoons, one of Mendelssohn's best-loved tunes.
- The calls: short fanfares in the woodwinds and brass, like voices or signals heard across the water.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the very beginning: the rocking phrase almost never stops. It is the sea under everything else.
2. Notice how quietly the overture ends. After all the storms, there is no loud final chord.

### Threads

- **A picture in sound.** The overture describes no events, only a place and a mood. Later composers would call such pieces [[symphonic poem|symphonic poems]]; Mendelssohn called it an overture.
- **Scotland.** The same journey of 1829 also gave Mendelssohn the first idea for his *Scottish* Symphony, which he finished only thirteen years later.
- **Revisions.** Mendelssohn kept rewriting his music. He reworked this overture several times between 1830 and 1832 before letting it be published, and the opening sketch from the letter survived almost unchanged.
