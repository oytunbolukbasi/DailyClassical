### The big picture

- Rimsky-Korsakov wrote the Capriccio in the summer of 1887, at a rented villa on a lake near Luga, south of St Petersburg. He was spending that summer orchestrating his late friend Borodin's opera *Prince Igor*, and broke off to write it.
- It grew out of sketches for a showpiece for violin and orchestra on Spanish themes. He decided the tunes would do better in a work for the whole orchestra, and kept a part for solo violin among many other solos. The themes are real Spanish dance tunes; the opening one comes from a published collection of Spanish folk music.
- At the first rehearsal, he wrote, the orchestra had hardly finished the first movement when it began to applaud. He asked the players for permission to dedicate the piece to them, and the printed score lists all 67 of them by name. The premiere, in St Petersburg in the autumn of 1887 with the composer conducting, ended with an insistent encore.
- People praised the orchestration, and Rimsky-Korsakov thought they missed the point. The Capriccio, he wrote, is "a brilliant composition for the orchestra": the changes of colour, the solo cadenzas and the rhythm of the percussion are the music itself, "not its garb".
- The five movements are played without a break, in two groups: I–III, then IV–V.
- In one line: a dawn song (I), a set of variations (II), the dawn song again (III), a gypsy scene with five cadenzas (IV) and a whirling dance from Asturias (V).

### I. Alborada: Vivo e strepitoso

**Summary.** An [[alborada]] is a morning song, a dance to greet the sunrise, here in the manner of Asturias in northern Spain. This one bursts out at full force, in A major: a stamping, two-beat dance for the whole orchestra with ringing percussion. A clarinet and then a solo violin answer with quick, showy runs, and the violin closes the movement quietly.

**Main ideas**

- The alborada tune: a bright, stamping dance theme in a fast two-beat rhythm.
- The solos: quick, decorated runs for the clarinet and the solo violin.

### II. Variazioni: Andante con moto

**Summary.** A gentle tune, first in the horns, then passed from section to section in a set of [[variation]]s. Each time it gets a new colour and a new mood: serene, then a little mournful, then rising to a climax, then calm again. The movement ends with a slow, slithering line in the flute.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a simple, singing Spanish folk melody, first heard in the horns.

### III. Alborada

**Summary.** The dawn song returns, almost note for note, but in a different key, B-flat major, and with the solos swapped: now the violin goes first and the clarinet answers. Rimsky-Korsakov later admitted that here the brass can drown the woodwinds, and advised conductors to play it a little less loudly.

**Main ideas**

- The alborada tune, as in movement I, in new colours.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The stamping dance again, in the full orchestra | The alborada, in B-flat major |
| ≈ 0:15 | The solo violin's runs | The solos reversed: violin first |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare it with the first movement. The tune is the same; listen to how much the different key and the swapped soloists change its colour.

### IV. Scena e canto gitano: Allegretto

**Summary.** A gypsy scene and song. Over drum rolls, five free, unaccompanied [[cadenza]]s follow one another: first the horns and trumpets with a fanfare, then the solo violin, the flute, the clarinet and finally the harp, with the triangle. Then the gypsy song begins, a swaying dance in three beats, while the string players pluck chords like guitarists.

**Main ideas**

- The fanfare: a call for horns and trumpets over a drum roll.
- The cadenzas: short solo flights for the brass, violin, flute, clarinet and harp.
- The gypsy song: a passionate, swaying dance tune in three beats, with the strings imitating guitars.

### V. Fandango asturiano

**Summary.** A [[fandango]], a fast Spanish dance in three beats, here from Asturias in the north of Spain. The trombones begin the tune and the woodwinds finish it; castanets click throughout. The dance whirls faster and faster, and at the end the alborada of the first movement bursts back in to finish the piece.

**Main ideas**

- The fandango tune: a vigorous, clicking dance in three beats, first half in the trombones, second half in the woodwinds.
- The alborada: the opening dance, returning as the finale's climax.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | The trombones start a vigorous dance tune; the woodwinds finish it; castanets click | The fandango |

**Things to notice**

### Threads

- **A concerto for the orchestra.** Nearly every player has a solo moment: clarinet and violin in the alboradas, horns in the variations, five cadenzas in the gypsy scene, trombones in the fandango.
- **Spain seen from Russia.** Glinka had travelled in Spain and written overtures on the tunes he collected there; Rimsky-Korsakov follows him, with real folk melodies but a Russian orchestra's colours.
- **Beginning and end.** The alborada opens the piece, returns in the middle and comes back to finish it, so that the whole Capriccio swings around one dance.
