### The big picture

- In 1874 Smetana, then the leading Czech composer and chief conductor of Prague's Provisional Theatre, began to hear a ringing in his ears. Within months he was completely deaf. In 1876 he moved out of Prague to Jabkenice, a village in the forests of central Bohemia, to live with his eldest daughter's family.
- There, between the autumn and 29 December 1876, he wrote this quartet. He called it a private piece, "four instruments conversing among themselves" about the things that tormented him. The noise in his ears let him work for only about an hour at a time.
- In a letter to his friend Josef Srb-Debrnov he explained each movement: youth and a warning of misfortune, the dances of his young days, his first love, and the joy of creating Czech music, cut short by deafness.
- Players at first found it too difficult. It was tried out privately in Prague with Antonín Dvořák playing the viola, and first performed in public on 29 March 1879, at the Konvikt hall in Prague.
- In one line: the call of fate (I), a polka from his dancing youth (II), a love song (III), the joy of Czech music, broken by a piercing high note (IV).

### I. Allegro vivo appassionato

**Summary.** It opens with a cry: a sharp chord, a restless trembling in the violins, and a passionate solo for the viola, which Smetana called "the call of fate into the struggle of life". The movement is about his young self, he wrote: a love of art, romantic longings "for something I could not express", and a warning of the misfortune to come.

**Main ideas**

- The viola's theme: a bold, aching melody that starts with a leap, played by the viola alone over agitated accompaniment.
- Theme 2: a warmer, more hopeful tune in G major, Smetana's "inclination towards romanticism in music and in love".

1. A quartet usually begins with the first violin. Here the viola, the quartet's inner voice, gets the opening tune. At the first private play-through, the viola player was Dvořák.

### II. Allegro moderato à la Polka

**Summary.** A [[polka]], the Czech dance Smetana grew up with. He wrote that it took him back to "the joyful days of youth", when he wrote dance music and was known as a passionate dancer, among country people and in elegant salons. There are three kinds of dance here: a sturdy country polka, a trumpet-like call on the viola, and a gentler salon dance in the middle.

**Main ideas**

- The polka: a strong, bouncy dance tune, first with all four playing together.
- The call: a contrasting figure on the viola, marked "quasi tromba", like a trumpet.
- The middle section: the viola and cello keep a polka rhythm while the violins play smooth chords above.

### III. Largo sostenuto

**Summary.** A love song for Kateřina Kolářová, Smetana's first wife, who died in 1859. He wrote that it recalls "the happiness of my first love for the girl who later became my faithful wife". It opens with a solo for the cello, then the first violin sings the main melody. The music grows into a passionate struggle before it settles again into a quiet, reflective end.

**Main ideas**

- The cello's opening: a long, searching phrase, alone at first.
- The love theme: a tender, singing melody in A-flat major for the first violin.
- The second melody: different in shape but just as ardent.

### IV. Vivace

**Summary.** The finale celebrates what Smetana called "the discovery that I could treat national elements in music", with a lively, folk-like dance. Then, near the end, everything stops. Over a shivering [[tremolo]], the first violin plays a long, piercing high E: the whistling in his ears that in 1874 announced his deafness. Fragments of the first movement drift back, and the quartet ends in resignation.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a bright, quick folk-like tune in E major, full of energy.
- The high E: a long, thin, whistling note in [[harmonics]] on the first violin.
- The memories: a quiet return of ideas from the first movement.

**In this recording.** The Pavel Haas Quartet, from Prague, recorded both Smetana quartets in 2014; the album won the 2015 Gramophone Award for chamber music.

### Threads

- **Music as autobiography.** Smetana gave each movement a chapter of his life. A programme like this was then very unusual in a string quartet; it later inspired Janáček's two programmatic quartets.
- **The warning.** The misfortune announced in the first movement arrives in the last, as the high E. Smetana said the long note in the finale grew out of the warning at the beginning.
- **Czech voice.** The polka, the folk-like finale and the joy in "national elements" make this one of the first great Czech chamber works.
