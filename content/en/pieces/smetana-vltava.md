### The big picture

- *Vltava* is a [[symphonic poem]], the second of the six in *Má vlast* ("My Country"), Smetana's portrait of the landscape, history and legends of Bohemia, written between 1874 and 1879. It is the best known of the six and is often played on its own.
- Smetana wrote it between 20 November and 8 December 1874. A few weeks earlier, in October, he had gone completely deaf. It was first performed in Prague on 4 April 1875, conducted by Adolf Čech.
- It is [[programme music]], and Smetana wrote the programme himself: the river rises from two springs, one cold and one warm, joins into one stream, flows through woods and meadows past a hunt and a village wedding, through a moonlit night with water nymphs dancing, past castles and ruins, crashes through the St John's Rapids, widens towards Prague, flows past the old castle of Vyšehrad and vanishes into the distance.
- The great river tune is an adaptation of an old melody known across Europe as *La Mantovana*. The same ancestor lies behind a Czech folk song and the Israeli national anthem, *Hatikvah*.
- In one line: two springs become a river, which passes a hunt, a wedding and a moonlit dance, rushes through rapids and flows proudly through Prague.

### I. Vltava

**Summary.** One continuous journey down the river. The flowing river tune returns between scenes from the banks, like a [[rondo]] theme, and each scene has its own colour: horns for the hunt, a polka for the wedding, muted shimmering strings for the moonlight, crashing cymbals for the rapids. At the end the river tune turns from minor to major, and the theme of Vyšehrad, from the first poem of the cycle, sounds out in the brass.

**Main ideas**

- The springs: quick, rippling notes in two flutes, then two clarinets, with single plucked notes like drops of water.
- The river tune: a broad, singing melody in the violins, rising and falling like a current.
- The scenes on the banks: a horn hunt, a village polka, a night-time dance of water nymphs.
- Vyšehrad: a slow, proud brass theme from *Vyšehrad*, the first poem of *Má vlast*.

**Things to notice**

1. Count how often the river tune returns. Between its returns, the river is passing something on its banks.
2. The St John's Rapids no longer exist. In the 1940s and 1950s they disappeared under the water of a dam, about twenty kilometres south of Prague.

**In this recording.** Rafael Kubelík, a Czech conductor, left Czechoslovakia after the Communist coup of 1948 and recorded *Má vlast* in exile, here in Boston in 1971. In 1990, after the fall of Communism, he returned to Prague to conduct it with the Czech Philharmonic at the Prague Spring festival.

### Threads

- **A cycle in miniature.** The Vyšehrad theme near the end links *Vltava* to the first poem of *Má vlast*, and the river itself flows past the castle that the first poem portrays.
- **Minor to major.** The river tune starts in E minor. When the river reaches Prague it returns in E major, the same tune in a brighter light.
- **A national piece.** Since 1952 the Prague Spring festival has opened every year on 12 May, the anniversary of Smetana's death, with a performance of the complete *Má vlast*.
