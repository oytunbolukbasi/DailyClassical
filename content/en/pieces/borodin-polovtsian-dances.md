### The big picture

- *Prince Igor* was Borodin's great opera project. He took the story from *The Lay of Igor's Campaign*, a medieval Russian epic about a prince who rode out against the Polovtsy, nomads of the southern steppe, in 1185, was defeated and taken prisoner. The critic Vladimir Stasov suggested the subject in 1869.
- Borodin was a professor of chemistry first and a composer in his spare time, and he worked on the opera, on and off, for almost eighteen years. When he died suddenly in 1887 it was still unfinished. Rimsky-Korsakov and Alexander Glazunov completed and orchestrated it, and it was first staged in St Petersburg in 1890.
- The dances end Act 2 (Act 1 in some versions). In the Polovtsian camp Khan Konchak treats his captive Igor as an honoured guest, and calls his slaves to dance and sing for him while the Polovtsy sing the Khan's praises.
- The dances were ready long before the opera. For a concert in 1879 Rimsky-Korsakov, who was rehearsing the chorus, lost patience and helped Borodin and Anatoly Lyadov orchestrate them in a single night. They wrote in pencil, and Borodin coated the pages with gelatine and hung them up to dry like washing.
- In one line: the slave girls' longing song (I), the men's wild dance (II), praise of the Khan (III), the boys' dance (IV), the girls' song again (V), the boys once more (VI) and everyone together (VII).

### I. Introduction – Gliding Dance of the Maidens

**Summary.** A soft, shimmering introduction, then one of the most famous melodies in Russian music: a slow, curving tune with a touch of the East, first in the oboe and then in the cor anglais. The captive women take it up. They sing of their homeland, asking their song to fly there on the wings of the wind.

**Main ideas**

- The maidens' melody: long, swaying and sad, with ornaments that curl around the main notes. In 1953 the Broadway musical *Kismet* turned it into the song "Stranger in Paradise".

**Main ideas**

- The praise of the Khan: a broad, swinging tune in three beats, sung by the full chorus over a pounding orchestra.

**In this recording.** Beecham made the recording at Abbey Road in November 1956 with his own orchestra and choir. On this album, a 1999 CD reissue, the dances follow his *Scheherazade* as tracks 5–11, divided into seven parts.

### Threads

- **Two worlds in one camp.** The captive women's longing song (I, V) and the warriors' wild dances (II, IV, VI) belong to the same scene. Borodin keeps them apart at first, then makes them sound together.
- **The East as seen from Russia.** Curling ornaments, repeated drumming rhythms and bright percussion: Borodin's picture of the steppe peoples, as imagined by a nineteenth-century Russian, not a record of their real music.
- **A composer with a day job.** Borodin wrote this music in the time his laboratory left him, and the dances survived only because his friends pushed him to finish them for a concert.
