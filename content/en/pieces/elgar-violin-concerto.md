### The big picture

- In 1907 the violinist Fritz Kreisler, who had called Elgar the greatest living composer, asked him for a concerto. The Royal Philharmonic Society commissioned it in 1909. Elgar, himself a violinist, worked out the solo part with W. H. "Billy" Reed, leader of the London Symphony Orchestra.
- Kreisler gave the first performance in London on 10 November 1910, with the London Symphony Orchestra and Elgar conducting. Reed remembered it as a complete triumph. It was the last of Elgar's works to win immediate popular success.
- The score carries a Spanish quotation from the novel *Gil Blas*: "Aquí está encerrada el alma de .....", "Herein is enshrined the soul of .....". Whose soul the five dots hide is one of Elgar's riddles. The favourite answer is Alice Stuart-Wortley, daughter of the painter John Everett Millais, whom Elgar called "Windflower"; he gave that name to several of the concerto's themes. There is no proof.
- It is one of the longest concertos in the repertoire, and one of the hardest to play. Its most famous moment is not a showpiece at all: near the end, the violin muses alone on earlier themes while the orchestra accompanies it with a soft, thrumming sound.
- In one line: a long, passionate drama with a tender "Windflower" theme (I), a song of quiet devotion (II), a brilliant finale that stops to remember everything (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra alone sets out six related themes, some striding, some tender. Then the violin enters, quietly, as if finishing the orchestra's sentence. The rest of the movement is a large [[sonata form]] in which the soloist takes the themes, above all the gentle second one, and makes them its own.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a striding, sighing phrase in B minor for the full orchestra.
- The "Windflower" theme: a tender melody that the orchestra only hints at, and which the violin later sings in full.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the violin first enters. It does not announce itself; it slips into the orchestra's music, almost in mid-sentence.
2. Notice how often the violin plays two or more notes at once. These [[double stop]]s are one reason the concerto is so hard.

**In this recording.** Nigel Kennedy was 27 when he recorded the concerto with Vernon Handley and the London Philharmonic in Watford Town Hall in March 1984. He takes nearly 54 minutes, one of the broadest performances on record; Yehudi Menuhin's 1932 recording, conducted by Elgar himself, takes almost 50. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 4–6, after Elgar's *Introduction and Allegro* for strings.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow song in B-flat major, far from the B minor of the first movement. The orchestra begins with a calm melody, and the violin enters to carry it further. The music is mostly quiet and intimate, but it rises to one passionate climax before settling back.

**Main ideas**

- The opening melody: a simple, rocking tune for the strings, which the violin takes up.
- The [[nobilmente]] theme: a broad, noble phrase. Elgar once said he would like it inscribed on his tomb.

### III. Allegro molto

**Summary.** The finale starts as a dazzling display, with the violin racing through fast runs and chords. But just when the end seems near, the music stops to look back. In a long [[cadenza]], the violin muses on themes from the first two movements, while the orchestra, unusually, accompanies it with soft, thrumming strings. Then the opening theme of the concerto returns, and the work ends in a blaze of B major.

**Main ideas**

- The finale theme: fast, restless runs for the violin, answered by a bold tune in the orchestra.
- The cadenza: the violin alone remembers the "Windflower" theme and others, over a soft hum from the strings.

**Things to notice**

1. In the cadenza, listen for the soft thrumming of the strings. The players strum across the strings with their fingers; Elgar marks it *pizzicato tremolando*.
2. Try to recognise the "Windflower" theme of the first movement when the violin remembers it.

### Threads

- **The riddle.** "Herein is enshrined the soul of ....." Elgar never said whose soul. The tender "Windflower" theme is the clue most listeners follow.
- **Partners.** The violin rarely fights the orchestra. It enters by finishing the orchestra's phrase, and in the cadenza the orchestra stays to accompany it.
- **Remembering.** The finale looks back over the whole work before it ends. Elgar said of the concerto: "I love it".
