### The big picture

- Rachmaninoff left Russia with his family at the end of 1917 and never returned. To support them he became a touring pianist, and for eight years he wrote almost nothing new. Some ideas for a fourth concerto went back to his last years in Russia.
- In 1926 he took a year off from touring and finished the concerto, mostly in New York, with the last work done in Europe. He dedicated it to his friend and fellow exile Nikolai Medtner, and joked to him that it was so long it would have to be performed "like *The Ring*: on several evenings in succession."
- He gave the first performance in Philadelphia on 18 March 1927, with Leopold Stokowski conducting. The critics were scathing. Rachmaninoff cut it and published it in 1928, then withdrew it. In 1941 he revised it again, more deeply, and recorded the new version with Eugene Ormandy. It was the last original work he took up.
- The music is leaner than in his earlier concertos: shorter themes, sharper rhythms, sudden turns. Rachmaninoff loved American jazz bands and pianists, and many listeners hear their influence here, especially in the slow movement.
- In one line: a restless, surging Allegro with a sudden end (I), a hesitant, bluesy nocturne (II), a hectic chase that ends in a hymn and a blaze of G major (III).

### I. Allegro vivace (Alla breve)

**Summary.** There is no slow build-up: after a brief surge in the orchestra, the piano arrives as if on the crest of a wave, with a big theme in heavy chords. The movement is in a compressed [[sonata form]], with a gentler second theme and a development that brings in a new, singing melody. Instead of a grand finish, it stops short.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a broad theme in the piano's chords that climbs and then falls, with the orchestra surging around it.
- Theme 2: quieter, falling, decorative lines in the piano, joined by the strings.
- The singing theme: a slower, lyrical melody in the middle of the movement. Remember it: it returns at the end of the concerto.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the movement ends. After so much energy, Rachmaninoff stops almost in mid-thought instead of building to a big final cadence.
2. Keep the singing melody of the development in your ear. It is the concerto's secret thread.

**In this recording.** Lisitsa plays the 1941 revision, the version usually heard today. She recorded all four concertos and the *Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini* with Michael Francis and the London Symphony Orchestra at Abbey Road in 2009–10; this Fourth was recorded in December 2009 and issued by Decca in 2013.

### II. Largo

**Summary.** A slow movement in C major that never quite settles. The piano starts alone, as if improvising; then the violins offer a very simple idea of three falling notes, which piano and orchestra turn over and over. An outburst breaks in, and the close reuses music Rachmaninoff had written in 1911 for a piano étude and never published. The movement leads straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The three-note idea: three slow, falling notes, first in the violins.
- The harmonies: soft, sliding chords that many listeners hear as close to the blues.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | The piano alone, quiet and hesitant, as if searching for a tune | An improvised-sounding introduction |
| ≈ 0:30 | The violins play a simple figure of three falling notes; the piano answers with soft chords | The main idea |

**Things to notice**

1. Sing the three falling notes to yourself. Almost everything in the movement is built from them.
2. Listen for the end: the movement does not finish; it dissolves into the finale.

### III. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The finale bursts in with a crash of cymbals. The piano drives on in fast, hectic rhythms, broken by a quiet, more lyrical episode in D-flat major with a gentle horn tune. In the 1941 version the usual return of the themes is replaced by a long [[coda]]: over a held bass note the tension builds, the singing melody from the first movement comes back as a hymn, and the concerto ends in G major.

**Main ideas**

- The chase: a fast, nervous theme in the piano, falling in shape, pushed on by the orchestra.
- The horn tune: a simple, gentle melody in D-flat major.
- The hymn: the lyrical theme from the first movement's development, now broad and triumphant.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the horn in the quiet episode. After the noise around it, the simplicity is the surprise.
2. When the hymn arrives near the end, try to recognise it from the first movement.

### Threads

- **Revision.** Rachmaninoff cut the concerto from 1,016 bars to 902 in 1928 and to 824 in 1941. Some pianists, among them Vladimir Ashkenazy, think the uncut original of 1926 was the better piece; it was published only in 2000.
- **One theme across the concerto.** The singing melody from the first movement's development returns as the finale's hymn. In the 1941 version this is how the concerto ties itself together.
- **New York.** Written mostly in the city where Rachmaninoff now lived, the concerto has a nervous, modern edge, and the slow movement in particular has been heard as an echo of the jazz he loved.
- **Dedications.** Rachmaninoff dedicated the concerto to Nikolai Medtner; the next year Medtner dedicated his own Second Piano Concerto to Rachmaninoff.
