### The big picture

- No one knows exactly when Bach wrote it. The only source in his hand is a set of parts copied in Leipzig around 1730 by Bach, his son Carl Philipp Emanuel, his pupil Johann Ludwig Krebs and another copyist. For a long time it was thought to come from his years at Köthen (1717–23), where he led the prince's small orchestra; some scholars now think he wrote it for the Collegium Musicum, the Leipzig concert society he took over in 1729.
- Bach learned the concerto from Vivaldi. In Weimar, around 1713–14, he arranged a number of Vivaldi's violin concertos for keyboard and organ. This one follows the Italian plan: three movements, fast–slow–fast, with a returning band refrain, the [[ritornello]], in the outer movements. But Bach weaves the band and the soloist far more tightly together.
- Bach was a violinist himself. His son Carl Philipp Emanuel later wrote that his father played the violin "cleanly and penetratingly" until late in life. Later Bach arranged this concerto for harpsichord, a tone lower, in G minor (BWV 1058).
- It never quite dropped out of sight. In 1841 Felix Mendelssohn passed the score to Ferdinand David, leader of the Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra, and it was first printed in 1852. Not everyone was won over: after its first American performance, in 1860, a critic called it "very heavy and uninspired".
- In one line: a fierce, purposeful A minor (I), a slow song over a rolling bass (II), a quick jig (III).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The band opens with a firm, assertive theme, the [[ritornello]], which comes back in fragments and in new keys throughout the movement. When the violin enters it brings its own, more yearning idea. The two characters alternate and overlap until the opening theme returns in A minor to close.

**Main ideas**

- The ritornello: a bold, purposeful theme for the band, starting with a short, emphatic figure and running on in quick notes.
- The violin's theme: a longer, more lyrical, yearning line that the band never takes over.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow movement in C major built over a short, insistent figure in the bass that comes back again and again, while the harmony above it changes. Over it the violin sings a long, free line, almost like an opera aria. Only in the last phrase do bass, band and soloist come together.

**Main ideas**

- The bass figure: a short, rolling pattern in the cellos and bass, repeated through the movement, an [[ostinato]].
- The violin's song: long, decorated phrases above, sometimes soaring, sometimes almost speaking.

### III. Allegro assai

**Summary.** A fast jig in nine quick beats to the bar, grouped in threes. Each time the band plays its refrain, the instruments enter one after another as in a [[fugue]]. Between these entries the soloist races in brilliant passages, and near the end the violin rocks rapidly between two strings, one of them left open, so that a single line seems to shimmer.

**Main ideas**

- The jig theme: a bouncing, skipping tune in threes, passed from part to part in the band.
- The solo passages: quick runs and, near the end, a rapid rocking across the strings.

### Threads

- **From Vivaldi to Bach.** The plan is Italian: fast–slow–fast, a returning refrain, a virtuoso soloist. What is Bach's own is the counterpoint: fugal entries in the finale and a bass that never stops working in the Andante.
- **A violinist's concerto.** Bach knew the instrument from the inside: the yearning solo line of the first movement, the long song of the second and the string-crossing effects of the third are all written for the violin's own strengths.
- **The same music, another instrument.** Bach later turned it into a harpsichord concerto in G minor. Hearing that version shows how much of the violin part he had to rewrite for the keyboard.
