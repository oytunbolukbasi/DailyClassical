### The big picture

- Beethoven spent the summer of 1806 at the Silesian estate of his patron Prince Lichnowsky. In September they visited Count Franz von Oppersdorff, who kept a private orchestra and played Beethoven's Second Symphony for him. The count then paid for a new symphony, and Beethoven wrote this one, dedicating it to him.
- He had already begun the Fifth, but set it aside. Why the commission became this lighter, more Haydn-like work instead is not known; one suggestion is that the count's liking for the Second invited something in the same vein.
- It was first played privately in March 1807 at the Vienna palace of Prince Lobkowitz, and in public at the Burgtheater in April 1808.
- It has always lived in the shadow of its neighbours. Schumann is said to have called it "a slender Greek maiden between two Norse giants"; Berlioz wrote that the slow movement seemed the work of the Archangel Michael rather than of a human being.
- In one line: a dark, hushed introduction that bursts into high spirits (I), a slow song over a steady heartbeat (II), a dance that keeps tripping over its own beat (III), and a perpetual-motion finale that ends with a joke (IV).

### I. Adagio – Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The symphony begins almost in the dark: a soft, low note, a long-held chord in the winds, and strings creeping downwards in the minor, as if the music cannot find its key. After thirty-eight slow bars, loud chords break the spell and the music races off in high spirits. The fast part is a [[sonata form]], with a famous hushed drum roll leading back to the main theme.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: quiet, slow, uncertain, wandering through distant keys.
- Theme 1: a quick, springing tune that shoots upwards.
- Theme 2: a little "conversation between the bassoon, the oboe and the flute" (Tovey).

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A long, slow song in E-flat. Under it, from the first bar to the last, runs a steady figure of a long and a short note, like a quiet heartbeat. The song returns several times, more richly decorated each time, and between its returns the clarinet sings a second, still gentler melody. At the very end the timpani play the heartbeat alone.

**Main ideas**

- The heartbeat: a long–short figure, first in the second violins.
- The song: a broad, tender melody in the first violins.
- The second melody: a soft, singing tune for solo clarinet.

**Listening stops**

### III. Allegro vivace – Un poco meno allegro

**Summary.** Many scores call it a minuet, but it is far too fast to dance to: a [[scherzo]] whose tune keeps pulling against its own three-beat bar, so that the music seems to stumble in twos. Its gentler middle section, the [[trio]], comes round twice, and the main section three times, a lay-out Beethoven used again in the Seventh Symphony. At the end two horns blow the movement away.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: loud, jabbing phrases whose accents fall in twos across the three beats, a kind of [[hemiola]].
- The trio: slower, quiet woodwind phrases, with the violins chirping in between.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Loud, jerky phrases that seem to limp across the beat | The scherzo |
| ≈ 1:05 | A gentler, slower tune in the woodwind, with little interjections from the violins | The trio |
| ≈ 2:25 | The jerky dance returns | The scherzo, second time |
| ≈ 3:15 | The woodwind tune again | The trio, second time |
| Last minute | The dance a third time, shortened; then the two horns call out, and the movement is over | Last return and coda |

**Things to notice**

1. Try tapping three beats to a bar in the opening section. Within seconds Beethoven's accents will throw you off.

### IV. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** A finale in non-stop motion, a [[perpetuum mobile]] of quick notes passed around the orchestra, full of jokes and sudden contrasts. Beethoven called this mood *aufgeknöpft*, "unbuttoned". Near the end, in Haydn's manner, he brings back the main tune at half speed, broken by pauses, before a last rush.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a whirl of fast notes in the violins.
- The bassoon: in the return of the theme, the first bassoon has to play the whirling notes alone, a passage bassoonists know well.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The violins whirl off in fast, continuous notes; loud chords answer | The main theme |
| ≈ 0:25 | A smoother tune in the woodwind, over the busy strings | The second theme |
| ≈ 1:05 | The whirling theme from the start | The exposition is repeated |
| ≈ 2:10 | The music turns stormier, the fast notes tossed between sections | The development |
| ≈ 3:05 | The bassoon alone races through the fast theme | The [[recapitulation]] begins with the bassoon solo |
| Last 20 seconds | The main tune slowed down, with pauses, then a sudden loud dash to the end | The joke ending: the theme at half speed, then the final flourish |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the bassoon around three minutes. The fast notes that the violins whirled through at the start are now given to one wind player, unaccompanied.

### Threads

- **Darkness to light, briefly.** The symphony opens in hushed uncertainty, but the darkness lasts only a minute and a half. Everything after is light, fast and witty.
- **Haydn's shadow.** The slow introduction, the wit, the pauses before the end all recall Haydn, Beethoven's former teacher, still alive when the Fourth was first played.
- **The timpani.** The soft drum roll in the first movement, the drum heartbeat at the end of the Adagio: Beethoven gives the timpani quiet, important moments, not only loud ones.
