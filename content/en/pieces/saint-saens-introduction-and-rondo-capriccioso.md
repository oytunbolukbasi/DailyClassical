### The big picture

- Saint-Saëns wrote this piece in 1863 for Pablo de Sarasate, a Spanish violinist nine years younger who had come to study in Paris as a boy. Saint-Saëns had already written a concerto for him in 1859, when Sarasate was only 15; the Introduction and Rondo capriccioso came next. Sarasate gave the first performance in Paris on 4 April 1867, with the composer conducting.
- It is a showpiece in two parts played without a break: a short, sad song, then a fast [[rondo]], a form in which one tune keeps coming back between new episodes. *Capriccioso* means whimsical: the rondo tune hesitates, skips and lands on the wrong beats.
- Paris in those years loved anything Spanish, and the rondo has the lilt of a Spanish dance, with sharp off-beat accents and sudden swerves. It made the piece a favourite of violinists from the start. Georges Bizet, the future composer of *Carmen*, arranged it for violin and piano, and his version was printed in 1869, ten years before the orchestral score.
- In one line: a melancholy song with a flash of fireworks, then a skittish dance that returns again and again, and a race to the finish.

### I. Andante malinconico – Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** The introduction is a sad, sighing song for the violin over quiet chords, broken once by a sudden burst of fast notes. Then the rondo begins: a jumpy, syncopated tune that comes back several times, with more lyrical tunes and dazzling passages in between. Near the end the music speeds up and the violin races to the last chords.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a melancholy melody, slow and close to speech, marked *Andante malinconico* ("walking, melancholy").
- The rondo tune: short, light phrases that start off the beat, as if the violin were hesitating before each step. Its [[syncopation]] gives it a Spanish swagger.
- The episodes: a smoother, singing melody, and passages of rapid runs and leaps that test the soloist.

**Things to notice**

1. Tap your foot to the rondo tune. Its accents fall where you do not expect them, and the violin seems to arrive a moment late or early. That is the *capriccioso*.
2. Compare the end with the beginning: a piece that opens in sorrow ends in a blaze.

**In this recording.** Perlman made this album with Jean Martinon and the Orchestre de Paris in July 1974, together with Saint-Saëns's *Havanaise*, Chausson's *Poème* and Ravel's *Tzigane*. Martinon had himself trained as a violinist at the Paris Conservatoire.

### Threads

- **Written for a player.** Saint-Saëns wrote his First and Third Violin Concertos, and this piece, for Sarasate. Sarasate's light, pure tone and easy speed are built into the music: few long, heavy notes, many quick, clean ones.
- **Spanish Paris.** Bizet's *Carmen*, Lalo's *Symphonie espagnole*, also for Sarasate, and this rondo all come from the same French fascination with Spain.
- **Sorrow, then sparkle.** The slow, sad introduction is short, but it makes the rondo's high spirits sound brighter.
