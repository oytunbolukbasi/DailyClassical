### The big picture

- Mendelssohn wrote the Octet in Berlin in the autumn of 1825 and finished it on 15 October. He was sixteen. He gave the score to his friend and violin teacher Eduard Rietz as a birthday present.
- It is written for four violins, two violas and two cellos. In Louis Spohr's double quartets, two separate quartets face each other; Mendelssohn treats the eight as one ensemble, and wrote in the score that it must be played "in symphonic orchestral style", with louds and softs sharper than usual.
- The scherzo is thought to have been inspired by the "Walpurgis Night's Dream" in Goethe's *Faust*, a scene of spirits and fairies that ends with mist lifting and everything vanishing. Mendelssohn later orchestrated this movement and used it in place of the minuet of his First Symphony.
- Music from the scherzo comes back in the finale, and the finale opens with all eight players entering one after another. The work was revised before it was published in 1832.
- In one line: a soaring, symphonic opening (I), a lilting song in the minor (II), a whispered flight of spirits (III), a race in which all eight voices chase each other (IV).

### I. Allegro moderato ma con fuoco

**Summary.** The first violin climbs in great leaps over a shimmering, throbbing accompaniment from the other seven, and the music sounds less like chamber music than like a small orchestra. The movement is in [[sonata form]] and takes almost half the length of the work.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, first violin: a rising tune that leaps upwards in long strides, over quick, trembling chords.
- Theme 2: a quieter, gentler melody, moving in smaller steps, shared among the upper instruments.

1. In the first minute, listen under the violin's tune. Seven players keep up a quick, trembling pulse, and that pulse is what makes the music sound orchestral.
2. In the build-up at the end of the development, notice how the music starts from almost nothing: long, quiet notes that gradually break into quicker and quicker motion.

They play the repeat of the exposition, which is why the first movement lasts over fourteen minutes. On the Spotify album the Octet is tracks 1–4, followed by a cello quintet by Boccherini.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow movement in C minor with a gentle, rocking lilt, like a song in a swaying rhythm. The eight voices move closely together, sometimes in rich chords, sometimes in simple lines; the mood is tender rather than tragic.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a lilting melody in six, first heard low in the ensemble and then rising to the violins.
- The answer: a warmer passage in a major key, with the parts weaving around each other.

### III. Scherzo: Allegro leggierissimo

**Summary.** Very fast and very quiet: tiny, detached notes flicker from one player to another, never settling. Mendelssohn's marking means "as light as possible". It was probably inspired by Goethe's spirits, and it ends as if everything had simply blown away.

**Main ideas**

- The flicker: quick, short, whispered notes in G minor, passed between the instruments.
- The flights: little runs and trills that dart upwards in the violins.

**Things to notice**

1. Try to hear how soft this movement is. Eight string players almost never play loudly here, and the effect depends on that restraint.

### IV. Presto

**Summary.** The finale starts in the depths: the second cello alone sets off with a rushing tune, and the others enter one after another, upwards, until all eight are racing. Later the whispering music of the scherzo slips back in. It is a [[fugato]] that turns into a high-speed celebration.

**Main ideas**

- The race: a quick, rushing tune that each instrument takes up in turn.
- The quotation: a broad, hymn-like phrase that recalls "And he shall reign for ever and ever" from the "Hallelujah" Chorus in Handel's *Messiah*.
- The return: the scherzo's flickering theme, heard again inside the finale.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | The second cello alone rushes off with a quick, rumbling tune; one by one the others join, each higher than the last | An eight-part [[fugato]], from the lowest instrument to the highest |

**Things to notice**

1. In the first seconds, count the entries from the bottom upwards: cello, cello, violas, violins. By the time the first violin enters, all eight are playing.
2. When the scherzo's theme returns, listen for how quickly it is swept back into the race.

### Threads

- **A small orchestra.** Mendelssohn asked for a symphonic style, and the Octet often sounds bigger than eight players: trembling accompaniments, sharp contrasts of loud and soft, all the voices together.
- **Spirits.** The scherzo's lightness, linked with Goethe's *Faust*, returns in the finale. The next year Mendelssohn wrote the overture to *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, with the same fairy lightness.
- **Counterpoint.** From the finale's fugato to the voices chasing each other in the scherzo, the sixteen-year-old shows off his [[counterpoint]].
