### The big picture

- In 1721 Bach, then music director to Prince Leopold at Köthen, copied out six concertos in his own hand and sent them to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg-Schwedt, with a dedication in French dated 24 March. He had played for the margrave a few years earlier, probably in Berlin in 1719. Bach called them simply *Six concertos for several instruments*; the name "Brandenburg" was given to them later.
- Each of the six uses a different group of soloists. No. 5 has three: a flute, a violin and a harpsichord, with a small string band. The harpsichord has two jobs at once: it is one of the soloists, and it is also the [[continuo]], filling in the chords under everyone else.
- It was probably the last of the six to be written, perhaps to show off a new harpsichord by the Berlin maker Michael Mietke that Bach had bought for the Köthen court in 1719. Bach almost certainly played the harpsichord part himself.
- The first movement ends with a long [[cadenza]] for harpsichord alone, far longer than anything a keyboard had been given in a concerto before. Scholars see in this work the beginning of the keyboard concerto.
- In one line: a bright race that ends in a huge harpsichord solo (I), a quiet conversation for three (II), a skipping finale that starts like a fugue (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** A firm, sunny refrain for everyone, the [[ritornello]], returns again and again, while the flute and the violin trade ideas between its returns. Little by little the harpsichord's running notes push forward, until everyone else falls silent and it plays alone for minutes on end.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a bold tune for the whole group, built on repeated notes and a steady, striding bass.
- The duet: the flute and the violin pass a short rising figure back and forth.
- The harpsichord's runs: quick, unbroken streams of notes in the right hand, first as decoration, then as the main event.

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the harpsichord from the start. At first it is just chords in the background; listen for the moment when you realise it has become the leading voice.
2. The cadenza lasts about three minutes. In Bach's time a concerto movement rarely let one player go on alone for so long.

### II. Affettuoso

**Summary.** "Tender, with feeling." The string band is silent: only the three soloists play, like a trio in a small room. The flute, the violin and the harpsichord's right hand each sing the same sighing tune in turn, while the harpsichord's left hand plays the bass.

**Main ideas**

- The sighing tune: a slow, falling melody in B minor, with small dotted rhythms.
- Imitation: whatever one instrument sings, another repeats a moment later.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Bach wrote this movement for three players but four lines of music: the harpsichordist plays a melody with one hand and the bass with the other.

### III. Allegro

**Summary.** A light, skipping finale. It begins like a [[fugue]]: one soloist plays the tune alone, and the others enter one by one with the same tune. A smoother middle section in B minor follows, and then the whole opening section comes back unchanged.

**Main ideas**

- The skipping tune: quick and dance-like, with a lilting, bouncing rhythm.
- The middle section: a longer-breathed melody in B minor, more singing than dancing.

**Things to notice**

1. The last part is an exact repeat of the first. Listen for whether the players change anything the second time.

**In this recording.** Trevor Pinnock is both the harpsichord soloist and the director, so the cadenza is played by the person leading the performance. Lisa Beznosiuk plays a wooden Baroque flute, softer than a modern metal one, which keeps the balance with the violin and the harpsichord.

### Threads

- **The harpsichord's rise.** It starts in the background, takes over the first movement, shares the second as an equal, and in the third enters the fugue as one of the three voices.
- **One small band.** The orchestra is only a handful of strings. In the slow movement even they stop, and the concerto becomes chamber music.
- **Imitation.** Everywhere one instrument says something, another repeats it: the duets of the first movement, the sighs of the second, the entries of the third.
