### The big picture

- Schubert wrote it in Vienna in 1827, the year of *Winterreise*; his title page reads "Nov. 1827, Op. 100". He was thirty and had a year to live. It is the second of two large piano trios from those months, darker and more dramatic than the first, in B-flat.
- The slow movement grew from a Swedish song, *Se solen sjunker* ("See, the sun is sinking"), which Schubert heard sung by the Swedish tenor Isak Albert Berg at the home of his friends the Fröhlich sisters.
- Its official premiere was the centrepiece of the only public concert Schubert ever gave of his own music, on 26 March 1828, the first anniversary of Beethoven's death, in the hall of Vienna's Musikverein. The players were Carl Maria von Bocklet (piano), Joseph Michael Böhm (violin) and Joseph Linke (cello). The concert was a success, but the press barely noticed: Paganini's first Vienna concert, three days later, swept everything else aside.
- Before sending it to the Leipzig publisher Probst, Schubert cut about a hundred bars from the finale and its repeat, and asked that "the cuts marked in the last piece are to be most strictly observed". The trio appeared in late 1828, just after his death in November; he probably never saw a printed copy. Asked whom it should be dedicated to, he replied: to no one, except those who find pleasure in it.
- In one line: a bold opening on a large scale (I), a slow, walking lament (II), a light dance in canon (III), a long, restless finale where the slow movement's song comes back (IV).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** A large [[sonata form]] movement that starts with a bold call from all three players together. Schubert introduces three themes in turn, the second making a surprising journey to a distant key. Most of the development is built on a small, tripping figure, and the movement fades out with that figure heard softly, as if from far away.

**Main ideas**

- The opening call: all three play a strong, rising phrase together, in [[unison]].
- Theme 2: a quiet, nodding tune that starts in E-flat minor and slides, almost by magic, into B minor.
- The tripping figure: one longer note and four quick ones, a smaller cousin of the opening call, tossed between the instruments.

### II. Andante con moto

**Summary.** Over a steady, walking accompaniment in the piano, the cello sings a sad tune in C minor taken from a Swedish song about the sunset. The tune passes from instrument to instrument, brightens for a while, then breaks out in a passionate climax before returning, quieter, at the end.

**Main ideas**

- The walk: short, detached chords in the piano, like footsteps.
- The song: a long, sad melody in the cello, with a falling octave like a farewell; the violin and the piano take it up later.
- The climax: the same song, loud and agitated, with the piano in full force.

**On film.** This movement is one of the central themes of Stanley Kubrick's *Barry Lyndon* (1975), and has been used in many films since.

### III. Scherzando. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** A light, dancing movement in three beats. It begins as a [[canon]]: the piano plays a tune and the strings follow it a bar later. The middle section ([[trio]]) is heavier, with stamping accents. Schubert asked for the outer part to be kept at a moderate tempo and quiet, and the middle to be forceful.

**Main ideas**

- The canon: a neat, bouncing tune in the piano, copied by the violin and cello one bar behind.
- The trio: a heavier, stamping dance with sharp accents.

### IV. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** A long finale that mixes [[sonata form]] with the returning theme of a [[rondo]]. A plain, cheerful tune alternates with a second idea in a different metre, full of rapid repeated notes like a cimbalom. Twice the slow movement's song returns, the second time over those repeated notes, before the trio ends in a bright E-flat major.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a simple, skipping tune in the piano, almost like a street song.
- Theme 2: the beat changes from a lilting six to a firm two, and the violin, then the piano in octaves, plays rapid repeated notes, like a cimbalom.
- The song from II: the cello's sad melody, back in a new setting.

**In this recording.** The Beaux Arts Trio play the shorter version Schubert sent to the publisher, as most recordings do. His manuscript has the longer finale, with the repeat and two more passages, one of which combines the slow movement's song with the finale's own themes.

### Threads

- **The song that returns.** The Swedish sunset song of the slow movement comes back twice in the finale, an early example of [[cyclic form]]. Bringing an earlier theme back in a later movement was still unusual in 1827.
- **Symphonic size.** At over forty minutes, the trio is as long as many symphonies of its time, and Schubert fills it with orchestral contrasts: unison outbursts, hushed passages, long crescendos.
- **Schubert and Beethoven.** He chose the anniversary of Beethoven's death for the concert where the trio was first played in public. Beethoven, who had died the year before, was the composer he most revered.
