### The big picture

- In 1939 the Philadelphia businessman Samuel Fels commissioned a concerto from Barber for his ward, the young violinist Iso Briselli. Barber began it in Switzerland that summer; when war threatened at the end of August he sailed home and finished it in Pennsylvania.
- The first two movements are lyrical rather than showy. The accounts differ about what Briselli thought of them, but the fast finale was not what he had hoped for: according to Barber, he found it unidiomatic and slight beside the other two. Barber refused to replace it, and Briselli gave up the concerto.
- To show that the finale was playable, Barber had a Curtis Institute student, Herbert Baumel, try it; Baumel gave a private performance in early 1940. The public premiere was in February 1941, with Albert Spalding and the Philadelphia Orchestra under Eugene Ormandy.
- It soon became one of the most often played concertos of the 20th century. The orchestra is small, with a piano among the instruments, and the violin sings far more than it dazzles, until the last four minutes.
- In one line: a long, warm song for the violin (I), an oboe melody at dusk (II), a four-minute dash in [[perpetuum mobile]] (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** There is no orchestral introduction: the violin begins at once with a long, lyrical melody. Barber said the movement is closer to a sonata than to a concerto: the soloist and orchestra share the ideas instead of competing. A second, perkier tune brings contrast, and the movement ends calmly.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a broad, singing melody for the solo violin, with a little snap in its rhythm.
- Theme 2: a short, folk-like tune, simple and a little cheeky.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |

| ≈ 0:50 | The orchestra takes up the melody; the violin weaves around it | The theme passes to the orchestra |
| ≈ 1:40 | A quicker, jaunty little tune with a skipping rhythm | Theme 2 |
| ≈ 2:30–4:00 | The violin takes up both ideas; the piano adds a dry, bright colour to the orchestra | The [[exposition]] completes |

| Last minute | The violin climbs softly; the movement settles on quiet, pulsing chords | A calm ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the first seconds: the soloist has the main tune before the orchestra has said anything.
2. Listen for the piano inside the orchestra. It is used as a colour, not as a soloist.

**In this recording.** Gil Shaham recorded the concerto with André Previn and the London Symphony Orchestra in June 1993 at Henry Wood Hall, London. On the Spotify album it is followed by Korngold's Violin Concerto.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow movement that opens with a long oboe solo. The violin enters with a different, more rhapsodic theme, grows passionate, and later takes over the oboe's melody. Barber described the plan himself in his note for the premiere.

**Main ideas**

- The oboe melody: long, sad and floating, over soft strings.
- The violin theme: freer and more searching, rising to a passionate climax.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft strings, then a solo oboe with a long, wistful melody | The oboe theme. The soloist waits |
| ≈ 1:50 | The violin enters with a new, freer melody | The violin's own theme |
| ≈ 3:30–5:30 | The violin climbs high, the orchestra swells, the music becomes intense | The climax of the movement |
| ≈ 6:00 | The violin plays the oboe's opening melody | The opening theme returns, now on the violin |
| Last minute | Very soft; the violin holds high, quiet notes as the music fades | A hushed ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the oboe at the start with the violin near the end. It is the same melody in a different voice.
2. The violin waits almost two minutes before it plays. Notice how its entrance changes the mood.

### III. Presto in moto perpetuo

**Summary.** "Perpetual motion": after a few bars of drumming, the violin plays fast, even notes in groups of three almost without a pause to the very end. The orchestra jabs in with off-beat chords. It lasts under four minutes and ends with a burst of speed.

**Main ideas**

- The drive: rapid triplets in the violin, hardly ever interrupted.
- The jabs: sharp, off-beat chords and brass accents in the orchestra, full of [[syncopation]].

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The timpani alone, a quick, rattling rhythm; then the violin dashes in | The start of the [[perpetuum mobile]] |
| ≈ 0:15–2:00 | The violin races without a break; the orchestra answers with short, jagged chords | The violin's nonstop line against the orchestra's accents |
| ≈ 2:00–3:20 | The music grows wilder; the violin leaps around its whole range, brass and snare drum push it on | The tension rises |
| Last 25 seconds | The violin's notes get faster still; brass punch out off-beat chords; a sudden end | A breathless close |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to hear where the violin stops to breathe. It almost never does.
2. Listen to the orchestra's accents: they fall in unexpected places, against the violin's even notes.

### Threads

- **Song before display.** Two movements of long melodies, then one of pure speed. The contrast is the shape of the work, and the reason for the quarrel over the finale.
- **The finale question.** Briselli, by his own account, had asked for a more virtuosic finale; by Barber's, he then found it slight. Barber kept it, and it is now the part audiences remember.
- **A small orchestra.** Pairs of woodwind, horns and trumpets, timpani, snare drum, piano and strings: Barber never covers the violin, and the oboe gets a long solo of its own.
