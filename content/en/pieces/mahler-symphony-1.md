### The big picture

- Mahler wrote the symphony mainly between late 1887 and March 1888 in Leipzig, where he was second conductor at the opera. He was 27, and far better known as a conductor than as a composer.
- He borrowed from his own songs. The main theme of the first movement is the tune of "Ging heut' Morgen übers Feld" ("I walked across the fields this morning"), and the slow movement quotes another song from the same cycle, the *Songs of a Wayfarer*.
- The first performance, in Budapest on 20 November 1889, presented it as a "symphonic poem" in two parts, and it was not well received. For Hamburg in 1893 Mahler added a programme and the title *Titan*, after a novel by Jean Paul. Later he dropped the programme, the title and an extra movement, *Blumine*. The four-movement symphony was published in 1899.
- The third movement turns the children's round "Frère Jacques" (in Austria "Bruder Martin") into a funeral march in the minor. Mahler once titled it a funeral march "in the manner of Callot", after the French printmaker of grotesque scenes, and the idea behind it is a hunter's funeral with the animals of the forest following the coffin.
- In one line: nature waking at dawn and a walk in the fields (I), a stamping country dance (II), a mock funeral (III), a storm that fights its way to victory (IV).

### I. Langsam, schleppend – Immer sehr gemächlich

**Summary.** The symphony opens with a single note, A, held by the strings across seven octaves, the highest violins playing ghostly [[harmonics]]. Out of this haze come sounds of nature: a falling two-note call, distant fanfares, a cuckoo. Then the cellos start a cheerful walking tune, borrowed from Mahler's song about crossing the fields one morning. The movement is a loose [[sonata form]], and at the end it bursts into D major.

**Main ideas**

- The falling call: two notes a fourth apart, first in the woodwinds and later in a clarinet as a cuckoo. It returns in every movement.
- The fanfares: military calls, first in the clarinets, then trumpets placed far away.
- The walking song: a carefree tune in the cellos, from "Ging heut' Morgen übers Feld".

1. Listen to how long it takes for anything like a tune to arrive. The first three minutes are only a held note and scattered calls, as if the music were waking up.
2. The two-note falling call is everywhere: in the introduction, in the cuckoo, and as the first two notes of the walking song.

**In this recording.** Kubelík recorded the symphony in Munich in 1967, as part of his complete Mahler cycle with the Bavarian Radio Symphony Orchestra for Deutsche Grammophon. On the Spotify album the symphony is tracks 1–4, followed by the *Songs of a Wayfarer* sung by Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau, the cycle the symphony borrows from.

### II. Kräftig bewegt, doch nicht zu schnell

**Summary.** A robust, stamping country dance, a [[Ländler]], in three beats. The low strings thump out the rhythm, the tune swaggers, the horns whoop. In the middle, the [[trio]] is softer and more graceful, a gentle sway in the strings. Then the stamping dance returns, shorter and louder.

**Main ideas**

- The Ländler: a heavy-footed tune over a thumping bass, with whooping horns and shrill clarinets.
- The trio: a slower, swaying melody for the strings, with a touch of Viennese charm.

### III. Feierlich und gemessen, ohne zu schleppen

**Summary.** A funeral march made from a nursery round. A timpani taps out two notes, and a solo double bass, high and strained, plays "Bruder Martin" in the minor. The other instruments join one by one, as in a [[canon]]. The procession is interrupted by a village band, vulgar and tearful, and then by a dream: a tender melody from Mahler's own song about a linden tree. The march returns, and fades away.

**Main ideas**

- The round: "Frère Jacques", made sad and slow, in the minor.
- The band: a cheap, sobbing tune for oboes, clarinets and trumpets, with cymbal and bass drum.
- The dream: a soft melody for the strings, from the song "Die zwei blauen Augen" ("The two blue eyes").

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. The solo double bass is a deliberately odd choice: it plays high in its range, where it sounds thin and uneasy. Mahler wanted a funeral march that is both sad and absurd.
2. In the dream section the words of the song, which you do not hear, speak of lying under a linden tree, where all was well again.

### IV. Stürmisch bewegt

**Summary.** The finale begins with a cymbal crash and a scream from the whole orchestra, straight out of the quiet end of the funeral march. A fierce theme in F minor storms through the brass; a long, tender second theme in the strings offers relief. The struggle goes on, twice reaching for D major, and memories of the first movement return. In the end the brass win through with a [[chorale]], and the symphony closes in triumph.

**Main ideas**

- The storm theme: a fierce, jagged melody in the brass, in F minor.
- The love theme: a broad, warm melody in the violins, in D-flat major.
- The chorale: a solemn, hymn-like phrase in the brass, which becomes the symphony's victory song.

**Things to notice**

1. The first move to D major, in the middle of the movement, collapses. Notice how much harder the music has to work to reach it the second time.
2. In most performances all seven horn players stand up for the final chorale, so that they ring out above the whole orchestra.

### Threads

- **The falling call.** The two-note fall of the opening is in the cuckoo, in the walking song, in the Ländler's bass, in the timpani of the funeral march and in the finale's themes. It ties the symphony together.
- **Songs inside a symphony.** Two of Mahler's *Songs of a Wayfarer* are woven in: the morning walk in the first movement and the linden-tree dream in the third. The words are absent, but the tunes carry their meaning.
- **From dawn to triumph.** The symphony moves from a held note at first light, through a dance and a mock funeral, to a hard-won victory. Mahler's discarded programme called the finale "From Hell to Paradise".
