### The big picture

- A partita, for Bach, is a [[suite]]: a chain of dances in one key, opened by a free prelude. He published his six keyboard partitas one by one from 1726, then together in 1731 as the first part of his *Clavier-Übung*, "keyboard practice", "for music lovers, to refresh their spirits". He called the volume his Opus 1: it was the first of his works he published himself.
- The Sixth Partita is the last of the set and one of the two largest, with the Fourth. An early version of it stands near the beginning of the music notebook he began for his wife, Anna Magdalena, in 1725, just after the Third Partita.
- It opens with a [[toccata]], a "touch piece" meant to show off the player. Bach's is weighty rather than brilliant: a free opening, a long three-voice [[fugue]], and the opening again. Then come the dances: [[allemande]], [[courante]] (here *Corrente*), a short Air, [[sarabande]], a [[gavotte]]-like *Tempo di Gavotta* and a [[gigue]] written as a fugue.
- This recording uses the harpsichord, one of the keyboard instruments Bach wrote for. It has no [[sustain pedal]] and cannot play louder or softer by touch: the player shapes the music by choosing between its two keyboards and their sets of strings, by spreading chords, by ornaments and by timing.
- In one line: a toccata with a fugue inside it (I), two richly ornamented dances (II–III), a lively interlude (IV), the slow heart of the work (V), a quick gavotte, and a jig in fugue whose theme turns upside down halfway (VI–VII).

### I. Toccata

**Summary.** The longest movement and the gateway to the partita. A free, declamatory opening, full of runs and full chords; then a long, grave fugue in three voices; then the opening music returns to close the frame.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: rolling chords and sweeping runs over a slow bass, like an improvisation.
- The sigh: a three-note figure, a clashing note that falls to rest, first heard in the opening and then built into the fugue's theme.
- The fugue theme: slow, serious, beginning with that sigh.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:45 | The runs settle into a pattern, with a falling, sighing figure repeated | The sigh motif that will feed the fugue |
| ≈ 1:35 | A single line begins a solemn theme; a second voice enters, then a third | The fugue in three voices |
| ≈ 3:30 | The theme passes through new keys; the texture thickens and the sighs multiply | The fugue's middle and its long final build |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for how the harpsichord makes a big sound without a pedal: chords are spread from the bottom note upwards, and quick notes fill the space between them, so the instrument keeps ringing.

**In this recording.** Igor Kipnis (1930–2002), son of the bass Alexander Kipnis, was one of the best-known American harpsichordists. His two albums of the six partitas first appeared in 1977, when the magazine *High Fidelity* called the playing "glitteringly bright" and "robustly sonorous"; they were reissued on CD in 2000, which is the edition on Spotify. On this album the Sixth Partita is tracks 15–21, after the Third and Fifth. A reviewer of the reissue singled out this Toccata as among the finest on record.

### V. Sarabande

**Summary.** The slow heart of the partita, and one of the most intense sarabandes Bach wrote. The melody is so densely ornamented that the ornaments become the music: broken chords, turns and trills carry the feeling.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: slow, searching, constantly decorated, over chords that lean on the second beat.
- The harmony: unexpected, aching chords that pull the music away from home.

### VII. Gigue

**Summary.** The finale is a jig written as a [[fugue]]: an angular, jagged theme enters in three voices. In the second half Bach turns the theme upside down, so that where it rose it now falls, and builds a second fugue from it.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: angular, with sharp dotted rhythms and wide leaps, clashing with the line set against it.
- The inversion: the same theme, mirrored.

### Threads

- **The sigh.** The three-note falling figure heard in the Toccata's opening launches the theme of its fugue and comes back again and again as the fugue drives to its end. Listen for falling, sighing steps in the slow dances too.
- **Fugue as frame.** The partita begins and ends with fugues: one inside the Toccata, one disguised as a jig. Between them stand the dances.
- **The harpsichord's art.** No pedal and no touch dynamics: everything the listener hears as loud, soft, sustained or singing comes from registration, spread chords, ornaments and timing.
