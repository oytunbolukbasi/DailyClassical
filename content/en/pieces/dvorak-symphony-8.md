### The big picture

- Dvořák wrote the symphony between 26 August and 8 November 1889, mostly at Vysoká, his summer house in the Bohemian countryside south-west of Prague. It was the place where he was most at ease, and the music is full of open air.
- He had just been elected to the new Bohemian Academy of Sciences, Literature and Arts, and dedicated the score to it "in thanks for my election". He wanted a symphony "different from the other symphonies", with ideas "worked out in a new way". His Seventh, four years earlier, had been dark and stormy; this one is sunny.
- He conducted the first performance in Prague on 2 February 1890, and the London premiere that April. He wrote to a friend that the applause in London grew after every movement, until it was "almost unbearable".
- It was published in London by Novello, not by his usual publisher Simrock, after the two quarrelled over the fee. That is why it is sometimes called the "English" symphony, although nothing in it is English.
- In one line: a hymn and a birdcall in the open fields (I), a slow movement of light and shadow (II), a wistful waltz (III), trumpets and a set of variations that ends in a whirl (IV).

### I. Allegro con brio

**Summary.** The symphony is in G major, but it begins in G minor, with a warm, hymn-like melody for cellos and low winds. Then a flute takes off on a bright birdcall, and the major key arrives. That opening melody comes back like a signpost at the start of each new section: Dvořák builds a [[sonata form]] around it rather than around one main theme.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: a broad, songful G minor melody in cellos, clarinets, bassoons and horn, seventeen bars long, which drifts through other keys before landing on G major.
- The birdcall: a light, chirping tune in the flute, rising and falling over soft strings.
- The second theme: a dotted, dancing tune in the woodwinds, in B minor, that soon swells into a bright B major tutti.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the hymn each time it returns: at the start, at the start of the development, and at the climax in the trumpets. It tells you where you are in the movement.
2. The cor anglais plays only a few bars in the whole symphony. Wait for it in the recapitulation: the birdcall sounds darker and more tender in its low, reedy voice.

**In this recording.** Kubelík, born in Bohemia, recorded the symphony with the Berlin Philharmonic in 1966 as part of his complete Dvořák cycle for Deutsche Grammophon. He is reported to have told an orchestra that in Bohemia the trumpets never call to battle: they call to the dance. On the Spotify album the symphony is tracks 1–4, followed by the Ninth, "From the New World".

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A slow movement that keeps changing its mind. It opens in C minor with a hesitant phrase in the strings, answered by birdlike calls in the woodwinds. Then the sun comes out: a C major melody for flute and oboe over gently falling violin scales, taken up by a solo violin and then by the whole orchestra with brass fanfares. The dark music returns, a storm breaks out, and the movement ends in calm.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a soft, halting string phrase that stops and starts, as if unsure where to go.
- The birdcalls: short falling figures in the flutes and oboes, answered by the clarinets.
- The C major tune: a simple, swaying melody in flute and oboe over scales that trickle down in the violins.

1. Many listeners hear a summer day in this movement, with birds, sunshine and a passing thunderstorm. Dvořák left no programme, so it is only one way to hear it, but the woodwind calls really do sound like birds.

### III. Allegretto grazioso – Molto vivace

**Summary.** In place of a fast, joking [[scherzo]], Dvořák writes a waltz: graceful, a little melancholy, in G minor. The middle section is a brighter folk-like tune in G major that he borrowed from his own comic opera *The Stubborn Lovers* (1874). At the end the tempo changes from three beats to two, and the borrowed tune rushes past in a quick dance.

**Main ideas**

- The waltz: a sighing, gliding melody in the violins, in three beats.
- The trio tune: a simple, swinging G major melody for flutes and oboes, later for oboe and bassoon.

### IV. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** A trumpet fanfare, a silence, and then a quiet tune in the cellos. The rest of the movement is a set of [[variation]]s on that tune, some fast, some slow, one in the minor like a stamping village band. The fanfare returns halfway through, a slow, tender variation follows, and the symphony ends in a blaze of brass and timpani.

**Main ideas**

- The fanfare: two trumpets, bright and ceremonial.
- The theme: a gentle, rocking tune in the cellos, which will be varied again and again.
- The village band: a march-like section in C minor, with stamping rhythms and shrill woodwinds.

### Threads

- **Nature and birds.** The flute's birdcall in the first movement, the woodwind calls in the Adagio and the flute's flight in the finale: the countryside around Vysoká is heard throughout.
- **Signposts.** The hymn of the first movement and the fanfare of the finale both come back to mark the start of a new section. Dvořák uses them to guide the ear through forms he treated freely.
- **Song and dance.** Almost every theme could be sung, and many could be danced: the waltz, the borrowed opera tune, the village band. Dvořák's love of Czech folk music shapes the whole symphony, though he quotes no actual folk song.
