### The big picture

- Antonio Salieri was the leading musician of the Habsburg court in Vienna: Hofkapellmeister, in charge of the music of the imperial chapel, from 1788 to 1824, longer than anyone before or since. In 1804 he wrote a [[requiem]], the Catholic Mass for the dead, to be performed at his own funeral. At the head of the score he wrote: "Picciolo Requiem composto da me, e per me, Ant. Salieri, piccolissima creatura" ("A little Requiem, composed by me, and for me, Antonio Salieri, the smallest of creatures"). Why he wrote it then is not known; deaths in his family, the Requiem his teacher Florian Gassmann left unfinished, a new Requiem by Joseph Eybler (1803) and the first printed edition of Mozart's (1800) may all have played a part.
- The work then waited 21 years. Salieri died in Vienna on 7 May 1825, and on 22 June his Requiem was heard for the first time, at the memorial Mass in the Minoritenkirche, the church of Vienna's Italian community. The court musicians sang and played it under Joseph Eybler, his successor as Hofkapellmeister, the same Eybler who had once begun to complete Mozart's Requiem.
- The music is quiet and devout rather than theatrical, although Salieri was above all an opera composer. The choir carries almost everything, mostly in chords, all voices moving together; the soloists sing only as a group of four. The text is gathered into seven large movements, and the long poem of the Last Judgment, the Dies irae, runs as one continuous movement of about fifteen minutes. The orchestra is the court chapel's: strings, oboes, bassoons, trumpets, three trombones, timpani and organ, with a [[cor anglais]] whose plaintive voice opens the work.
- Today Salieri is best known through a legend: that he envied Mozart and poisoned him. The rumour began decades after Mozart's death in 1791, and there is no evidence for it; Mozart's symptoms do not point to poison. Pushkin's short play *Mozart and Salieri* (1831), Rimsky-Korsakov's opera on it (1898) and Peter Shaffer's *Amadeus* (1979, filmed in 1984) made it famous. The two men were rivals for posts and commissions, but in October 1791 Mozart drove Salieri to *The Magic Flute* and wrote to his wife that there was not a number that did not draw a "Bravo!" from him.
- In one line: eternal rest and mercy (I), the Day of Judgment in one long movement (II), the Offertory (III), Sanctus and Benedictus (IV–V), Agnus Dei and Communion (VI), and the prayer sung at the coffin (VII).

### I. Introitus and Kyrie

**Summary.** "Grant them eternal rest, Lord, and let perpetual light shine on them." The Requiem opens very quietly, with the orchestra alone and a long, sad solo for the cor anglais; then the choir's basses begin the prayer, and the whole choir answers. The Kyrie ("Lord, have mercy") follows without a break, a little quicker, and the movement ends slowly again.

**Main ideas**

- The cor anglais: a low, plaintive oboe voice singing long Italian lines over strings.
- The plea: soft choral phrases in a gently rocking rhythm, then the full choir at full voice.

### II. Sequence: Dies irae

**Summary.** "Day of wrath, that day will dissolve the world in ashes." The whole medieval poem of the Last Judgment, set as one continuous movement in six sections at different speeds. Salieri's Day of Judgment is stately rather than violent: the brass blaze in the "Tuba mirum" and the "Rex tremendae", but the Confutatis, where Mozart has flames, is calm and resigned. The four soloists sing for the first time here.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: choral cries of "Dies irae!" over strings and bassoons, then trumpets, trombones and timpani.
- The Recordare: the soloists as a quartet, taking turns with the choir.
- The Lacrimosa: weighty and grand, more a warning than a lament.

**Things to notice**

1. Salieri sets the whole poem as one movement, almost a quarter of an hour of music. Notice how often the speed changes: each new verse brings a new pace and a new colour.
2. At the "Confutatis", about ten and a half minutes in, compare it with Mozart's: no fire for the damned here, only a quiet acceptance.

### III. Offertory: Domine Jesu and Hostias

**Summary.** "Lord Jesus Christ, King of glory, free the souls of all the faithful departed from the pains of hell." The Offertory opens in a stately but moving tempo, turns quick for a choral [[fugue]] on God's promise to Abraham, and ends with the calm prayer "Hostias".

**Main ideas**

- The opening: firm, measured choral writing.
- The fugue "Quam olim Abrahae": voices entering one after another on a lively theme.

**Main ideas**

- The Sanctus: broad, repeated chords, Largo e maestoso.
- The Osanna: a nimble fugue, Allegretto non molto.

**Summary.** "Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world, grant them rest." A forceful, slow opening softens into quiet pleas for rest. The Communion follows, "Let eternal light shine on them, Lord, with your saints for ever", quicker, with a fugue led by the basses and a final rise in volume on "for you are merciful".

**Main ideas**

- The Agnus Dei: strong calls, then soft answers.
- The fugue "Cum sanctis tuis": a rolling theme that starts in the basses.

### VII. Libera me

**Summary.** "Deliver me, Lord, from eternal death on that fearful day, when the heavens and the earth shall be moved." The Libera me is not part of the Mass itself: it is a [[responsory]] sung beside the coffin after the Requiem, before the burial. Salieri set it as a quiet, simple prayer, with the soloists at its centre, and ends his own funeral music almost abruptly.

**Main ideas**

- The prayer: calm, steady choral writing.
- The soloists' "Requiem aeternam": plain and song-like.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The choir, quiet and steady | "Libera me, Domine, de morte aeterna" ("Deliver me, Lord, from eternal death") |
| ≈ 1:30–2:30 | The four soloists, in a simple, song-like tune, answered by the choir | "Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine, et lux perpetua luceat eis" |
| Last 30 seconds | Very quiet: the choir almost alone, its lines doubled by a few winds; then the music simply stops | "Quando caeli movendi sunt et terra" ("when the heavens and the earth shall be moved") |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the very end. There is no grand final chord: the work Salieri wrote for his own funeral closes quietly, almost in mid-thought.

### Threads

- **For himself.** "Composed by me, and for me": Salieri wrote the Requiem as an act of personal devotion and meant it for his own funeral; it waited 21 years to be sung. Its quiet start and its abrupt, quiet end fit that purpose.
- **The choir first.** The choir sings nearly every word, mostly in [[homophony]], all voices in the same rhythm so the text is clear. The soloists appear only in the Recordare, the Benedictus and the Libera me, and always as a quartet. Short fugues mark the Offertory, the two Osannas and the Communio.
- **The court orchestra.** Oboes, bassoons, trumpets, three trombones, timpani, strings and organ, the usual forces of the Viennese court chapel, with the cor anglais added for its sad colour. The brass flare up in the Dies irae; elsewhere the orchestra mostly supports the voices.
