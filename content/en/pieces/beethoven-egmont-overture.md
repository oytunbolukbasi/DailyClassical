### The big picture

- In 1810 the court theatre in Vienna, the Burgtheater, revived *Egmont*, the tragedy Johann Wolfgang von Goethe finished in 1787 and published in 1788, and asked Beethoven for the music: an overture and nine numbers for songs, interludes and the final scene. He had begun sketching it by the autumn of 1809. It was not ready for the opening night on 24 May 1810; his music was first heard at the fourth performance, on 15 June.
- The play is about a real man. Lamoral, Count of Egmont, a nobleman of the Low Countries, stood up against the harsh rule of Spain. The Duke of Alba, sent by King Philip II, had him arrested, and he was beheaded in the main square of Brussels on 5 June 1568. His death helped start the revolt that led to the independence of the Netherlands.
- In Goethe's play Egmont goes to his death unbroken, and the drama ends with what Goethe calls a "victory symphony". Beethoven, who revered Goethe, wrote that he had composed the music "purely out of love for the poet" and took no fee from the theatre.
- The overture soon led a life of its own. Breitkopf & Härtel printed it separately in December 1810, well before the rest of the music, and it has been a concert favourite ever since.
- In one line: a slow, heavy dance of oppression, a fierce fight in quick triple time, a sudden death, a hush, and a victory march in the major.

### I. Sostenuto, ma non troppo – Allegro – Allegro con brio

**Summary.** One movement in three parts. A slow introduction sets heavy, stamping chords against sad woodwind sighs. A fast section in [[sonata form]] tells of struggle. Then, after a single crushing blow and a silence, the music turns from F minor to F major and ends in a brilliant victory march.

**Main ideas**

- The tread: loud, heavy string chords with the weight on the second beat, the rhythm of a slow [[sarabande]], a dance of Spanish origin. It is often heard as the power of Spain.
- The sighs: quiet, falling phrases in oboe, clarinet and bassoon answering the chords, like voices of complaint.
- Theme 1 of the Allegro: a falling, restless line that starts in the cellos and is passed up to the violins.
- The victory music: a quiet, rustling figure in the strings that grows into fanfares for horns and trumpets, with the shrill piccolo on top.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the silence after the violent stroke near the end. Everything stops, and then the soft woodwind chords begin, as if the music were holding its breath.
2. Compare the opening chords with Theme 2 of the Allegro. Theme 2 is the same heavy dance rhythm, only faster.

**In this recording.** George Szell made the Cleveland Orchestra famous for its precision. He recorded the overture in Severance Hall in October 1966, during the years in which he also recorded all nine Beethoven symphonies with the orchestra.

### Threads

- **Freedom.** Beethoven wrote the music in 1809–10, the year Napoleon's army occupied Vienna. A play about a man who dies resisting a foreign power had a clear meaning for its audience.
- **Death turned into victory.** The overture ends in the major and at full force: the hero is dead, but his cause wins. Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, finished two years earlier, also moves from struggle in a minor key to triumph in the major.
- **Goethe.** Beethoven asked his publisher to send Goethe the score and wrote to the poet himself in April 1811. The two men met in the summer of 1812 at the spa town of Teplitz.
