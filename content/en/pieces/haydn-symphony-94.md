### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the symphony in London in 1791, on the first of his two visits to England. It was first played on 23 March 1792 at the Hanover Square Rooms, with Haydn directing from the keyboard. It is the second of the twelve symphonies he wrote for London.
- The famous surprise comes in the slow movement: a simple tune is played softly, then even more softly, and is cut off by a loud chord from the whole orchestra with a stroke on the timpani. In German the work is called the symphony "with the drumstroke".
- A popular story says Haydn wanted to wake up listeners who dozed through slow movements. Haydn's own explanation, given years later to his biographer Griesinger, was that he wanted to surprise the public with something new, so that his former pupil Ignaz Pleyel, whose rival concerts had begun a week earlier, would not outshine him.
- The premiere audience greeted the slow movement with great enthusiasm. Haydn later reused its tune in his oratorio *The Seasons* (1801), where a ploughman whistles it.
- In one line: a gentle introduction and a bustling dance (I), the surprise and its variations (II), a minuet too fast to dance (III), a racing finale with drums (IV).

### I. Adagio cantabile – Vivace assai

**Summary.** A short, gentle slow introduction leads into a quick movement in [[sonata form]] with a skipping, dancing rhythm. The fast music starts almost in a whisper before the whole orchestra bursts in.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: slow, singing phrases passed between woodwinds and strings.
- Theme 1: a quick, light tune in a rocking six-beat rhythm that starts quietly in the strings.
- Theme 2: a graceful, lilting tune in a new key.

**Things to notice**

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A simple tune and four variations on it: in each [[variation]] the tune stays, but something around it changes. Everyone knows what is coming; the trick still works because the tune is so innocent and so quiet.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a simple, nursery-rhyme tune in short, detached notes, first in the strings.
- The surprise: one loud chord for the whole orchestra, with the timpani.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The strings play a simple, detached tune quietly; the lower strings pluck | The theme |

**Things to notice**

1. The surprise happens only once. Listen to how Haydn keeps the later variations interesting without repeating the joke.
2. The coda sets the innocent tune against darker harmonies. It sounds thoughtful, almost wistful.

### IV. Finale: Allegro di molto

**Summary.** A fast, high-spirited finale that combines [[sonata form]] with a [[rondo]]: the opening tune keeps returning, even in the middle of the stormier passages. The timpani get the last word.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a quick, lightweight tune in the violins that seems to run on tiptoe.
- The drums: in the closing pages the timpani come to the front.

### Threads

- **Quiet before loud.** Haydn often starts softly so that the full orchestra lands with more force: in the first movement, in the slow movement and in the finale.
- **The timpani.** The drum stroke of the surprise returns as the leading voice at the end of the symphony.
- **Simple tunes.** Every movement is built on tunes that are easy to sing, which is why the surprises in harmony and volume are so clear.
