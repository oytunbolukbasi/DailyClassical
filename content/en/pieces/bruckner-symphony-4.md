### The big picture

- Bruckner wrote the symphony in 1874 and kept reworking it for more than a decade. In 1878 he replaced the scherzo with a new "hunt" scherzo, and in 1879–80 he composed a new finale. This revised version was first performed on 20 February 1881 in Vienna, conducted by Hans Richter, and it was a success. It was the first premiere of a Bruckner symphony that he did not conduct himself.
- The nickname "Romantic" is Bruckner's own. Later, in letters, he sketched a programme: in the first movement a horn announces the day from the town hall; the second theme is the song of a great tit; the scherzo is a hunt, and its trio a dance tune played during the hunters' meal in the forest. A friend passed on a fuller version, with a medieval city at daybreak, gates opening and knights riding out into the forest.
- Bruckner was an organist, and it shows: the orchestra often works in blocks, strings, woodwinds and brass taking turns like the stops of an organ. The horns lead almost everywhere.
- A rhythm runs through the symphony: two notes, then three in the same time. It is so typical of him that it is called the [[Bruckner rhythm]].
- In one line: daybreak and a horn call (I), a slow walk and a song (II), a hunt with a country dance in the middle (III), a vast finale that ends with the opening horn call (IV).

### I. Bewegt, nicht zu schnell

**Summary.** Out of a soft shimmer of strings, a single horn calls. That call is the seed of the movement, and of the whole symphony. Like most of Bruckner's first movements it is in [[sonata form]] with three groups of themes instead of two, laid out on a huge scale.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: a falling and rising fifth, played softly by one horn over trembling strings.
- The [[Bruckner rhythm]]: two plus three, hammered out by the full orchestra.
- The song theme: a lilting melody in D-flat major for the violas, with a little bird call in the violins above.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Count how often you hear the opening horn call. It returns in the woodwinds, in the brass hymn, in the recapitulation and at the end, and once more at the very end of the symphony.
2. Listen for the moments when the full orchestra stops dead and a few quiet instruments carry on alone. Organists change stops the same way.

**In this recording.** Karl Böhm recorded the symphony with the Vienna Philharmonic in the Sofiensaal in Vienna in November 1973. He plays the 1878–80 version in Leopold Nowak's edition. Decca first issued it on two LPs in 1974.

### II. Andante quasi allegretto

**Summary.** A slow walk in C minor, not a lament: Bruckner marks it "quasi allegretto", almost moving along. Two ideas take turns, a sombre song for the cellos and a long, warm melody for the violas over plucked strings. The movement rises to one great climax near the end and then fades away.

**Main ideas**

- The cello song: a sombre melody low in the strings, over a soft, steady tread.
- The viola melody: a long, singing line over [[pizzicato]] strings, like a serenade.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the violas when they take the melody. In most symphonies they accompany; here they have the most beautiful tune.

### III. Scherzo: Bewegt – Trio: Nicht zu schnell. Keinesfalls schleppend

**Summary.** Bruckner called it a hunt. Horn calls echo across the orchestra, and the full brass rings out like a hunting party in full cry. In the middle, the [[trio]] is a slow country dance, which Bruckner described as a tune played during the hunters' meal in the forest. Then the hunt starts again.

**Main ideas**

- The hunting calls: fanfares for the horns, then trumpets and trombones, over rushing strings.
- The dance: a gentle, swaying tune in G-flat major, in the style of an Austrian [[Ländler]].

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Rustling strings; the horns call to one another, softly and then louder | The hunt begins |
| ≈ 0:45 | The full brass rings out in a blazing fanfare | The first climax of the [[scherzo]] |
| ≈ 1:45 | The fanfares quieten and drift into softer, distant keys | A calmer middle passage |
| ≈ 3:15 | The calls return and build to a second blaze | The scherzo closes |
| ≈ 4:40 | Slower and gentle: a swaying dance tune in the woodwinds and strings | The [[trio]], a [[Ländler]] for the hunters' meal |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the horns in the scherzo with the dance in the trio. The same orchestra sounds first like an open forest, then like a village inn.

### IV. Finale: Bewegt, doch nicht zu schnell

**Summary.** The longest movement, and the most dramatic. It starts in darkness and works its way, through storms, quiet songs and solemn brass hymns, towards the light. At the end the horn call from the very beginning of the symphony comes back, and the symphony closes in a radiant E-flat major.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a huge, stark melody played by the whole orchestra in [[unison]].
- The song theme: a gentle melody for the strings, with bird-like woodwinds.
- The horn call from the first movement, which returns at the end.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A low, throbbing figure in the strings; horns recall the hunting calls of the scherzo | The introduction, building slowly |
| ≈ 1:50 | The whole orchestra hurls out a huge, stark theme together | The main theme, in [[unison]], the first great climax |
| ≈ 3:45 | Calm: the strings sing a gentle melody; woodwinds chirp around it | The second group, another "song period" |

**Things to notice**

1. In the last pages, listen for the horns. The call that opened the symphony comes back as the final word.

### Threads

- **The horn.** It opens the symphony alone, leads the hunt in the scherzo and closes the finale with the opening call. This is a symphony for the horn.
- **Two plus three.** The [[Bruckner rhythm]] stamps through the first movement and the finale, a signature as clear as a name.
- **Song and storm.** Every movement sets a broad, singing tune against massive blocks of brass, and Bruckner names the quiet sections "songs".
- **The long road.** Bruckner revised this symphony again and again between 1874 and 1888. What you hear is one stage of that journey, the version that made it a success.
