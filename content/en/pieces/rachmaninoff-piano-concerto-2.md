### The big picture

- In March 1897 the premiere of Rachmaninoff's First Symphony was a failure, savaged by the critics. He was 23, fell into depression and wrote almost nothing for three years.
- From January to April 1900 he saw the physician Nikolai Dahl almost daily for treatment that included hypnosis. He wrote the second and third movements that year, partly in Italy, and finished the first movement in April 1901. The concerto is dedicated to Dahl.
- He played the second and third movements in Moscow in December 1900. The complete concerto was first heard on 9 November 1901 in Moscow, with Rachmaninoff at the piano and his cousin Alexander Siloti conducting. It was a success, and it is still his most popular work.
- Piano and orchestra are partners here more than rivals. The piano often accompanies while the orchestra has the tune, and the first movement has no [[cadenza]]. Its melodies were later borrowed by the film *Brief Encounter* (1945) and by pop songs.
- In one line: bells and a dark, surging song (I), a still, glowing nocturne (II), a playful chase that ends in a blaze of C major (III).

### I. Moderato

**Summary.** The piano starts alone, like a bell slowly growing louder, and then the orchestra sweeps in with a dark Russian melody. The movement is in [[sonata form]], with two contrasting themes. It has no cadenza: the energy is held back for the moment when the opening theme returns as a march.

**Main ideas**

- The bells: slow piano chords, each answered by a deep note in the bass.
- Theme 1, dark and broad: a long melody in C minor for the strings, with the piano rolling underneath it.
- Theme 2, tender: a wide, lyrical melody in E-flat major, first sung by the piano alone.

**Things to notice**

1. In the opening chords, listen to the lowest note after each chord. It is the bell's deep stroke, and it grows with the chords.
2. When Theme 1 first appears, the piano is busy but does not have the tune. At the march it does not have it either: it pounds out its own rhythm against the orchestra.

**In this recording.** Richter recorded the concerto in Warsaw in April 1959, a year and a half before his first concerts in the United States. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–3, followed by Tchaikovsky's First Piano Concerto with Karajan.

### II. Adagio sostenuto

**Summary.** A slow movement in a warm E major, after the C minor of the first. A simple, songlike melody passes from flute to clarinet to piano, over a gently rocking accompaniment. In the middle the music becomes more animated, rises to a climax and a short piano cadenza, and then the melody comes back.

**Main ideas**

- The rocking figure: broken chords in the piano that never stop moving.
- The main theme: a long, slow, simple melody, first on the flute.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft, [[muted]] string chords that slowly shift; the dark colour melts into a warmer one | A short introduction that moves from C minor to E major |
| ≈ 0:30 | The piano enters alone with a gentle, rocking pattern of broken chords | The accompaniment that runs through most of the movement |
| ≈ 0:50 | Above the piano, a solo flute plays a long, slow, simple melody | The main theme |
| ≈ 1:30 | A solo clarinet takes over the melody and carries it further | The theme continues in a darker, warmer colour |
| ≈ 2:45 | The piano now sings the melody itself; the strings answer | Piano and orchestra swap roles |
| ≈ 4:30–6:30 | The music moves faster; quick, dancing figures in the piano, answered in turn by bassoon, horn and violas | The middle section, *Più animato* |
| ≈ 7:00–8:00 | A rush of fast notes builds to a loud climax; then the piano alone plays a brief, brilliant flourish | The climax, and a short [[cadenza]] |
| ≈ 8:30 | The violins sing the main theme; the piano returns to its rocking pattern | The main theme comes back |
| Last minute | The piano alone plays soft, rising chords; the movement ends quietly in E major | A calm [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the piano's rocking figure. It hardly stops for the whole movement, even when the melody moves elsewhere.
2. Compare the flute and the clarinet in the opening minutes. It is the same melody; only the colour changes.

### III. Allegro scherzando

**Summary.** After a quiet build-up the piano crashes in with a quick, jumpy theme. Its opposite is one of Rachmaninoff's most famous melodies, broad and longing. The two alternate, and at the end the longing melody returns in the full orchestra, now in C major.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, playful: a fast, jumpy tune in C minor, mostly for the piano. *Scherzando* means "playfully".
- Theme 2, the big tune: a long, warm, sad melody, first on oboe and violas together.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A quiet, quick march in the orchestra that keeps building | The introduction, moving from E major back towards C minor |
| ≈ 0:30 | The piano bursts in with a sweeping flourish | The piano's entry |
| ≈ 0:50 | A fast, jumpy, nervous tune in the piano, with crisp chords in the orchestra | Theme 1, in C minor |
| ≈ 2:00 | The music relaxes; oboe and violas play a long, warm, longing melody | Theme 2, in B-flat major. It later became the song "Full Moon and Empty Arms" (1945) |
| ≈ 2:45 | The piano takes up the melody in rich chords | Theme 2 on the piano |
| ≈ 4:00–6:00 | Theme 1 returns and is tossed between piano and orchestra; at one point the instruments enter one after another with the same quick tune | The development, with a short [[fugato]] |
| ≈ 7:00–8:00 | The warm melody returns, now in a different key, on oboe and strings and then the piano | Theme 2 comes back |
| ≈ 9:30 | Rising scales and a long build; the tension climbs | The approach to the climax |
| ≈ 10:15–10:45 | The whole orchestra plays the warm melody at full strength, now in C major; the piano crashes out chords | Theme 2 as the climax (*Maestoso*), played [[tutti]]. The minor has become major |

**Things to notice**

1. Theme 2 appears three times: on oboe and violas, then in a new key, then in C major for everyone. Notice how it becomes broader each time.
2. The concerto began in C minor with lonely piano chords. It ends in C major with piano and orchestra together.

### Threads

- **Darkness to light.** The concerto moves from C minor to C major. The last movement's big tune is the moment the minor finally turns major.
- **Bells.** The opening chords sound like Russian church bells. Rachmaninoff later wrote a whole choral symphony called *The Bells* (1913).
- **Partners, not rivals.** The piano often accompanies the orchestra instead of competing with it, and the big moments arrive when the two join forces.
- **Long melodies.** Each movement has a melody that keeps unfolding without seeming to stop for breath.
