### The big picture

- This is the overture to *Guillaume Tell*, Rossini's last opera, first performed at the Paris Opéra on 3 August 1829. He was thirty-seven and at the height of his fame, and he lived almost forty more years without writing another opera.
- The opera, in French, is based on Friedrich Schiller's play *Wilhelm Tell* (1804): the legend of the Swiss archer who led his people against Austrian rule. With about four hours of music it is rarely staged complete, and the overture has become far better known than the opera itself.
- It is not a medley of the opera's tunes but a set of four connected scenes from Alpine life: dawn, a storm, a shepherd's call, and a military gallop. Hector Berlioz, who usually disliked Rossini, called it "a symphony in four parts".
- The final gallop has had a long second life: it was the theme of *The Lone Ranger* on radio, television and film, and appears in countless cartoons and adverts. Liszt arranged the whole overture for piano in 1838.
- In one line: five cellos at dawn, a mountain storm, a shepherd's tune on the cor anglais, and a gallop led by trumpets, all without a break.

### I. Andante – Allegro – Andante – Allegro vivace

**Summary.** Four scenes played without a break. A slow dawn for five solo cellos and double basses; a storm for full orchestra that builds and fades; a calm pastoral scene where a cor anglais plays a Swiss herdsman's call; and a fast march heralded by trumpets, which ends the overture at full speed.

**Main ideas**

- Dawn: a quiet, singing melody for a solo cello, answered by four more cellos in rich harmony, with soft timpani rolls like distant thunder.
- The storm: rushing strings, short three-note cries from the woodwinds, then the full crash of brass and bass drum.
- The [[ranz des vaches]]: a lilting, open-air tune on the cor anglais, echoed by the flute, with a tinkling triangle.
- The gallop: a trumpet fanfare, then a racing tune in the strings in a quick two-beat rhythm, a [[galop]].

**Things to notice**

1. Count the cellos at the start. There are five solo parts, an unusual sound for an opera overture: the orchestra begins with its darkest voices.
2. Listen for the flute at the end of the storm (≈ 5:00). It is the last voice left, and it leads straight into the calm.

**In this recording.** Karajan recorded six Rossini overtures with the Berlin Philharmonic in 1971; he had recorded much the same programme in London with the Philharmonia years earlier. Here the whole overture lasts twelve minutes.

### Threads

- **Pictures without words.** Each part paints a scene: dawn, storm, pasture, march. It is [[programme music]] before the word was common.
- **Colour.** Rossini gives each scene its own instruments: cellos for the dawn, brass and drums for the storm, cor anglais and flute for the pasture, trumpets for the march.
- **A farewell.** This was Rossini's last opera. After it he wrote sacred music, songs and piano pieces, but never another work for the stage.
