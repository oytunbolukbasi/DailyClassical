### The big picture

- Prokofiev was one of the great pianists of his time, and he wrote this concerto to play himself. It is the most popular of his five piano concertos.
- It grew slowly. He wrote the theme of the second movement in 1913 and gathered more ideas over the next years, in Russia. He left Russia in 1918, and in the summer of 1921, staying on the coast of Brittany in France, he finally put the concerto together.
- He played the first performance on 16 December 1921 in Chicago, with the Chicago Symphony Orchestra conducted by Frederick Stock. Success came a year later, after a much-praised performance in Paris conducted by Serge Koussevitzky.
- The piano part is famously hard: fast runs, hammered chords, leaps across the keyboard. But the concerto is also full of tunes and jokes, and the orchestra is a real partner.
- In one line: a dreamy clarinet that launches a race (I), a theme with five very different variations (II), an argument between piano and orchestra that ends in a blaze of C major (III).

### I. Andante – Allegro

**Summary.** A quiet, folk-like melody on a solo clarinet, then a sudden race. The piano bursts in with brilliant, fast music, and a second theme brings a spiky, mocking dance. In the middle the clarinet's slow melody returns in the full orchestra; then the race starts again and drives to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The opening song: a calm, Russian-sounding melody on a solo clarinet.
- The race: fast, glittering runs in the piano, with the strings rushing alongside.
- The mocking dance: a spiky, sardonic tune, first in the oboe, then in the piano, with castanets.

### II. Tema con variazioni

**Summary.** A short, elegant theme in the rhythm of an old dance, the gavotte, followed by five [[variation]]s, each with its own character: dreamy, galloping, jazzy, mysterious and fierce. At the end the theme returns in the orchestra while the piano races around it, and the movement fades out quietly.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a poised, slightly ironic melody in the woodwinds, in the steps of a gavotte.
- The variations: the same theme, transformed each time.

**Things to notice**

1. Keep the opening theme in your head. In some variations it is easy to find; in others only its shape or rhythm remains.
2. In the final return of the theme, the orchestra and the piano play at two different speeds at once.

### III. Allegro, ma non troppo

**Summary.** Prokofiev called this movement an argument between soloist and orchestra. The bassoons start a dry, stalking tune; the piano interrupts with its own ideas. A long, lyrical middle section brings a broad, romantic melody. Then the argument resumes and builds to a thrilling finish in C major.

**Main ideas**

- The argument: a stalking tune for bassoons and [[pizzicato]] strings, and the piano's interruptions.
- The lyrical theme: a slow, yearning melody in the woodwinds, later sung by the full strings.

2. The lyrical theme is the concerto's broadest melody, almost like Rachmaninoff. Prokofiev turns it sarcastic before letting it bloom.

### Threads

- **Song and sparkle.** Each movement sets a singing melody (the clarinet, the gavotte, the lyrical middle section) against fast, glittering piano writing.
- **A pianist's concerto.** Prokofiev wrote it for his own technique: percussive chords, lightning runs and leaps. In 1932 he recorded it himself with the London Symphony Orchestra, his only recording of one of his own concertos.
- **Ending in C major.** The concerto starts with a quiet clarinet in C major and ends with a huge C major chord for everyone.
