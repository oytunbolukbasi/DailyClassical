### The big picture

- A piano trio is a work for violin, cello and piano. Mendelssohn had planned one for years before he wrote this one in 1839, at thirty, when he was music director of the Gewandhaus concerts in Leipzig. The first movement is dated 6 June and the whole score 18 July 1839.
- He kept working on it. After playing it privately with his friend the violinist Ferdinand David in August, he rewrote the whole piece. Then the composer Ferdinand Hiller, a friend of Chopin and Liszt, told him that some of the piano writing, built on broken chords, sounded old-fashioned. Mendelssohn rewrote the piano part again, in a richer and much harder style.
- The first public performance was at the Leipzig Gewandhaus on 1 February 1840, with David on violin, Carl Wittmann on cello and Mendelssohn at the piano. Breitkopf & Härtel published it that April.
- Robert Schumann reviewed it as "the master trio of our time", as Beethoven's and Schubert's great trios had been in theirs. It is still the most often played of Mendelssohn's two piano trios.
- In one line: a passionate song over a storm (I), a song without words (II), a scherzo as light as air (III), and a finale that fights its way from D minor into D major (IV).

### I. Molto allegro agitato

**Summary.** The cello begins with a long, dark song while the piano throbs underneath in restless, off-beat chords. The movement is in [[sonata form]], built from two singing tunes, and the piano keeps up an almost unbroken stream of fast notes beneath them. It ends faster still, in a rush.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, cello: a broad, sad melody in long notes, rising and falling, over [[syncopation|syncopated]] piano chords that never settle on the beat.
- Theme 2, cello again: a warmer, calmer tune in A major.
- The piano's flood: fast broken chords and runs, the "modern" writing Hiller asked for.

### II. Andante con moto tranquillo

**Summary.** A song for the piano alone, then for the strings. It sounds like one of Mendelssohn's *Songs without Words*, his short piano pieces shaped like songs: a melody in the right hand, a gentle accompaniment and a steady bass. The middle section grows darker and more passionate, then the song returns.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a calm, eight-bar melody in B-flat major, first in the piano's right hand.
- The middle: in the minor, more restless, with a falling figure that recalls the first movement.

### III. Scherzo: Leggiero e vivace

**Summary.** A [[scherzo]] at top speed, light and quiet almost all the way through. The piano starts alone with a skipping tune; the strings join with short, quick notes, and the three toss the tune between them like a ball.

**Main ideas**

- The skipping tune: fast, light, short notes in groups of three, first in the piano.
- A brief singing passage in the middle, which some hear as related to the first movement.

### IV. Finale: Allegro assai appassionato

**Summary.** A restless, dance-like theme in D minor keeps coming back, in the manner of a [[rondo]], with contrasting ideas between its returns. The piano part is the busiest in the work. In the middle the cello sings a broad, hopeful new melody, and shortly before the end the music breaks into D major.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a nervous, jumping tune in D minor, first heard in the piano.
- The cello's song: a broad, warm melody, marked *cantabile*, that arrives halfway through.

**In this recording.** The violinist Itzhak Perlman, the cellist Yo-Yo Ma and the pianist Emanuel Ax recorded both of Mendelssohn's piano trios in New York in March 2009, two hundred years after the composer's birth. It was their first recording together as a trio.

### Threads

- **The singing cello.** The cello begins the first movement with its long song and brings the hopeful tune of the finale; the violin answers it, and often joins it.
- **The piano in charge.** After Hiller's advice the piano part became the heart of the work: it holds the music together, it starts three of the four movements alone, and it rarely stops moving.
- **Songs.** Mendelssohn's gift for melody runs through all four movements, from the cello's first phrase to the *Song without Words* of the Andante.
