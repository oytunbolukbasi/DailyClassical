### The big picture

- Mahler wrote the symphony in the summers of 1903 and 1904 at Maiernigg, his lakeside retreat on the Wörthersee in southern Austria, and kept revising it until 1906. He conducted the premiere in Essen on 27 May 1906.
- These were happy years: he had married Alma Schindler in 1902, and their second daughter was born in June 1904. Yet this is his darkest symphony, and the only one of his symphonies that ends in the minor.
- The nickname "Tragic" is not in the score. Bruno Walter wrote that Mahler called it his "Tragic Symphony", and the programme of the first Vienna performance, in January 1907, printed "Tragische".
- The order of the middle movements is still argued over. Mahler composed and published the work with the Scherzo second. During the rehearsals for the premiere he moved the Andante to second place, and he kept that order in the three performances he conducted. In 1919 Alma Mahler sent the conductor Willem Mengelberg a telegram saying "first Scherzo, then Andante", and the critical edition of 1963 followed her; later research has argued for Andante first again. Conductors still choose either. This recording plays the Scherzo second.
- In one line: a march fought to a bright end (I), the same march twisted into a grotesque dance (II), a song from far away (III), a half-hour battle that ends in defeat (IV).

### I. Allegro energico, ma non troppo

**Summary.** A relentless march in A minor, *heftig, aber markig* (vehement but sturdy). Against it Mahler sets a soaring melody that Alma said was a portrait of her. The movement is a classical [[sonata form]], with its whole first part repeated, and it ends in a blaze of A major: the brightest moment of the symphony.

**Main ideas**

- The march: basses and cellos tramp out a rhythm, then the violins leap and stride in wide, angry intervals.
- The [[motto]]: trumpets play a loud major chord that drains into a soft minor one, over a fixed drum rhythm on the timpani. It comes back in every movement.
- The "Alma theme": a long, sweeping violin melody that surges upward, warm and impatient.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the motto at about 1:40. The same chord changes from major to minor while the drum rhythm stays the same. When it returns later in the symphony, it marks a turning point.
2. Compare the cowbell episode with the march around it. Mahler wanted the bells to sound like a herd grazing in the distance, a world the march cannot reach.

**In this recording.** Currentzis takes the repeat of the exposition, which is why the movement lasts almost 25 minutes. He slows down a great deal in the quiet passages and drives hard in the loud ones, and he starts the Scherzo almost without a pause.

### II. Scherzo

**Summary.** The march comes back, but now in three beats instead of four, heavy and lurching: Mahler marks it *Wuchtig*, "weighty". Twice it gives way to a dainty, old-fashioned dance that keeps tripping over its own feet. The movement is a distorted mirror of the first.

**Main ideas**

- The [[scherzo]]: stamping low strings and timpani, shrill woodwinds, yelping horns, the clatter of the xylophone.
- The [[trio]]: marked *Altväterisch* ("old-fashioned"), a polite little dance in the woodwinds in which the number of beats in the bar keeps changing.

**Things to notice**

1. Tap the rhythm of the opening and compare it with the first movement's march. The pattern is the same, but with three beats instead of four it limps.
2. Alma Mahler wrote that this movement pictured their two small daughters "tottering in zigzags over the sand". The dates do not fit: when Mahler wrote the movement in 1903, his first daughter was less than a year old and the second not yet born.

### III. Andante moderato

**Summary.** The only movement that is not haunted by the march. In E-flat major, far from A minor, a gentle song unfolds in the strings and woodwinds. Cowbells sound again, now inside the orchestra, and the music grows to a broad, glowing climax before settling into calm.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a tender, introspective phrase for the violins, which often clouds over towards the minor.
- The woodwind songs: plaintive solos for the woodwinds and horn that answer the strings.

### IV. Finale

**Summary.** More than half an hour long, the battle of the whole symphony. A dark, slow introduction leads to a march that keeps fighting its way upward and is struck down by two blows of a [[hammer]]. Mahler's instruction for the sound: "brief and mighty, but dull in resonance and with a non-metallic character (like the fall of an axe)". The end is in A minor, without the major chord.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: a shimmering swirl in the high strings, harps and celesta, a violin phrase that climbs and then sinks, dark chords in the brass and tuba.
- The march: a fierce main theme in A minor, led by the horns and strings.
- The second theme: a bright, leaping tune that seems to promise victory.
- The motto: the major-to-minor chord from the first movement, which returns at turning points.

**Things to notice**

1. Count the hammer blows. Alma Mahler said her husband described them as three blows of fate falling on a hero, "the third of which fells him like a tree". Mahler cut the third, and the passage where it was now ends in a sudden stillness.
2. Listen to the very last chord. In the first movement, the motto always went from major to minor. Here there is no major chord left.

**In this recording.** Currentzis uses two hammer blows, as in Mahler's revision. Reviewers noted that he does not make them deafening: what matters to him is what happens after each one, a sudden acceleration after the first and a seething struggle after the second.

### Threads

- **Major turning into minor.** The motto chord sums up the symphony: a moment of light that drains into darkness. The first movement still ends in A major; the finale ends on the minor chord alone.
- **The march.** The first movement's march returns in triple time in the Scherzo and fights its last battle in the finale. Only the Andante escapes it.
- **Cowbells.** In the first movement, the Andante and the finale, the bells open a door to a distant, peaceful world. Each time, the struggle closes it again.
- **Two orders.** Played with the Scherzo second, as here, the march rhythm hammers through the first half of the symphony and the Andante comes as a release before the finale. With the Andante second, the Scherzo leads straight into the finale's darkness. Abbado's recording, listed below, uses that order.
