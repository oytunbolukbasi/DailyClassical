### The big picture

- Tchaikovsky began the concerto in October 1879 at his sister's estate at Kamenka, in Ukraine, wrote the finale in Paris that November and orchestrated it at Kamenka in the spring of 1880. He scored it without trombones or tuba.
- He dedicated it to Nikolai Rubinstein, who had once savaged his First Concerto and then become its champion. Rubinstein died in March 1881 before he could play it, and the first performance took place in New York on 12 November 1881, with the pianist Madeline Schiller and the conductor Theodore Thomas.
- Tchaikovsky was disappointed that the concerto never caught on, and late in life wrote that he liked it "far more than the first". His pupil Alexander Siloti pressed him for cuts; Tchaikovsky refused most of them. Siloti's edition, published in 1897 after the composer's death, cut and rearranged the first two movements, removed most of the solo violin and cello in the slow movement, and was for decades the version everyone played. Tchaikovsky's original was published in 1955.
- The concerto is unusual in how often piano and orchestra take turns rather than playing together. Its first movement has a long solo [[cadenza]], and its slow movement turns for long stretches into chamber music for piano, violin and cello.
- In one line: a proud, festive march with a huge solo cadenza (I), a tender song for a [[piano trio]] inside the orchestra (II), a quick, carnival-like dance (III).

### I. Allegro brillante

**Summary.** The orchestra opens with a proud, march-like theme, and the piano takes it up at once. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but built in large blocks: the orchestra plays, then the piano plays, and they rarely mix. Its centre is a solo cadenza several minutes long, the climax of the movement, placed in the middle rather than near the end.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, the march: bold and festive, stepping up and down from the home note, with a crisp rhythm.
- Theme 2: tender and singing, in E-flat major, first on clarinet and horn over quivering strings.

1. Count how often you hear the piano alone and the orchestra alone. Tchaikovsky once said he could not stand the sound of piano and orchestra together; here he mostly keeps them apart.
2. In the cadenza the piano builds the movement's climax entirely on its own. Notice how long the orchestra stays silent.

**In this recording.** Nebolsin plays Tchaikovsky's original version, without Siloti's cuts. The concerto was recorded in Wellington in November 2014 with the New Zealand Symphony Orchestra.

### II. Andante non troppo

**Summary.** A slow movement in D major in which the piano is, for long stretches, only one of three soloists. A solo violin sings the long main melody, a solo cello takes it up, and the piano joins them so that the three play almost like a [[piano trio]] with the orchestra around them. In the middle the music speeds up and the piano has a cadenza of its own; then the melody returns, shared by all three.

**Main ideas**

- The main melody: long, warm and nostalgic, first on the solo violin.
- The trio: piano, violin and cello in close conversation.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for how long the piano waits before it enters. For the first minutes this is a violin and cello concerto.
2. This is the music Siloti cut most heavily. Most of the violin and cello solos you hear were missing from performances for decades.

**In this recording.** The violin and cello soloists are the orchestra's own players: the concertmaster Vesa-Matti Leppänen and the cellist Andrew Joyce.

### III. Allegro con fuoco

**Summary.** A short, quick finale in a skipping, dotted two-beat rhythm. Three lively, carnival-like tunes follow one another in a loose [[rondo]], and here, for once, piano and orchestra play closely together. There is no cadenza; the end is a blaze of piano and orchestra that recalls the close of the First Concerto.

**Main ideas**

- Tune 1: quick and skipping, in a dotted rhythm.
- Tune 2: more exotic in colour.
- Tune 3: another contrasting idea, with the piano sparkling in its high register.

### Threads

- **Piano alone, orchestra alone.** In the first two movements piano and orchestra mostly take turns. Only in the finale do they play as one.
- **A concerto with three soloists.** The slow movement's violin and cello make the piece, for a while, almost a triple concerto. Tchaikovsky's former pupil Sergei Taneyev, who gave the first Russian performance, criticised exactly that.
- **Siloti's version.** For most of the 20th century the concerto was heard in Siloti's cut edition; George Balanchine's ballet *Ballet Imperial* (1941) used it too. Recordings of the original, like this one, have restored the work Tchaikovsky wrote.
