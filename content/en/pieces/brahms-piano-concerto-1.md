### The big picture

- Brahms began the music in 1854, in his early twenties, as a sonata for two pianos. It grew into a symphony, then, in 1855–56, into a piano concerto. He finished it in 1858, after years of advice from his friend the violinist Joseph Joachim, who helped him with the orchestra.
- Those were the years of Robert Schumann's breakdown. Schumann, Brahms's mentor, was taken to an asylum in 1854 and died there in 1856. Brahms stayed close to his wife, Clara, and many listeners hear that grief in the concerto.
- Brahms played the solo part at the first performance, in Hanover on 22 January 1859. The audience was cool. Five days later in Leipzig it was hissed. It took years for the concerto to be accepted.
- It is a concerto on the scale of a symphony. The first movement alone lasts over 20 minutes, the piano often works inside the orchestra rather than in front of it, and there is no showy [[cadenza]] in the first movement.
- In one line: a tragic storm (I), a hushed prayer (II), a fierce chase that breaks through into D major (III).

### I. Maestoso

**Summary.** The orchestra opens with a thunderous drum roll and a wild, jagged theme. When the piano finally enters, after more than three minutes, it does so quietly, with a new idea of its own. The movement is a vast [[sonata form]] with a [[double exposition]]. Its noblest tune belongs to the piano alone, and the ending is tragic, not triumphant.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, the storm: a leaping, angular theme in violins and cellos over a roll of timpani, with fierce [[trill]]s.
- Theme 2a: a sad, swinging tune, first heard quietly in the orchestra.
- Theme 2b: a broad, noble melody in F major, played by the piano alone, rising like a hymn.

**Things to notice**

1. When the piano first enters, it does not play the storm theme. It begins something new and quiet, as if it had a different story to tell.
2. Listen for the timpani. Brahms uses them far more than was usual, from the first rumble to the last chord.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** After the storm, a prayer. [[muted|Muted]] strings and bassoons play a slow, hymn-like melody, and the piano answers alone, almost in a whisper. Under the opening notes in his manuscript Brahms wrote the words of the *Benedictus*, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord". The movement is quiet almost throughout and ends with a short, inward [[cadenza]] for the piano.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: a broad, slow melody in muted violins and violas, with bassoons moving gently below.
- The piano's answer: a quiet, prayer-like theme in soft chords, played alone.

**Things to notice**

1. Sing the words to the opening melody: "Be-ne-dic-tus qui ve-nit". They fit the notes. Brahms often called Schumann "Mynheer Domini", and many hear the movement as a memorial to him; Brahms also described it as a portrait of Clara.

### III. Rondo: Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** The piano starts alone with a fierce, leaping theme in D minor, and the orchestra roars back. This is a [[rondo]]: the theme keeps returning between contrasting episodes. All the themes share a rising shape. There is a hushed [[fugato]] in the strings, two short cadenzas, and in the end the music breaks into D major.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a fast, leaping, aggressive tune, first in the piano alone.
- The second theme: noble and rising, in F major, again for the piano alone.
- The third theme: gentle and yearning, in the strings.

### Threads

- **A symphony in disguise.** The concerto began as a symphony, and it shows: the orchestra is a full partner, and the piano often plays inside the texture instead of over it.
- **Schumann.** The first movement's storm, the second movement's *Benedictus* and its portrait of Clara: the concerto was written in the shadow of Robert Schumann's illness and death.
- **The rising shape.** The noble piano melody of the first movement climbs upward; the themes of the finale all share that rising shape.
- **Minor to major.** D minor rules the first movement and the start of the finale; the concerto ends in D major.
