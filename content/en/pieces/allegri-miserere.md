### The big picture

- Gregorio Allegri, a singer in the Pope's choir, wrote this setting of Psalm 51, "Have mercy on me, O God", in the 1630s, some time before 1638. It was written for the Sistine Chapel in Rome and sung there during the services of Holy Week.
- The written music is simple: slow chords, verse after verse, alternating with [[plainchant]]. What made it famous were the ornaments the papal singers added, a tradition passed on by ear and closely guarded.
- The best-known story is about Mozart. In April 1770, aged fourteen, he heard the Miserere in Rome and wrote it down from memory. The only source is a letter from his father. Historians doubt parts of the story, since copies of the music were already known outside the Vatican; it had been sung in London since about 1735.
- The famous high note, a top C for a solo soprano, is not Allegri's. It comes from a version published in 1880 and became famous through an English edition of 1951 and a 1963 recording by the choir of King's College, Cambridge.
- In one line: a full choir, plainchant and a solo quartet take turns, verse by verse, until both choirs join for the last verse.

### I. Miserere mei, Deus

**Summary.** Twenty verses of the psalm, sung by three groups in turn: a five-voice choir, plainchant on a single melodic line, and a four-voice solo quartet whose top line climbs to the high C. For the last verse both choirs sing together, nine voices in all.

**Main ideas**

- The choir: five voices singing slow, plain chords.
- The chant: the words sung to a simple, ancient melody, a single line with no harmony.
- The quartet: four solo voices, lighter than the full choir, with the top soprano floating up to a very high note and down again in ornamental turns.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The full choir, slow and quiet, in rich chords | Verse 1, the five-voice choir |
| ≈ 0:45 | A single plain melody, with no harmony under it | Verse 2, in plainchant |
| ≈ 1:00 | Four solo voices, lighter than the full choir; the highest climbs to a very high note and holds it | Verse 3, the solo quartet, with the high C |
| ≈ 2:15 | After another chanted verse, the full choir again | Verse 5. The cycle begins again: choir, chant, quartet, chant |
| ≈ 3:15 | The solo quartet and the high note again | Verse 7 |
| ≈ 5:35 | The quartet again | Verse 11 |
| ≈ 7:55 | The quartet again | Verse 15 |
| ≈ 10:15 | The quartet for the last time | Verse 19 |
| ≈ 11:15 | Both groups sing together, a fuller sound than before, then a long, quiet close | Verse 20: nine voices together |

**Things to notice**

1. The high C comes five times, once in each quartet verse. Each time, listen for how the soprano reaches it and how slowly she comes down.
2. Between the high notes, the chanted verses are very plain. The contrast is part of the effect.

**In this recording.** The Sixteen and Harry Christophers recorded the Miserere in November 1989 at St Jude-on-the-Hill, a church in north London. The soloists are Ruth Dean and Sally Dunkley (sopranos), Christopher Royall (alto) and Christopher Purves (bass). On the Spotify album the Miserere is track 3, after Lotti's *Crucifixus* and Palestrina's *Stabat mater*; Palestrina's *Missa Papae Marcelli* follows.

### Threads

- **Three groups.** The piece is a conversation between the full choir, the plain chant and the solo quartet.
- **Ornament over plain chords.** The written music is simple; the beauty comes from the decoration that singers added over generations.
