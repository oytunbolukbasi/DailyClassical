### The big picture

- Gregorio Allegri, a singer in the Pope's choir, wrote this setting of Psalm 51, "Have mercy on me, O God", in the 1630s, some time before 1638. It was written for the Sistine Chapel in Rome and sung there during the services of Holy Week.
- The written music is simple: slow chords, verse after verse, alternating with [[plainchant]]. What made it famous were the ornaments the papal singers added, a tradition passed on by ear and closely guarded.
- The best-known story is about Mozart. In April 1770, aged fourteen, he heard the Miserere in Rome and wrote it down from memory. The only source is a letter from his father. Historians doubt parts of the story, since copies of the music were already known outside the Vatican; it had been sung in London since about 1735.
- The famous high note, a top C for a solo soprano, is not Allegri's. It comes from a version published in 1880 and became famous through an English edition of 1951 and a 1963 recording by the choir of King's College, Cambridge.
- In one line: a full choir, a single chanting voice and a distant solo quartet take turns, verse by verse, until both choirs join for the last verse.

### I. Miserere mei, Deus

**Summary.** Twenty verses of the psalm, sung by three groups in turn: a five-voice choir, a single voice singing plainchant, and a four-voice solo quartet whose top line climbs to the high C. For the last verse both choirs sing together, nine voices in all.

**Main ideas**

- The choir: five voices singing slow, plain chords.
- The chant: one man's voice alone, singing the words to a simple, ancient melody.
- The quartet: four solo voices, placed further away, with the top soprano floating up to a very high note and down again in ornamental turns.

**Things to notice**

1. The high C comes five times, once in each quartet verse. Each time, listen for how the soprano reaches it and how slowly she comes down.
2. Between the high notes, the chanted verses are very plain. The contrast is part of the effect.

**In this recording.** The album has the Miserere twice. The times here follow the first track, the familiar version with the high C sung by Deborah Roberts. The last track adds further ornaments that Roberts developed over many years of performing the piece. The solo quartet was placed at a distance to suggest the sound of the singers in the Sistine Chapel, and Andrew Carwood sings the chant.

### Threads

- **Three groups.** The piece is a conversation between the full choir, the single chanting voice and the distant quartet.
- **Ornament over plain chords.** The written music is simple; the beauty comes from the decoration that singers added over generations.
