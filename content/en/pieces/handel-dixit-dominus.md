### The big picture

- Handel, born in Halle in 1685, had already worked as a violinist, harpsichordist and opera composer in Hamburg when he left for Italy in 1706. In Rome the Pope had banned opera, so music lived in the palaces and churches of wealthy cardinals and princes, and the young Saxon soon had powerful patrons among them.
- *Dixit Dominus* sets Psalm 110 (109 in the Latin Bible), "The Lord said unto my Lord", one of the psalms sung at Vespers, the evening service. Handel probably began it in Venice, on Venetian paper, and dated the finished score in Rome, April 1707. It is the earliest of his manuscripts to survive.
- Where and when it was first sung is not known. Scholars have suggested Easter or the first days of May 1707, or the Carmelite feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel in mid-July, in 1707 or 1708, at Santa Maria in Montesanto, a church under the protection of Cardinal Carlo Colonna. There is no document to settle it.
- It is written for five solo singers (two sopranos, alto, tenor, bass), a five-part choir and strings with two viola parts, over the [[continuo]]. In Italy Handel met Alessandro Scarlatti and heard the music of Corelli and Vivaldi; the Dixit Dominus is Italian in its brilliance and German in its dense [[counterpoint]], and full of word-painting.
- In one line: the Lord's command (I), two solo songs (II–III), an oath and a priesthood (IV–V), judgement and battle (VI–VII), a quiet duet by the brook (VIII), and a blazing doxology that brings back the opening chant (IX).

### I. Dixit Dominus Domino meo

**Summary.** "The Lord said unto my Lord: sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." A large, driving movement in G minor for choir, solo voices and strings. Against the busy counterpoint Handel sets a slow church chant, sung in long notes by the sopranos.

**Main ideas**

- "Dixit": short, punched-out chords on the word "said", returning again and again.
- The chant: a plain psalm tone from [[plainchant]], held in long notes as a [[cantus firmus]] while the other voices race around it.

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the chant at about two and a half minutes: long, even notes in the top voice, as if a much older piece of music were being carried along inside a new one. It comes back in the last movement.

**In this recording.** The Scholars Baroque Ensemble, founded in 1987 by the bass David van Asch, sing and play with one voice and one instrument to a part: five singers, who are both the soloists and the choir, two violins, two violas, cello, violone and organ, and no conductor. The result is lean and clear rather than grand. It was recorded in All Saints Church, East Finchley, in London, in October 1994; on the album the Dixit Dominus is tracks 1–9, followed by Handel's *Salve Regina* and *Nisi Dominus*, both also from 1707.

### II. Virgam virtutis tuae

**Summary.** "The Lord shall send the rod of thy power out of Zion: rule thou in the midst of thine enemies." A short [[aria]] for alto, sung here by a countertenor, with only the continuo beneath it: a forceful, declamatory solo.

**Main ideas**

- The voice: strong, proclaiming phrases, with quick runs on the words of power and rule.
- The bass line: cello and organ, walking steadily under the voice.

### VII. Judicabit in nationibus

**Summary.** "He shall judge among the nations; he shall fill the places with dead bodies; he shall wound the heads over many countries." The most violent movement, a sequence of vivid word-pictures: ruins falling, and heads being shattered, heard as hammered, broken syllables.

**Main ideas**

- "Judicabit": a vigorous theme passed between the voices.
- "Implebit ruinas": runs that tumble downwards.
- "Conquassabit": the word cut into separate, hammered syllables, "con-quas-sa-bit", over pounding strings.

### VIII. De torrente in via bibet

**Summary.** "He shall drink of the brook in the way: therefore shall he lift up the head." After the violence, the stillest music in the work: two sopranos in a slow, aching duet, over a soft chant from the tenors and basses and quiet repeated chords in the strings.

**Main ideas**

- The duet: two soprano lines that rub against each other, one holding a note while the other moves, then changing places.

### IX. Gloria Patri

**Summary.** "Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit; as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end. Amen." The words of praise that close every psalm at Vespers, set as a brilliant finale. At "as it was in the beginning" the chant of the first movement returns, and the work ends with a fugue on "Amen".

**Main ideas**

- "Gloria": fast, leaping, ringing lines for choir and strings.
- The chant: the slow psalm tone from the first movement, back in long notes.
- "Amen": a fugue, entering voice by voice.

**Listening stops**

### Threads

- **The chant.** An old psalm tone, sung in long notes, appears in the first movement and returns in the last, at the words "as it was in the beginning".
- **Words made audible.** Hammered syllables for shattered heads, tumbling runs for ruins, a whispered chant for the brook: Handel paints the psalm line by line, as he would later do in his oratorios.
- **One to a part.** In this recording every line is sung or played by one performer, so the choir is also the group of soloists, and every strand of the counterpoint can be heard.
