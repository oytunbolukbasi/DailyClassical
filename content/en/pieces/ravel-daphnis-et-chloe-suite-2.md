### The big picture

- *Daphnis et Chloé* is a ballet in three parts, which Ravel finished in 1912 after several years' work, for Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes. It tells a story from an ancient Greek romance by Longus: the young goatherd Daphnis and the shepherdess Chloé, their first love, Chloé's capture by pirates and her rescue by the god Pan.
- The premiere was at the Théâtre du Châtelet in Paris on 8 June 1912, conducted by Pierre Monteux, with Vaslav Nijinsky and Tamara Karsavina as the lovers, choreography by Michel Fokine and sets by Léon Bakst. At almost an hour, it is Ravel's longest work. He called it a "choreographic symphony".
- Ravel made two concert suites from it. Suite No. 2, published in 1913, is the whole of the last part: daybreak, a pantomime and a final dance. In the ballet a wordless chorus joins the orchestra; the suite can be played with it or without it.
- The orchestra is very large, with a big percussion section, two harps and celesta, and Ravel uses it with great precision: the sound of dew, birds, a shepherd's pipes, the sun.
- In one line: the sun rises and the lovers meet again (I), they act out the story of Pan and Syrinx (II), and everyone dances (III).

### I. Lever du jour

**Summary.** Daybreak at the grotto of the Nymphs. Daphnis lies asleep at the entrance. The only sound is the trickle of dew from the rocks; birds begin to sing, a shepherd passes playing his pipe, and a great melody rises from the depths of the orchestra as the sun comes up. Daphnis wakes and looks for Chloé; she appears, and they fall into each other's arms. The old shepherd Lammon explains that Pan saved her, in memory of the nymph Syrinx he once loved.

**Main ideas**

- The murmur of water: rippling flutes, clarinets, harps and muted strings, never still.
- The dawn melody: a long, slow, rising tune in the low strings, which grows until the whole orchestra sings it.

### II. Pantomime

**Summary.** To thank the god, Daphnis and Chloé act out the story of Pan and the nymph Syrinx. Chloé plays the nymph, wandering in the meadow; Daphnis plays Pan, who declares his love. She runs away and hides among the reeds. In despair Pan cuts some reeds, makes a pipe and plays a sad melody: here the flute has a long solo, one of the best known in the orchestral repertoire. Chloé dances to it, faster and faster, and falls into Daphnis's arms. Before the altar of the Nymphs he swears his love.

**Main ideas**

- The nymph's music: a delicate oboe tune, light and wandering.
- Pan's flute: a long, free solo, sad at first, then more and more agile and whirling.

### III. Danse générale

**Summary.** The final dance, a bacchanal for everyone on stage. It is in five beats to the bar, which gives it a limping, breathless energy. Fragments of earlier themes flash past, the music rushes on without a pause, and the work ends with a huge explosion of sound.

**Main ideas**

- The dance rhythm: rapid swirling figures in groups of five beats, hammered out by the whole orchestra.
- A flash of the lovers' music in the middle, swept along by the dance.

### Threads

- **A day in one suite.** The suite runs without a break from first light to the height of the celebration, from the softest sound in the orchestra to the loudest.
- **Nature in sound.** Water, birdsong, a shepherd's pipe, the sun: Ravel paints each one with exact instrumental colours, never with vague washes.
- **One love story inside another.** The young lovers act out an older love story, Pan and Syrinx, and the flute is both Pan's reed pipe and the voice of their own feelings.
