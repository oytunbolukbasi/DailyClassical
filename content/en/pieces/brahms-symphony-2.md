### The big picture

- Brahms wrote the symphony in the summer of 1877 at Pörtschach, a village on the Wörthersee in Carinthia, southern Austria. His First Symphony had taken him some 21 years; the Second took a few months.
- It was first played in Vienna on 30 December 1877 by the Vienna Philharmonic under Hans Richter. The premiere had been planned for 9 December but was put off because the orchestra was busy learning Wagner's *Das Rheingold*.
- The music is warm and open, and it is often called Brahms's "pastoral" symphony, after Beethoven's Sixth. Brahms joked to his publisher that it was so melancholy that the score should "come out in mourning".
- There are shadows all the same. Trombones, tuba and timpani darken the first minute, and this is the only one of his four symphonies with a tuba. In a letter to the conductor Vinzenz Lachner, Brahms called himself "a severely melancholic person" and linked that to "those timpani and trombones".
- In one line: a calm, singing opening clouded by dark brass (I), a deep, searching slow movement (II), a graceful dance that keeps breaking into a scamper (III), a hushed start that bursts into Brahms's most exuberant finale (IV).

### I. Allegro non troppo

**Summary.** The symphony opens with a quiet three-note turn in the basses and a calm horn melody, and that turn grows into almost everything that follows. The movement is a long [[sonata form]] full of singing tunes, but twice it is darkened by trombones and timpani. It ends in deep calm.

**Main ideas**

- The turn: three notes in the cellos and basses (D, C-sharp, D), heard in the very first bar.
- Theme 1: a mellow, rocking melody for horns, answered by the woodwinds.
- Theme 2: a broad, swaying melody for violas and cellos, first in a minor key.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the three-note turn in the first bar. It comes back in the trombones' dark chords and, at full force, at the climax of the development.
2. Compare the two entries of the trombones: hushed at the start, violent in the development.

### II. Adagio non troppo

**Summary.** The most serious movement of the symphony, and one of the most complex Brahms wrote. A long, sighing melody in the cellos never quite settles, the rhythm often blurs, and a gentler swinging tune passes by. When the opening melody returns, a storm breaks out instead of the second tune.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a long, falling cello melody, with a slowly rising line in the bassoons against it.
- Theme 2: a lilting, swinging tune for flutes and oboes, in a rocking 12/8 rhythm.

**Things to notice**

1. In the first minute, try tapping the beat. The melody leans on the weak beats, so the pulse is hard to find, and this is part of its unease.

### III. Allegretto grazioso (quasi andantino)

**Summary.** Instead of a fast [[scherzo]], a gentle dance for oboe. Twice it suddenly speeds up into a scampering *Presto*, and both times the fast music is the same tune in disguise. It is the shortest movement in Brahms's four symphonies.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a lilting, minuet-like tune for oboe, with plucked cellos below.
- The *Presto*: the same tune, sped up into a quick two-beat rhythm, later into a skipping three-beat one.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 2:05 | The oboe's dance returns | The main section again |
| ≈ 2:50 | Fast again, now in a skipping three-beat rhythm | The second *Presto*, another transformation of the tune |

**Things to notice**

1. When the first *Presto* bursts in, hum the oboe's opening notes. The fast strings follow the same outline.

### IV. Allegro con spirito

**Summary.** The finale starts in a whisper, with the same three-note turn that opened the symphony, then explodes. A broad, warm second theme follows, and a calm, floating episode in the middle holds the energy back. The trombones wait almost to the end, then blaze out in the final pages.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a quiet, secretive tune in the strings that grows into a joyful outburst.
- Theme 2: a broad, warm melody for the violins, marked *largamente* (broadly).

### Threads

- **One small seed.** The three-note turn in the basses at the very start comes back across the symphony, most clearly at the hushed opening of the finale.
- **Light and shadow.** Brahms wrote his sunniest symphony and then darkened it with trombones, tuba and timpani. The same instruments turn the end of the finale into pure brilliance.
- **Transformation.** In the third movement, the fast sections are the dance tune in new rhythms, not new tunes. Brahms keeps reworking the same material instead of simply adding contrasts.
