### The big picture

- Schubert wrote it in Vienna in November 1824, a month after returning from a summer as music teacher to the Esterházy family at Zseliz, then in Hungary (today Želiezovce, Slovakia). It is for the arpeggione, a new instrument built a year or so earlier by the Viennese maker Johann Georg Staufer. It was a kind of bowed guitar: six strings, tuned like a guitar and with frets, held between the knees like a cello and played with a bow.
- He wrote it for Vincenz Schuster, a virtuoso on the instrument. The arpeggione never caught on, and Schubert's sonata is almost the only reason anyone remembers it.
- 1824 was a hard year for Schubert. He had been seriously ill, and that spring he wrote to his friend Leopold Kupelwieser that he felt himself "the most unhappy and wretched creature in the world". The sonata's mood moves between that melancholy and a lighter, almost carefree charm.
- It was not published until 1871, long after his death, and by then the arpeggione had fallen out of use. Today it is played on the cello, on the viola, and sometimes on other instruments. Both have four strings instead of six, tuned further apart, so players move some passages up or down an octave and work harder in the fast runs.
- In one line: a sad, singing Allegro with flashes of virtuosity (I), a short, hymn-like song (II) that leads without a break into a gentle, dancing rondo (III).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The piano begins alone with a sad, gently rocking tune; the cello takes it up and sings it in its warm middle register. A brighter second theme brings quick, playful figures that show off the instrument. The movement is in [[sonata form]], and in this recording the opening section is played twice.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a melancholy melody in A minor, first in the piano, then in the cello.
- Theme 2: a perky, bouncing tune in C major, with quick repeated notes and runs.
- The virtuoso figures: fast [[arpeggio|arpeggios]] and scales, the kind of writing that gave the arpeggione its name.

1. Compare the two themes: one sad and singing, the other playful. Much of the sonata swings between those two moods.

**In this recording.** Rostropovich and Britten recorded it at Snape, the concert hall Britten had opened the year before for his Aldeburgh Festival. The two were close friends; Britten wrote his own Cello Sonata and three suites for Rostropovich. Their tempos are slow, and the first movement here is much longer than in most recordings.

### III. Allegretto

**Summary.** A relaxed, good-natured [[rondo]] in A major. Its main tune is gentle and lilting, and it returns between livelier episodes, one in the minor with energetic leaps and another full of quick runs and broken chords. It ends quietly, without fuss.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo tune: a lilting, song-like melody in two beats, first in the cello.
- The minor-key episode: more energetic, with leaps and strong accents.
- The virtuoso episode: rapid runs, arpeggios and plucked notes ([[pizzicato]]).

### Threads

- **A forgotten instrument.** Every bar was written for an instrument almost nobody plays. What we hear is always a translation, and cellists and violists each make it sound like their own instrument's music.
- **Song.** Schubert wrote more than six hundred songs, and the sonata often sounds like one: a voice (the cello) over an accompaniment (the piano).
- **Minor and major.** The sonata starts in A minor and ends in A major, but the major-key finale never forgets the sadness of the opening.
