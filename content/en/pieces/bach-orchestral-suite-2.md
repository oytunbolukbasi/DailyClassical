### The big picture

- Bach wrote four orchestral suites, which he called *ouvertures* after their long opening movements; they were not planned as a set. This one survives in a set of parts from Leipzig dated 1738–39, and Bach wrote out the flute and viola parts himself. It is the latest of the four. In October 1739 he went back to directing the Collegium Musicum, the society that gave weekly concerts at Zimmermann's coffee house, and the suite may well have been played there.
- It is the only one of the four with a solo instrument: a transverse flute that plays almost all the time, so the suite is close to a flute concerto disguised as a set of French dances. The rest of the band is just strings and [[continuo]].
- The flute may not have been the first idea. The scholar Joshua Rifkin has argued, from mistakes in the surviving parts, that they were copied from an earlier version a tone lower, in A minor, for solo violin; at the top of the solo part Bach seems to have turned a "V" into the "T" of "Traversiere". Others think the original soloist was an oboe. The question is open.
- The dances are French, apart from the Polonaise, which is based on a Polish folk song. Bach's own sovereign, the Elector of Saxony, was also King of Poland, and in 1736 Bach received the title of court composer to him. The last movement, the Badinerie ("jest"; Bach spelled it "Battinerie"), has become one of the best-known showpieces for the flute.
- In one line: a grand French overture (I), five dances, from graceful to stately (II–VI), and a quick, playful finale for the flute (VII).

### I. Ouverture

**Summary.** A [[French overture]] in three parts: a slow, stately opening in jerky dotted rhythms, a fast middle section that starts like a [[fugue]], and a slow close. In the fast section the flute keeps stepping out of the band for solo passages, as in a concerto.

**Main ideas**

- The slow music: heavy, solemn chords in a long-short dotted rhythm, with the flute doubling the first violins.
- The fast theme: a busy line taken up by one part after another.
- The flute's solos: quick, light passages over a thinner accompaniment.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. In the fast section, listen for the moments when the band thins out and the flute is heard on its own. They are the concerto inside the suite.

**In this recording.** The English Concert play on period instruments, with Stephen Preston on a wooden Baroque flute, softer and more veiled than a modern metal one, and Trevor Pinnock directing from the harpsichord. In the overture they repeat only the slow opening section. On the Spotify album the suite is tracks 1–7, followed by Suite No. 3.

### III. Sarabande

**Summary.** A slow, dignified [[sarabande]], with the weight on the second beat of the bar. Under the calm surface Bach hides a puzzle: the bass plays the same melody as the flute and first violins, a little later and much lower, so the movement is a [[canon]].

**Main ideas**

- The melody: slow and noble, in the flute with the first violins.
- The bass: the same melody again, starting a moment later and an octave and a fifth lower.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

### V. Polonaise – Double

**Summary.** A stately Polish dance in three, built on a folk song. In the middle comes the Double, a variation: the tune moves down into the bass, and above it the flute plays a decorated line. Then the Polonaise returns.

**Main ideas**

- The Polonaise: a proud, stately tune with a dotted rhythm, in flute and violins.
- The Double: the same tune in the bass instruments, with a flowing flute line above and almost nothing else.

### Threads

- **A concerto in disguise.** The flute takes solos in the overture, gets its own bourrée and the decorations of the Double, and carries the finale alone.
- **France in Leipzig.** A French overture and French dances, written in Saxony for German players, with a Polish dance in the middle for good measure.
- **One key throughout.** Every movement is in B minor. The variety comes from the dances' rhythms and from what the flute is doing, not from changes of key.
