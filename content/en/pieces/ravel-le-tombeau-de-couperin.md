### The big picture

- A *tombeau*, literally a tomb, was a piece written in memory of someone, a custom of French composers in the 17th century. The Couperin of the title is François Couperin, called "the Great" (1668–1733), harpsichordist to Louis XIV. Ravel said he meant less a tribute to Couperin himself than to French music of that age: its dances, its ornaments, its clarity.
- He wrote the suite for piano between 1914 and 1917, in six movements, in the years he served in the war as an army lorry driver. Each movement is dedicated to the memory of friends who had died fighting. When critics found the music too cheerful for a memorial, Ravel answered: "The dead are sad enough, in their eternal silence."
- In 1919 he orchestrated four of the six pieces for a small orchestra (pairs of woodwind, two horns, one trumpet, harp and strings), leaving out the Fugue and the Toccata and putting the Rigaudon last. The orchestral suite was first played in Paris in February 1920 by the Pasdeloup Orchestra under Rhené-Baton.
- The oboe is the star: it opens the suite with a stream of rippling notes, sings the Menuet and the slow middle of the Rigaudon.
- In one line: a rippling prelude for the oboe (I), a lilting, bittersweet dance (II), a gentle minuet with a bagpipe-like middle (III), and a brisk, stamping finale with a pastoral heart (IV).

### I. Prélude

**Summary.** In memory of Lieutenant Jacques Charlot, who had arranged Ravel's *Ma mère l'Oye* for solo piano. The oboe sets off at once on a stream of quick, rippling notes that hardly stops for three minutes, passed between the woodwinds and the strings like light on water. It is a [[perpetuum mobile]], and a famous test for orchestral oboists.

**Main ideas**

- The ripple: a fast, even run of notes in groups of three, decorated with little turns, as in harpsichord music of Couperin's time.

### II. Forlane

**Summary.** In memory of Gabriel Deluc, a Basque painter from Saint-Jean-de-Luz. The [[forlane]] is an old Italian dance in a lilting six-beat rhythm; Ravel prepared for this piece by copying out a forlane by Couperin. Ravel's version keeps the old rhythm but adds harmonies that sound slightly sour, as if remembered through a clouded glass. A refrain keeps coming back between gentler episodes.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a lilting tune with a limping, dotted rhythm and bittersweet, clashing chords.
- The episodes: smoother, more tender music between the returns of the refrain.

### III. Menuet

**Summary.** In memory of Jean Dreyfus, at whose home Ravel recovered after leaving the army. The oboe sings a graceful minuet tune over a light accompaniment. In the middle comes a [[musette]], music in imitation of a bagpipe: a low held note underneath and a hushed, solemn tune that slowly grows to a powerful climax, before the minuet returns.

**Main ideas**

- The minuet: a calm, elegant tune for the oboe in three-beat time.
- The musette: a [[drone]] in the low instruments and a quiet tune that swells to a climax.

### IV. Rigaudon

**Summary.** In memory of Pierre and Pascal Gaudin, two brothers who were Ravel's childhood friends, killed by the same shell in November 1914. A [[rigaudon]] is a lively old dance from the south of France. The orchestra stamps it out with bright, brisk energy; in the middle the oboe slows down for a wistful, pastoral tune over plucked strings, like a guitar. Then the brisk dance returns and the suite ends.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: loud, quick and sharply accented, in C major.
- The middle: a slower, pastoral melody for the oboe in C minor, over [[pizzicato]] strings.

### Threads

- **Memory without mourning.** Each piece remembers someone killed in the war, but none is a lament. Ravel's answer was that the dead are sad enough; the music offers them clear, bright dances instead.
- **Old forms, new harmony.** Prélude, forlane, minuet and rigaudon were all dances of Couperin's time. Ravel keeps their shapes and rhythms and fills them with harmonies of his own day, an early example of [[neoclassicism]].
- **Piano first.** Like much of Ravel's music, the suite exists twice, for piano and for orchestra. In the orchestral version the piano's six pieces become four.
