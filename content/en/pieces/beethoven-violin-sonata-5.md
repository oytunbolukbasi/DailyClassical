### The big picture

- Beethoven finished the sonata in 1801, the year in which he first confided to friends, in letters, that he was losing his hearing. He meant it as the companion of the darker Sonata No. 4 in A minor; for reasons not known, the two were published separately, with different opus numbers. Both are dedicated to Count Moritz von Fries, a rich banker and patron, to whom Beethoven later dedicated the Seventh Symphony.
- The nickname "Spring" is not Beethoven's; it appeared after his death. It has stuck because the music is unusually warm and relaxed.
- It is the first of his violin sonatas in four movements, with a short [[scherzo]] added between the slow movement and the finale.
- The title of the time was "sonata for piano and violin", and the two are true partners: whoever has the tune, the other accompanies, and the roles keep changing.
- In one line: a long, sunny tune and its dramatic shadow (I), a slow song without words (II), a one-minute rhythmic joke (III), and a gentle [[rondo]] that ends at a gallop (IV).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The violin begins at once with a long, smiling melody over flowing piano figures; then the piano takes the melody and the violin accompanies. A more urgent second theme brings shadows and strong accents. The movement is a [[sonata form]], and Beethoven keeps passing his ideas back and forth between the two players.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a long, rising and falling melody, first in the violin.
- Theme 2: shorter, more insistent phrases with sharp accents, in a new key.

**Summary.** A slow song without words in B-flat major. The melody returns several times, more richly decorated each time, with the two instruments taking turns to sing it. Near the close, quiet repeated figures flutter like birdsong.

### III. Scherzo: Allegro molto

**Summary.** One of the shortest movements in all Beethoven's sonatas, and a joke. The piano plays a short, bouncing phrase, and the violin plays the same phrase a beat behind, so that the two sound out of step. The middle section, the [[trio]], is a quick run of scales.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: a bouncing phrase, with the violin apparently one beat late.
- The trio: fast scales running up and down in both instruments.

### IV. Rondo: Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** A gentle, unhurried [[rondo]]: a friendly refrain that comes back between contrasting episodes. Once it returns in a surprising, distant key before finding its way home. Near the end the refrain becomes a lively galloping variation.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a relaxed, lyrical tune, first in the piano, then the violin.

### Threads

- **Partners.** In all four movements the tune passes between violin and piano; neither is the accompanist for long.
- **Light and shade.** Each sunny movement has its shadow: the urgent second theme, the minor-key passage of the Adagio, the stormy episode of the finale.
- **Wit.** The scherzo's out-of-step joke and the finale's wrong-key return show the young Beethoven's humour, as in the symphonies of the same years.
