### The big picture

- Mozart wrote this short [[motet]] in June 1791; his autograph is dated 17 June. He was in the middle of *The Magic Flute*, and he died less than six months later.
- It was written for a friend, Anton Stoll, the choirmaster of the parish church of St Stephan in Baden, a spa town near Vienna, where Mozart's wife Constanze was staying while expecting their sixth child. It was meant for the feast of Corpus Christi, which celebrates the Eucharist.
- The text is a medieval Latin hymn: "Hail, true body, born of the Virgin Mary, which truly suffered and was sacrificed on the cross for mankind." Mozart set four couplets and left out the last lines.
- It is only 46 bars long, for four-part choir, strings and organ, and simple enough for a small-town choir. At the start Mozart wrote *sotto voce*: "under the voice", hushed.
- In one line: four short verses of one long, quiet prayer, darkening in the middle and rising to a gentle climax before it fades.

### I. Ave verum corpus

**Summary.** A slow, quiet hymn for choir with strings and organ. Each couplet of the text gets its own phrase. The first two are calm and simple; the third, about the wound in Christ's side, slips into darker, unexpected harmonies; the fourth, a prayer for strength at the hour of death, rises to the fullest sound of the piece and then sinks to rest.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: a long, smooth line in the sopranos that moves mostly by step, with the other voices singing the same words in chords.
- The dark turn: the harmony drifts away from the home key on "cujus latus perforatum", "whose side was pierced".
- The prayer: on "esto nobis praegustatum", "be for us a foretaste", the voices no longer move all together but enter one after another.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for how little changes in volume. The whole piece is quiet; the climax near the end feels large only because of what comes before.
2. In the third couplet, notice the moment the harmony slips away from the home key. It is the only real shadow in the piece.

**In this recording.** William Christie and Les Arts Florissants, a French ensemble that plays on period instruments, recorded the motet in Paris in November 1994 with Mozart's *Requiem*. On the Spotify album it is track 15, after the fourteen tracks of the *Requiem*.

### Threads

- **Simplicity on purpose.** Mozart wrote for a parish choir, not for virtuosos: short phrases, plain chords, and for most of the piece all four voices singing the same words together.
- **A late work.** It belongs to Mozart's last year, between *The Magic Flute* and the unfinished *Requiem*. The scholar Christoph Wolff hears in it gestures and textures that look ahead to the *Requiem*.
