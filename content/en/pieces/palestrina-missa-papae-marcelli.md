### The big picture

- A [[mass]] is the main service of the Catholic Church, and composers set five of its fixed texts to music: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus with Benedictus, and Agnus Dei. Palestrina, choirmaster at the great churches of Rome, wrote more than a hundred masses. This one, for six unaccompanied voices, is the most famous.
- It is named after Pope Marcellus II, who reigned for only three weeks in 1555. On Good Friday of that year he called the papal singers together and told them that the music of Holy Week should suit the day, and that the words should be heard and understood. Nobody knows whether the Mass was written in his memory. It was probably composed around 1562 and was printed in Rome in 1567, in Palestrina's Second Book of Masses.
- A legend grew around it. The Council of Trent, the great church council that ended in 1563, was said to be ready to ban music in many parts because the words were lost in the tangle of voices, until this Mass showed that [[polyphony]] could be clear. The story was first written down in 1607 and became famous in the 19th century. Historians have found no evidence for it: the Council never banned polyphony.
- What is true is that the Mass makes its words unusually easy to follow. In the long texts, the Gloria and the Credo, the voices often sing together in the same rhythm, in plain chords, and Palestrina keeps changing which voices sing each phrase.
- In one line: a woven plea for mercy (I), praise in clear chords (II), the long creed with its slow, hushed centre (III), the angels' "Holy" (IV), and two final pleas for mercy and peace, the second with seven voices (V–VI).

### I. Kyrie

**Summary.** "Lord, have mercy; Christ, have mercy; Lord, have mercy." Three short sections, each built the old way: one voice begins a slow, rising phrase, and the others enter one after another with the same idea, until all six are weaving around each other.

**Main ideas**

- The opening phrase: a slow leap upwards, then a gentle step-by-step return. It comes back, in different shapes, all through the Mass.
- The weave: six independent lines, two of them tenors and two basses, so the sound is warm and full in the middle.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | One voice sings a slow, rising phrase; the others take it up one by one until the sound is full | "Kyrie eleison" ("Lord, have mercy"): voices entering in imitation, the heart of [[polyphony]] |
| ≈ 1:20 | A pause, then a new beginning, a little lighter, with a new phrase passed from voice to voice | "Christe eleison" ("Christ, have mercy") |
| ≈ 2:55 | A third section starts; the lines move a little more and the sound grows richer | "Kyrie eleison" again |
| Near the end | All six voices settle together on one long, calm chord | The end of the Kyrie |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow one entry: when a new voice comes in, it sings the same rising idea as the one before, a little higher or lower. Try to catch four or five of them.

### II. Gloria

**Summary.** The hymn of praise, a long text. Palestrina sets most of it in plain chords, all the voices pronouncing each syllable together, so that the words come through. Instead of entering one by one, groups of voices answer each other: higher voices, then lower, then everyone.

**Main ideas**

- Chords that speak: the voices move together, word by word, in the rhythm of the Latin.
- Changing groups: three or four voices sing one phrase, a different group the next, and all six join for the most important words.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The choir sings in full chords, everyone on the same syllables at the same time | "Et in terra pax" ("and on earth peace"). The first words, "Gloria in excelsis Deo", belong to the priest and are sung to chant; Palestrina's music starts after them |
| ≈ 0:40 | Short phrases thrown between higher and lower groups of voices | "Laudamus te" ("we praise you") and "Gratias agimus tibi" ("we give you thanks") |
| ≈ 1:45 | A smaller group, then the full choir again | "Domine Deus" ("Lord God"), the titles of God and of Christ |
| ≈ 2:30 | The music becomes slower and more serious, the chords heavier | "Qui tollis peccata mundi" ("you who take away the sins of the world"), a plea for mercy inside the hymn |
| ≈ 4:20 | Brighter, more confident chords | "Quoniam tu solus sanctus" ("for you alone are holy") |
| ≈ 4:55 | The voices begin to move more freely again, overlapping, towards a long "Amen" | "Cum Sancto Spiritu… Amen" ("with the Holy Spirit… Amen") |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the words rather than the tune. Even without knowing Latin, you can hear each syllable land with all the voices at once. That is [[homophony]], and it is what made the Mass famous.

### III. Credo

**Summary.** The creed, the longest text of the Mass. Like the Gloria it moves mostly in clear chords, with ever-changing groups of voices for each phrase. In the middle the music slows almost to a stop for the birth of Christ and darkens for the crucifixion, then lifts for the resurrection.

**Main ideas**

- Phrase by phrase: each short sentence of the creed gets its own group of voices and a clear ending.
- The slow centre: "Et incarnatus est" ("and he was made flesh") in long, quiet chords.
- The Crucifixus: a smaller, lighter group of voices, as is traditional for this text.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Full chords, words sung together | "Patrem omnipotentem" ("the Father almighty"). As in the Gloria, the opening words, "Credo in unum Deum", belong to the priest |
| ≈ 1:00 | Higher and lower groups answer each other, phrase by phrase | "Deum de Deo, lumen de lumine" ("God from God, light from light") |
| ≈ 2:15 | Everything slows down; long, hushed chords | "Et incarnatus est" ("and he was made flesh"). The emotional centre of the creed |
| ≈ 3:15 | Fewer voices, a thinner, quieter sound | "Crucifixus" ("he was crucified") |
| ≈ 4:15 | The full choir returns with more movement and energy | "Et resurrexit" and "Et ascendit in caelum" ("he rose again… and ascended into heaven") |
| ≈ 7:10 | The voices begin to overlap again, building towards the end | "Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum" ("and I look for the resurrection of the dead"), then "Amen" |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the voices in each phrase. Palestrina almost never uses the same group twice in a row; the colour of the sound changes from line to line.

### IV. Sanctus and Benedictus

**Summary.** "Holy, holy, holy": the song of the angels. Here the voices weave again, entering one after another, and the music moves in short phrases that come to rest, then in longer ones. After a joyful "Hosanna", a smaller group sings the Benedictus, and the Hosanna returns.

**Main ideas**

- The Sanctus: slow, overlapping entries, each phrase coming to a resting point before the next begins.
- The Hosanna: a brighter, more lively shout of praise.
- The Benedictus: fewer voices, gentler and more intimate.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Voices enter one after another in long, slow notes; short phrases settle and begin again | "Sanctus, sanctus, sanctus" ("Holy, holy, holy") |
| ≈ 1:50 | The lines move faster, overlapping more busily | "Pleni sunt caeli et terra gloria tua" ("heaven and earth are full of your glory") |
| ≈ 2:45 | A bright, lively section | "Hosanna in excelsis" ("Hosanna in the highest") |
| ≈ 3:15 | A smaller group of voices, softer and more delicate | "Benedictus qui venit" ("blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord") |
| ≈ 5:30 | The full choir again | The Hosanna returns |

**Things to notice**

1. In the Sanctus, listen for the places where the music stops to breathe. Palestrina lets short phrases come to rest on different chords, which keeps the music moving without a regular beat.

### V. Agnus Dei I

**Summary.** "Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world, have mercy on us." The Mass returns to the woven style of the Kyrie, and even to its opening phrase: one voice begins, the others follow, and the six lines flow slowly towards a quiet close.

**Main ideas**

- The return: the opening of the Agnus Dei echoes the opening of the Kyrie.
- A long, unbroken flow, with hardly a pause from beginning to end.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A slow rising phrase passed from voice to voice, as at the very beginning of the Mass | "Agnus Dei" ("Lamb of God"), on the Kyrie's opening idea |
| ≈ 1:15 | The lines weave more densely, the sound at its fullest | "Qui tollis peccata mundi" ("who takes away the sins of the world") |
| ≈ 2:20 | The music turns softer and more inward | "Miserere nobis" ("have mercy on us") |
| Near the end | The voices slow down onto a long, calm chord | The end of the first Agnus Dei |

**Things to notice**

1. Think back to the first minute of the Kyrie. The same leap and step-by-step return come back here, nearly half an hour later, and close the circle.

### VI. Agnus Dei II

**Summary.** The last prayer, ending "dona nobis pacem", "grant us peace". Palestrina adds a seventh voice, and hides inside the texture a [[canon]]: three of the voices sing the same melody, each starting a little after the other. The sound is the richest of the whole Mass, and the slowest to fade.

**Main ideas**

- Seven voices instead of six, now with two soprano parts at the top.
- The hidden canon: one melody sung three times over, overlapping, beginning with the Mass's opening idea.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Voices enter one by one; the texture is thicker and brighter than before | "Agnus Dei", now in seven parts |
| ≈ 1:10 | A dense, glowing web of lines; one melody can be heard returning in different voices | The canon at work |
| ≈ 2:20 | The words change; the lines broaden | "Dona nobis pacem" ("grant us peace") |
| Last 30 seconds | The seven voices draw out the last chord | The end of the Mass |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to follow the top line, then a middle one. Somewhere in the weave, the same melody is being sung by three voices at once, each a little behind the other.

**In this recording.** The Sixteen recorded the Mass in a north London church in November 1989, on the same album as their Allegri *Miserere*. They sing without instruments, as the papal choir did in Palestrina's time.

### Threads

- **Clear words.** In the long texts, the Gloria and the Credo, the voices often sing together so the words come through; in the short ones, the Kyrie, Sanctus and Agnus Dei, they weave freely. That balance is the reason for the Mass's reputation.
- **A circle.** The Mass begins and ends with the same rising idea, and the last movement grows from six voices to seven.
- **The legend.** The story that this Mass saved church music is a legend, but it shows how the Mass was heard for centuries: as the model of music that serves its words.
