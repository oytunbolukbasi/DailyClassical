### The big picture

- Beethoven sketched the sonata in late 1803, just before starting work on his opera *Fidelio*, and finished it in 1804. In August 1804 he offered it to the publisher Breitkopf & Härtel together with the sonatas Op. 54 and the *Appassionata*. It belongs to the years of the *Eroica* Symphony, when his music grew bigger and bolder.
- It is dedicated to Count Ferdinand von Waldstein, one of his first patrons in Bonn. When the young Beethoven left Bonn for Vienna in 1792, Waldstein wrote in his album: "receive Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands". It is the only work Beethoven dedicated to him, though by 1804 the two seem to have had little contact.
- Beethoven first wrote a long, gentle slow movement. According to his pupil Ferdinand Ries, a friend said the sonata was too long; Beethoven was furious, then agreed. He replaced it with a short *Introduzione* and published the original separately as the *Andante favori*.
- The sonata asks a great deal of the pianist and of the instrument: rumbling repeated chords, long trills, runs in both hands and, in the last minutes, scales in octaves marked to be played as glissandos. Many writers have heard it as orchestral, almost a concerto without orchestra.
- In one line: a quiet engine starts and builds (I), a dark, hushed doorway (II), a sunrise and a dance (III).

### I. Allegro con brio

**Summary.** The sonata begins with no tune at all, only soft chords repeated low on the piano, like an engine starting. Out of that pulse grows a [[sonata form]] of great energy. The contrast comes from a calm, hymn-like second theme in an unexpected key.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: quick, quiet repeated chords in the low-middle register, with short answering flicks high up.
- Theme 2: a slow, hymn-like tune in chords, marked *dolce e molto legato*, in E major.

2. Compare the hymn's two appearances: in E major in the first half, in A major after the return. The same tune seems to change colour with the key.

**In this recording.** Kempff recorded the sonata in September 1964 in the Beethovensaal in Hanover, for his stereo cycle of all 32 sonatas. He does not take the repeat of the exposition, so the first movement lasts about eight and a half minutes; pianists who repeat it, Pollini among them, take about ten.

### II. Introduzione. Adagio molto

**Summary.** A short, dark, very quiet slow movement that sounds like the door to the finale rather than a movement of its own. Deep chords and a hesitant rhythm wander through distant keys; a brief singing line appears, and the music sinks onto a long-held note that leads straight into the rondo.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: deep, soft chords with a short, dotted figure, moving through strange harmonies.
- The song: a brief, singing melody in the middle of the keyboard.

### III. Rondo. Allegretto moderato – Prestissimo

**Summary.** Out of the hush, a simple, glowing tune rises in the middle of the piano, held in a haze of pedal. This is the "dawn" of the Italian nickname. The tune returns again and again in a [[rondo]], growing to full brilliance, and the movement ends in a breathless *Prestissimo*.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a slow-moving, folk-like tune, played by the left hand crossing over the right, over a low bass note that keeps ringing.
- The stormy episodes: driving triplets, a fierce passage in A minor, and a central section in C minor built on hammered octaves.
- The *Prestissimo*: the theme sped up, with scales in octaves and a long trill.

1. Listen to the haze in the first minute. Beethoven marks long stretches with the pedal held down, so the harmonies blur into each other like light through mist.
2. In the *Prestissimo*, listen for the moment the hands seem to slide along the keyboard. These are the octave glissandos, passed between the two hands.

### Threads

- **Pulse before melody.** The first movement starts with rhythm alone; the finale's theme grows out of a held haze. Beethoven makes sound and motion as important as tunes.
- **Bright, distant keys.** The hymn of the first movement appears in E major and A major, far from home, which is why it sounds so luminous.
- **An orchestra in one piano.** Rumbling repeated chords, long trills, and runs in both hands at once: the sonata treats the piano as an orchestra.
