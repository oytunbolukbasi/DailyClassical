### The big picture

- Stalin died on 5 March 1953. Shostakovich had written no symphony since his Ninth in 1945; three years later, in 1948, his music had been officially condemned. The Tenth was first played in Leningrad on 17 December 1953, by the Leningrad Philharmonic under Evgeny Mravinsky.
- Shostakovich said he wrote it between July and October 1953, but some of its ideas are older: there are sketches from 1946, and one friend said it was finished in 1951.
- In *Testimony*, a memoir published in 1979 by Solomon Volkov, Shostakovich is quoted as saying the violent second movement is a portrait of Stalin. The book's authenticity is disputed, and nothing from 1953 confirms that reading.
- What is certain is a personal code. In the third and fourth movements Shostakovich signs the music with his own initials as notes, the [[DSCH]] [[motif]], and a horn call spells out the name of Elmira Nazirova, a young pianist and former pupil he was writing to at the time.
- In one line: a long, dark meditation (I), four minutes of violence (II), a coded dance of two names (III), and a finale where the composer's signature wins (IV).

### I. Moderato

**Summary.** The longest movement, slow and brooding. It grows from a quiet phrase in the low strings into an enormous climax and sinks back again. Two lonely woodwind melodies, one on the clarinet and one on the flute, carry most of the musical thought. It is in a free [[sonata form]].

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a slow, winding phrase in cellos and basses, quiet and grave.
- The clarinet theme: a long, sad, wandering melody.
- The flute's tune: a hesitant little dance, a kind of sad [[waltz]].

1. The movement's loudest point comes after about a quarter of an hour of slow growth. Listen to how gradually the tension rises.
2. The ending is given to the highest instruments in the orchestra, two piccolos, with almost nothing in between them and the bass.

**In this recording.** Karajan conducted this symphony for Shostakovich in Moscow in May 1969, on a Berlin Philharmonic tour, and the composer came on stage at the end. This is the second of his two studio recordings of it, made in Berlin in February 1981.

### II. Allegro

**Summary.** Four minutes of relentless speed and violence. Short, stabbing phrases, rushing strings and pounding drums never let up. It is the movement that *Testimony* calls a portrait of Stalin.

**Main ideas**

- The rush: fast strings in a hammering rhythm, with a snare drum driving it on.
- The shrieks: piercing, off-beat cries in the woodwinds and brass.

### III. Allegretto

**Summary.** A strange, wry dance, part waltz, part nocturne. Two four- and five-note codes run through it: Shostakovich's own initials, DSCH, and a horn call that spells the name Elmira. They take turns, as in a conversation, until the end.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a quiet, slightly mechanical tune in the strings.
- DSCH: four notes, D, E-flat, C, B, often in the woodwinds. Shostakovich's signature.
- The horn call: five notes, E, A, E, D, A, played twelve times by a solo horn. In note names it spells "E-La-Mi-Re-A", Elmira.

**Things to notice**

1. Count the horn calls. There are twelve, always the same five notes.
2. Listen for who answers whom. When the horn has called, the woodwinds often reply with DSCH.

### IV. Andante – Allegro

**Summary.** A slow introduction with long, lonely woodwind solos, then a fast, light-footed dance that slowly gathers energy. At its height the whole orchestra hammers out DSCH. After a quiet, uncertain passage the fast music returns, and the symphony ends in E major with the timpani pounding the composer's signature.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: long solos on oboe, flute and bassoon over low strings.
- The Allegro: a quick, almost cheerful dance tune in the violins.
- DSCH: now in the full orchestra and, at the end, on the timpani.

2. The finale's dance tune sounds cheerful. Ask yourself whether it stays cheerful when DSCH breaks in.

### Threads

- **A signature.** The DSCH motif runs through the second half of the symphony and closes it. Shostakovich used it again in later works, most famously the String Quartet No. 8 (1960).
- **Two names.** In the third movement Shostakovich's name and Elmira Nazirova's horn call alternate like a dialogue. In letters to her he described the horn call himself.
- **Dark to light.** The symphony starts in the low strings in E minor and ends in E major. Whether the ending is victory or defiance is for the listener to decide.
- **After Stalin.** Whatever its private story, the Tenth was the first symphony Shostakovich released after Stalin's death, and its premiere was followed by a long public debate in the Composers' Union about whether it was too gloomy.
