### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the concerto in Vienna in 1796, at 64, soon after his return from London. It was for Anton Weidinger, a Viennese trumpeter who later joined the imperial court trumpeters, and who had been working on a new kind of instrument: a [[keyed trumpet]], with holes in the tube covered by keys.
- The ordinary trumpet of the time had no valves or keys. It could play only the natural notes of its tube, which are far apart in the low and middle range and close together only at the top. Trumpet melodies therefore had to be written very high, as in Bach's music. With keys, Weidinger could play every note of the scale in every register, and Haydn wrote him tunes in the middle and low range that no trumpeter had played before.
- Weidinger gave the first performance on 28 March 1800 at the old Burgtheater in Vienna, almost four years after the concerto was written.
- The keyed trumpet did not last: its tone was criticised, and within a few decades instruments with valves replaced it. Haydn's concerto, played today on the modern trumpet, has been called possibly his most popular concerto.
- In one line: a bright, ceremonial Allegro (I), a gentle, swaying song (II), a quick, cheerful finale (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra plays a long introduction that sets out the themes, with horns, trumpets and drums adding a festive glow. Then the solo trumpet enters with the main tune, and later shows what the new instrument can do: scales that move by small steps, notes in the middle range, smooth melodies instead of fanfares. Near the end the orchestra stops for the soloist's [[cadenza]].

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a bright, stately tune in E-flat major, first in the strings.
- The new trumpet: smooth, stepwise lines and rapid runs that an old natural trumpet could not have played.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A gentle, swaying song in six beats to the bar, in A-flat major. The strings introduce it, and the trumpet sings it back, softly and smoothly, like a voice. In the middle the harmony slides into a distant key, a turn the old natural trumpet could not have followed.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a lilting, simple melody that rocks in groups of three.
- The trumpet's voice: soft and singing, far from the sound of fanfares.

### III. Finale: Allegro

**Summary.** A bright, quick finale that begins in a whisper: the violins play a cheeky little tune, and then the whole orchestra bursts in with it. The trumpet joins with the same tune and spends the rest of the movement trading it with the orchestra, adding quick runs along the way.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a lively, skipping melody, first quietly in the violins.
- The trumpet's flourishes: fast runs that show off the new keys.

### Threads

- **A new instrument.** Every movement shows off what the keyed trumpet could do: smooth tunes in the middle range, full scales, notes outside the old trumpet's natural series.
- **Festival and song.** The outer movements keep the trumpet's old ceremonial character; the Andante turns it into a singer.
- **Haydn in Vienna.** The concerto belongs to the years after London, when Haydn was back in Vienna and famous across Europe.
