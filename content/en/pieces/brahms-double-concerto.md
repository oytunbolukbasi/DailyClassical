### The big picture

- Brahms wrote the concerto in the summer of 1887 at Lake Thun in Switzerland. It was his last work for orchestra. The first performance was in Cologne on 18 October 1887, with the violinist Joseph Joachim, the cellist Robert Hausmann and Brahms conducting.
- Brahms and Joachim had been friends for more than thirty years, but they fell out when Joachim divorced his wife, Amalie, and Brahms took her side. The concerto was in part a peace offering. Brahms gave Joachim the manuscript inscribed "To him for whom it was written".
- Two soloists share the stage, and Brahms makes them sound like partners. They pass phrases back and forth, play in octaves, and sometimes run their scales together so that they sound like one huge string instrument.
- The early reception was cool; even Clara Schumann found it "not brilliant for the instruments". Today it is a favourite of violin and cello pairs.
- In one line: a dramatic dialogue that starts with two cadenzas (I), a warm song in octaves (II), a Hungarian-flavoured rondo that ends in A major (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra barely starts before it stops, and the cello speaks alone in a long [[cadenza]]; then the violin joins it. Only after this double introduction does the orchestra set out the themes properly. The movement is a [[sonata form]] with a [[double exposition]], and the two soloists spend it in close, sometimes stormy, conversation.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a strong, falling theme with a long-short rhythm, first in the full orchestra and then in the cello.
- Theme 2: a tender, sighing melody first hinted at by the violin in its cadenza.
- The syncopated repeated notes: an urgent, off-beat figure that drives the transitions.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A short, warm song. After a horn call, violin and cello play the melody together, an octave apart, as if they were one voice. A middle section for the woodwinds is calm and hymn-like; the violin then wanders in a gentle daydream before the song returns.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: two rising notes, answered by the woodwinds.
- The song: a broad, arching melody for violin and cello in octaves.
- The middle section: a quiet, hymn-like tune for flutes, clarinets and bassoons.

### III. Vivace non troppo

**Summary.** A [[rondo]] with a Hungarian flavour. The cello starts the jaunty main theme, the violin repeats it, and the orchestra joins in with full force. Between its returns come a noble, stately theme and a dramatic central section. The minor turns to A major at the end.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a jaunty, slightly gypsy-style tune with a long-short-short rhythm.
- The noble theme: broad and stately, first played by the cello in [[double stop]]s.

### Threads

- **Two voices, one friendship.** Violin and cello argue, answer and finally join in octaves. Brahms wrote it for Joachim, the friend he had lost, and for the cellist of Joachim's quartet.
- **A–E–F.** The concerto uses the notes A, E and F, a reordering of F–A–E, Joachim's old motto "free but lonely". In 1853 Brahms had used those notes in a sonata for Joachim written together with Robert Schumann and Albert Dietrich.
- **The last orchestral work.** After this Brahms wrote no more symphonies or concertos. He turned to chamber music, piano pieces and songs.
