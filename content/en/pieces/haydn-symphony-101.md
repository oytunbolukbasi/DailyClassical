### The big picture

- Haydn made two long visits to London, in 1791–92 and 1794–95, invited by the violinist and concert manager Johann Peter Salomon, and wrote twelve symphonies for London audiences. No. 101, the ninth, was mostly written in Vienna before the journey; only the first movement, on English paper, was composed after Haydn reached London early in 1794. It was first played on 3 March 1794 at the Hanover Square Rooms, with Salomon leading the orchestra and Haydn at the keyboard.
- It was a triumph. The first two movements were encored, and a newspaper wrote that the connoisseurs thought it his best work. The *Morning Chronicle* added: "Nothing can be more original than the subject of the first movement".
- The nickname is not Haydn's. It comes from the slow movement's steady "tick-tock" accompaniment: a piano arrangement of that movement published in Vienna in 1798 was already called *Rondo, Die Uhr* (The Clock), and in the 19th century the name passed to the whole symphony. There is a real clock in the story too: Haydn arranged the minuet for a musical clock built in 1793 by Father Primitivus Niemecz, the Esterházy court librarian.
- It is scored for pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, horns and trumpets, with timpani and strings. The clarinets may be a later addition: they are missing from Haydn's manuscript and from the London orchestral parts, and appear first in copies made in Vienna after his return.
- In one line: a dark doorway into a racing Presto (I), the ticking clock (II), a grand minuet with a clumsy village band in the middle (III), a quick finale with a fugue inside it (IV).

### I. Adagio – Presto

**Summary.** A slow, hushed introduction in D minor, opening with a quiet rising scale, leads into a fast Presto in a bouncing six-time. The Presto's main tune begins with the same rising scale, now quick and light in the violins. It is a [[sonata form]] full of energy, in which nearly everything grows from that first idea.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: slow, shadowy, starting with a rising scale low in the strings.
- Theme 1: a quick, skipping tune that runs up a scale, first in the violins alone.
- Theme 2: the same skipping rhythm, but running down instead of up, in A major.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the very first notes of the slow introduction, then to the start of the Presto. The same rising scale opens both, first slow and dark, then fast and bright.

**In this recording.** Herbert von Karajan conducts the Berlin Philharmonic, recorded in August 1971 in the French Church at St. Moritz, where the orchestra spent its summer sessions. His tempos are broad; the ticking Andante, for example, takes almost nine minutes. On the Spotify album the symphony is tracks 5–8, after Symphony No. 83, "La poule".

### II. Andante

**Summary.** The clock itself. Bassoons and plucked strings keep a steady tick-tock while the violins play a graceful tune above. In the middle the clock is swept aside by a loud, fierce passage in G minor. When the tick returns, Haydn keeps changing the clock: high and low, quiet and loud, and once he stops it altogether before starting again in an unexpected key.

**Main ideas**

- The tick: short, detached notes in the bassoons with plucked second violins and basses, an even "tick-tock".
- The tune: a light, elegant melody in the first violins, with little grace notes.
- The minor section: loud, stormy music in G minor for the whole orchestra.

### III. Menuetto: Allegretto – Trio

**Summary.** A large, stately [[minuet]], one of the longest Haydn wrote, with trumpets and drums. In the middle, the [[trio]] turns rustic: a solo flute plays a tune over strings that strum the same chord again and again, and at one point the strings stay on their chord when the tune has already moved on, like a village band that misses the change.

**Main ideas**

- The minuet: a broad, ceremonial dance in three for the full orchestra.
- The trio: a light flute tune over a stubborn, repeated chord in the strings.

### IV. Finale: Vivace

**Summary.** A quick, bright finale built almost entirely from one tune, which comes back again and again, each time changed. Halfway through, Haydn turns it into a [[fugue]]: the strings start it very quietly, one part after another, and it builds to a blaze for the whole orchestra.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a quick, cheerful melody in the violins that keeps returning.
- The fugue: the same tune, used as the subject of a quiet fugue in the strings.

### Threads

- **Scales.** A rising scale opens the introduction and the Presto; a running scale opens the second theme. Haydn builds much of the first movement from the simplest material there is.
- **Haydn's jokes.** A clock that stops and restarts in the wrong key, a band that misses its chord, a lively tune that suddenly becomes a learned fugue. The humour is part of the design.
- **Music for a big hall.** Trumpets and drums, and loud tuttis that burst in after quiet beginnings: this is Haydn writing for the large orchestra and audience of London.
