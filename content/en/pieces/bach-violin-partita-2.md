### The big picture

- Bach wrote out the six *Sei Solo a Violino senza Basso accompagnato*, "six solos for violin without bass accompaniment", in a fair copy dated 1720, at Köthen, where he was music director to Prince Leopold. Three are sonatas; three, including this one, are partitas, Bach's word for a [[suite]] of dances.
- The partita has four dances, [[allemande]], [[courante]] (here Italian *Corrente*), [[sarabande]] and [[gigue]] (*Giga*), followed by a [[chaconne]] (*Ciaccona*), a long chain of [[variation]]s on a four-bar phrase. The Chaconne lasts about as long as the four dances together.
- A violin plays one line at a time, but Bach asks it for chords of three and four notes and for several voices at once. Brahms, who arranged the Chaconne for piano left hand, wrote to Clara Schumann that in it Bach puts on one stave "a whole world of the deepest thoughts and most powerful feelings".
- In the summer of 1720, while Bach was away with the prince at the spa of Carlsbad, his first wife, Maria Barbara, died suddenly. The musicologist Helga Thoene has argued that the Chaconne is a memorial to her. It is a theory, and a disputed one.
- In one line: four dances in D minor (I–IV), then a set of variations that passes from minor to major and back (V).

### V. Ciaccona

**Summary.** A [[chaconne]]: a short phrase of four bars and its harmonies, repeated more than sixty times while everything above it changes. The violin begins with heavy chords, dissolves them into running notes and broken chords, turns to the major for a long, calmer middle section, and then returns to the minor and to the chords of the opening.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: slow, solemn chords in a four-bar phrase, with the weight on the second beat, like a [[sarabande]].
- The variations: the same harmonies dressed in quicker and quicker notes, runs, leaps and broken chords.
- The major section: a quieter, warmer world in D major, in the middle of the piece.

2. Listen for the moment the music turns to D major, roughly halfway. Many listeners hear it as a sudden light.

**In this recording.** Milstein recorded all six sonatas and partitas for Deutsche Grammophon in Conway Hall, London, in 1973, when he was in his late sixties; the set was released in 1975 and won a Grammy Award. It was his second recording of the set. He takes every repeat in the dances, which is why they last about four minutes each.

### Threads

- **One instrument, many voices.** In every movement the violin suggests more than one part: a bass under the melody in the dances, three and four voices at once in the Sarabanda and the Chaconne.
- **Dances, then something larger.** The four dances follow the usual order of a suite. The Chaconne, also a dance in origin, grows into a piece as long as the other four together.
- **D minor and its light.** Every movement is in D minor, and the dances only pass through other keys on the way. The turn to D major in the middle of the Chaconne, more than twenty minutes in, is the first time the music settles in the major.
