### The big picture

- *Swan Lake* was Tchaikovsky's first ballet. The Bolshoi Theatre in Moscow commissioned it in 1875, and he wrote it between August 1875 and April 1876. He took the work on, he told Rimsky-Korsakov, "partly for the money" and partly because he had long wanted to try this kind of music.
- The first production, at the Bolshoi on 4 March 1877, was poorly received: critics found the score too noisy and too symphonic for dancing. It still ran for six years. The *Swan Lake* that most companies dance today comes from a new production in St Petersburg in January 1895, after the composer's death, with choreography by Marius Petipa and Lev Ivanov and the score reordered and cut by the conductor Riccardo Drigo.
- The story, in the 1877 version: on the eve of his coming of age Prince Siegfried must choose a bride. Hunting by a moonlit lake, he meets Odette, a girl who is a swan by day and human only at night; only a vow of true love can free her. At the ball he is tricked into swearing his love to Odile, who looks just like her, and in the last act the lovers are swallowed by the stormy lake.
- A single melody, the swan theme, ties the ballet together and returns at every turning point of the story: a [[leitmotif]], as in opera.
- This guide is a selection: six of the ballet's numbers, one from Act 1, two from Act 2, two from Act 3 and the finale, enough to follow the story from the party to the lake. On the album, which has the complete ballet in 31 tracks, they are tracks 2, 10, 13, 24, 26 and 31. The complete ballet lasts about two and a half hours.
- In one line: a party in the park (I), the lake at night (II), the swans' dances and a love duet (III), a dance from the ball (IV), the betrayal (V) and the storm and the end (VI).

### I. Act 1, No. 2: Waltz

**Summary.** The ballet opens in the park of a castle, where Siegfried and his friends are celebrating. Peasants come to congratulate him, and his old tutor asks them to dance. This is the grand [[waltz]] they dance: nearly 400 bars of it, built from one sweeping tune and several lighter ones. It is the music of a carefree life that Siegfried is about to lose.

**Main ideas**

- The waltz tune: a long, swinging melody in the violins, rising and falling over a firm oom-pah-pah.
- The lighter tunes: shorter, more playful phrases passed between woodwinds and strings.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how often the tune seems about to end and then sets off again. Tchaikovsky writes it for dancers who need long, unbroken phrases, but he also keeps changing the colours underneath.

### II. Act 2, No. 10: Scene

**Summary.** The curtain rises on a wild mountain landscape. In the moonlight a lake shimmers by the ruins of a chapel, and a swan wearing a crown leads the others across the water. Over shimmering strings and rippling harp, a solo oboe plays the swan theme, the most famous melody of the ballet, in B minor. It grows until the whole orchestra takes it up, then sinks back into the night.

**Main ideas**

- The swan theme: a sad, rising and falling melody for the oboe, longing more than menacing.
- The setting: trembling strings and harp ripples, the sound of water under the moon.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Remember the oboe's tune. In the 1877 score it is first heard at the end of Act 1, as the swans fly over the park, and it comes back at the key moments of Acts 2, 3 and 4.

### III. Act 2, No. 13: Dances of the Swans

**Summary.** Siegfried has met Odette and learned of the spell: she and her companions are swans by day and girls only at night. Now the swans dance. The number is a chain of seven dances on one track: three waltzes for the whole flock, two solos, a small dance for the youngest swans and, at its centre, a long love duet for Odette and Siegfried, a [[pas d'action]] with solo violin and cello. Tchaikovsky took the duet's melody from *Undina*, an opera he had abandoned.

**Main ideas**

- The swans' waltz: graceful, gliding music for the whole flock.
- The little swans: a quick, pattering dance over staccato bassoons, often danced by four swans holding hands.
- The love duet: a slow, singing melody for solo violin over harp chords, later shared with a solo cello.

**Things to notice**

1. The dance of the little swans is the most famous minute of the ballet. Listen to the bassoons underneath: the dry, ticking notes are what make it sound so precise.
2. In the love duet, follow the two solo voices. The violin speaks first; when the cello enters, the music becomes a conversation, as in the opera duet it came from.

**In this recording.** On this album the seven dances of No. 13 are one track of almost eighteen minutes, with short pauses between the dances. The violin solo is played by Ida Haendel. Track 29, "Dance of the Little Swans", is a different dance from Act 4; the famous one is here, in Act 2.

### IV. Act 3, No. 22: Neapolitan Dance

**Summary.** The ball in the castle. Guests from many countries arrive, and dances from Hungary, Spain, Naples and Poland follow one another. The Neapolitan Dance is built on an Italian folk tune: a solo cornet sings it over the strings, then the music speeds up into a whirling [[tarantella]] with tambourine. Tchaikovsky later arranged it as the "Neapolitan Song" in his *Children's Album* for piano.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a sunny, swaying melody for the cornet, the trumpet's softer cousin.
- The tarantella: the same tune, faster and faster, with tambourine.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A short, bright opening | The dancers come forward |
| ≈ 0:10 | A solo cornet sings a sunny, swaying tune over a light accompaniment | The Italian folk tune |
| ≈ 0:55 | The tune again, with more instruments around it | The dance gathers pace |
| ≈ 1:15 | Suddenly very fast; the tambourine joins in | *Presto*: a [[tarantella]] |
| Last 20 seconds | Faster still, to a sharp ending | The whirl ends in a flourish |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the cornet. Its warm, slightly brassy voice belongs to the bandstand and the street, not to the symphony orchestra, and Tchaikovsky uses it to make the dance sound like Naples.

### V. Act 3, No. 24: Scene

**Summary.** Baron von Rothbart has brought his daughter Odile to the ball, and Siegfried, struck by her likeness to Odette, has danced with her. Now he announces that he will marry her. The ball resumes with a waltz. Then the stage darkens, an owl cries, Rothbart is revealed as a demon and Odette appears at a window as a white swan. The swan theme bursts out in the full orchestra. Siegfried realises he has been tricked and rushes out of the castle.

**Main ideas**

- The waltz of the ball: bright and carefree, the world of the court.
- The swan theme: now loud and harsh in the brass, the sound of the broken vow.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Brisk, excited music | Siegfried chooses Odile |
| ≈ 0:40 | A light, festive waltz | The ball goes on: Siegfried's mother and Rothbart join the couple's hands |
| ≈ 1:50 | The waltz breaks off; the music darkens | The stage goes dark |
| ≈ 2:00 | The swan theme blares out in the brass over the whole orchestra | Odette at the window: Siegfried sees what he has done |
| Last 40 seconds | Crashing, turbulent music to the end of the act | Siegfried rushes out of the castle |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the swan theme here with the oboe's quiet version at the start of Act 2. It is the same melody, but played by the brass at full force it sounds like an accusation.

### VI. Act 4, No. 29: Final Scene

**Summary.** Back by the lake. Odette has told the swans she was betrayed, and a storm is rising when Siegfried rushes in to beg forgiveness. In the 1877 libretto he tears the crown from her head and throws it into the lake, she dies in his arms, and the waters rise and swallow them both; as they subside, swans glide on the calm surface. The music moves from a sorrowful opening through the storm to a last, transformed statement of the swan theme in B major. The opening bars come from *The Voyevoda*, an earlier opera Tchaikovsky had destroyed.

**Main ideas**

- The storm: rushing strings, stabbing brass and rolling timpani.
- The swan theme transformed: no longer in the minor but in the major, broad and radiant.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A slow, sorrowful melody in the strings | Siegfried begs Odette's forgiveness. Music from *The Voyevoda* |
| ≈ 1:00 | The music quickens; strings rush and swirl | The storm breaks over the lake |
| ≈ 2:30–3:30 | Fragments of the swan theme in the brass, buffeted by the storm | The crown is thrown into the lake; Odette dies |
| ≈ 4:15 | The swan theme rings out slowly in the major, with the full orchestra | *Moderato e maestoso*: the lovers are engulfed |
| Last minute | The music settles into broad, calm chords | The waters subside; swans on the lake |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment the swan theme changes from minor to major. It is the same melody that the oboe first sang at the lake, now heard in triumph rather than sorrow.

### Threads

- **One melody for one story.** The swan theme is heard at the lake (II), at the moment of betrayal (V) and in the final storm (VI), each time in a different colour: quiet and longing, then harsh in the brass, then radiant in the major.
- **Two worlds.** The court dances in waltzes and national dances (I, IV); the swans have their own music of harp, oboe and trembling strings (II, III). In Act 3 the two collide (V).
- **A symphonist in the theatre.** The critics of 1877 complained that the music was too symphonic. It is what makes these numbers work in the concert hall: the themes return and change, and the orchestra tells the story even without the dancers.
