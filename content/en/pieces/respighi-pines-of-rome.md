### The big picture

- Respighi, born in Bologna, moved to Rome in 1913 to teach composition. He later said that two things in the city spoke to his imagination above all: its fountains, and the umbrella-shaped pines that appear on every horizon. *Fountains of Rome* (1916) made him famous; *Pines of Rome*, finished in the summer of 1924, was its sequel.
- It is a [[symphonic poem]] in four scenes, played without a break. Respighi wrote a short note for each: children playing in the Villa Borghese; a chant rising from a catacomb; a moonlit night on the Janiculum hill with a nightingale; and an army of ancient Rome marching along the Appian Way at dawn.
- The score asks for a huge orchestra with piano, celesta, organ and extra brass, some of them placed out of sight. In the third scene it also asks for a gramophone record of a real nightingale, one of the first uses of recorded sound in a concert work.
- The premiere, at the Augusteo in Rome on 14 December 1924, was a triumph. Toscanini conducted the American premiere in New York in 1926, and it is still the most recorded of Respighi's works.
- In one line: children's games (I), a hymn underground (II), a moonlit night (III), a march from silence to a blaze (IV).

### I. The Pines of the Villa Borghese

**Summary.** Respighi's note: children play in the pine wood of the Villa Borghese, dance in rings, play at soldiers, and shriek like swallows at evening. The music is bright, high and noisy, with no low instruments at all, and it stops dead.

**Main ideas**

- The glitter: trills in the woodwind, sweeps on harp and keyboard, bells and triangle.
- The game songs: short, simple tunes like nursery rhymes, passed around the woodwind and trumpets.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A burst of high, bright sound: trilling flutes and clarinets, rushing harp and piano, bells | The children's playground. Everything is in the treble |
| ≈ 0:20 | A short, catchy tune, repeated and passed between instruments | A ring-game song. Respighi's wife Elsa later recalled singing him the songs she had sung while playing in these gardens as a child |
| ≈ 1:00–1:50 | Trumpets play bugle calls and a jaunty march; the woodwind chatter around them | The children play at soldiers |
| ≈ 1:50–2:40 | Louder and shriller, the tunes piled on top of each other | The games become wild |
| Last 15 seconds | A harsh, clashing blast from the trumpets, and then nothing | The children vanish. The next scene follows at once |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the bass. There is almost none: no low strings, no tubas, no bassoons. That is why the sound is so light.
2. The movement does not end, it is cut off. Notice the shock of the silence.

**In this recording.** Karajan recorded the *Pines* with the Berlin Philharmonic in the Philharmonie between December 1977 and February 1978, together with *Fountains of Rome*. On the Spotify album the *Fountains* come first, as tracks 1–4; the *Pines* are tracks 5–8.

### II. The Pines near a Catacomb

**Summary.** Suddenly we are in shadow, among the pines over the entrance to a catacomb, an early Christian burial place under the ground. From below rises a slow chant, solemn like a hymn. It swells to a huge climax and sinks back into the earth.

**Main ideas**

- The chant: a slow, old church melody ([[plainchant]]), first in the low strings and horns, later in the trombones.
- The distant trumpet: another chant melody, a Sanctus from the Mass, played by a trumpet out of sight.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Very low, very soft: [[muted]] strings, deep horns, a bass note from the organ that you feel more than hear | The scene changes to the catacomb |
| ≈ 0:30 | A slow, solemn tune in the horns and low strings, moving step by step | The chant, a Kyrie melody |
| ≈ 1:45–3:00 | A single trumpet, far away, plays a slow, pure melody over hushed strings | The [[offstage]] trumpet with the Sanctus chant |
| ≈ 3:15–4:30 | The chant returns in the trombones and lower brass, and the orchestra slowly gathers weight | The hymn rises from below |
| ≈ 4:30–5:30 | Full orchestra and organ, the chant at its loudest | The climax |
| Last 80 seconds | The sound sinks and darkens; the chant fades into the depths | The hymn goes back under the ground |

**Things to notice**

1. The offstage trumpet should sound truly distant. Notice how much space it seems to open up.
2. Follow the chant from its first quiet appearance to the climax. It is the same slow melody, only louder and fuller.

### III. The Pines of the Janiculum

**Summary.** A [[nocturne]]. A breath of wind stirs the pines on the Janiculum hill, lit by the full moon, and a nightingale sings. A clarinet plays as if dreaming; strings, harp and celesta make a soft, shimmering night. At the end the real nightingale, a recording, sings alone.

**Main ideas**

- The clarinet song: a long, slow, floating melody, marked *come in sogno*, "as if in a dream".
- The night sounds: soft trills, harp, celesta, [[muted]] strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A few soft, rippling notes on the piano | Night falls. The piano opens the scene |
| ≈ 0:15 | A solo clarinet, very quiet, plays a long, wandering melody | The clarinet song, "as if in a dream" |
| ≈ 1:30–3:00 | The strings take up the melody, warm and hushed; harp and celesta glint around it | The moonlit pines |
| ≈ 3:30–4:30 | The music swells to a warm, full climax, then settles | The high point of the night |
| ≈ 5:15 | A real bird song, a nightingale, over soft, held chords and trills | The recorded nightingale |
| Last 40 seconds | The bird sings almost alone as the orchestra fades | The scene dissolves into the next |

**Things to notice**

1. The nightingale is not an instrument imitating a bird. It is a recording of one, played through a loudspeaker. Respighi named the exact record in his score.
2. Listen to how quietly the clarinet begins, as if from another room.

### IV. The Pines of the Appian Way

**Summary.** Misty dawn on the Appian Way, the old military road into Rome, guarded by lonely pines. Far off, the steady tread of countless feet. The poet imagines the ancient glories: trumpets blare, and a consul's army marches towards the Capitol in the light of the rising sun. Five minutes of [[crescendo]] from almost nothing to the whole orchestra, organ and extra brass.

**Main ideas**

- The tread: a slow, heavy, regular beat in the timpani, piano and low strings, over a deep held organ note.
- The melody: a long, curling tune, first on the cor anglais.
- The buccine: ancient Roman trumpets, played by extra brass (usually flugelhorns), sometimes placed away from the orchestra.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Almost silence: a soft, steady thud in the timpani and low piano, a deep organ note underneath | The army's tread, very far away. A [[pedal note]] holds the bass |
| ≈ 0:40 | A cor anglais plays a long, winding, sad melody above the tread | The melody, in the mist |
| ≈ 1:30–2:30 | The tread grows; horns and trumpets take up the melody; the sound thickens | The army comes closer |
| ≈ 2:30–3:30 | Extra brass break in with fanfares, answered by the orchestra | The buccine, the Roman trumpets |
| ≈ 3:30–4:40 | Everyone together, louder and louder: brass, organ, cymbals, the melody hammered out | The march at full strength. The climax of the whole work |
| Last 30 seconds | A blazing major chord, held, with the full weight of the organ and the brass | The army reaches the Capitol |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the steady beat from the first second to the last. It never changes speed; only its volume grows.
2. Listen for the moment the extra brass enter. In some performances they play from offstage or from elsewhere in the hall.

### Threads

- **Four pines, four times.** Each scene is set among the pines in a different place and a different time of day: morning play, a dark underground, night, and dawn. Each also goes further back in history, from children in the modern city to the early Christians to the armies of ancient Rome.
- **Colour as the subject.** Respighi studied orchestration with Rimsky-Korsakov in Russia, and this music is above all about sound: no bass in the first scene, no light in the second, moonlight in the third, and everything at once in the last.
- **Sound from elsewhere.** A trumpet out of sight, a recorded bird, extra brass in the gallery: Respighi places some sounds outside the orchestra to make the space feel larger.
