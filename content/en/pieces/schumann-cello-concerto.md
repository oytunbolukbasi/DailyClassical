### The big picture

- In September 1850 Schumann moved to Düsseldorf as the city's music director. Within weeks, between 10 and 24 October, he wrote this concerto. On the manuscript he called it a *Konzertstück*, a concert piece, rather than a concerto.
- Clara noted in her diary in November 1850 that it seemed to her "written in true violoncello style". It was never performed in Schumann's lifetime. He cancelled a planned performance in 1852, and the score was published only in 1854.
- The first public performances came in 1860, four years after his death, with the cellist Ludwig Ebert. Sources disagree on the first: Oldenburg in April or Leipzig in June.
- Years earlier Schumann had written that he could not write a concerto for the virtuoso and must think of something else. Here the cello sings almost all the time, and the three movements run into one another without a break.
- In one line: a long, searching song (I), a short, tender intermezzo with two cellos (II), a lively finale with a [[cadenza]] that the orchestra accompanies (III).

### I. Nicht zu schnell

**Summary.** Three soft chords, and the cello begins a long, yearning melody that seems to have no end. The orchestra answers, and the cello moves on to new ideas, some related to the first, some fresh, as if improvising. There is no showy cadenza: the movement winds down and leads straight into the slow movement.

**Main ideas**

- The chords: three quiet wind chords over plucked strings, like a curtain opening.
- Theme 1: a long, rising and falling melody for the cello, warm and restless.
- Theme 2: a gentler, brighter melody in a major key.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the cello's first entry. It does not open with fireworks but with a long song that keeps reaching higher.
2. Notice what is missing at the end: no big solo cadenza and no final full stop. The music simply opens into the slow movement.

**In this recording.** Jacqueline du Pré was 23. She and Daniel Barenboim had married the year before. Her tempo in this movement is broad: 12:20, against about 10 to 12 minutes in the other recordings checked.

### II. Langsam

**Summary.** A short, intimate song in F major, more an intermezzo than a full slow movement. The soloist sings over soft plucked strings, and at one point the orchestra's principal cellist joins in, so that two cellos play together. At the end the first movement's theme comes back, and the cello answers it in a speaking, [[recitative]]-like line that speeds up into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a short, tender melody, often falling, over plucked accompaniment.
- The duet: the soloist and the orchestra's first cello, side by side.

1. Listen for the two cellos together. Schumann gives the soloist a partner from inside the orchestra, an unusual sound in a concerto.

### III. Sehr lebhaft

**Summary.** The finale is brisk and determined, with the timpani joining for the first time in the work. Near the end comes a [[cadenza]], but an unusual one: the orchestra does not fall silent and keeps accompanying the soloist. Then the music turns to A major for a bright, quick ending.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a lively, march-like tune, passed between orchestra and cello.
- Theme 2: a more singing idea for the cello.
- The cadenza: the soloist's free passage, with soft interjections from the orchestra.

### Threads

- **A song without breaks.** The three movements run on without a pause, joined by the opening chords and by the first movement's theme, which comes back before the finale.
- **Not a showpiece.** The cello is used to the full, but for singing more than display. That was one reason the work was neglected for decades after Schumann's death.
- **Düsseldorf, autumn 1850.** Straight after the concerto, from November, Schumann wrote his "Rhenish" Symphony. The two works belong to his happy first months in the city.
