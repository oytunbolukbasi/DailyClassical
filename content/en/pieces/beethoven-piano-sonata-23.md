### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it mostly in 1804 and 1805, the years of the *Eroica* Symphony and his opera *Fidelio*. It was published in Vienna in February 1807 and dedicated to his friend Count Franz von Brunsvik, a cellist.
- The name "Appassionata", "impassioned", is not his. A publisher put it on a four-hand arrangement in 1838, eleven years after Beethoven's death, and it stuck.
- It pushes the piano of the time to its limits. The first movement keeps returning to the lowest F that Beethoven's instruments had, and its closing pages sweep across almost the whole keyboard.
- Much of it is quiet. The first movement begins almost in a whisper, and the loudest outbursts come without warning out of that quiet.
- In one line: a whisper and an eruption (I), a calm hymn with variations (II), a whirlwind that ends in the minor (III).

### I. Allegro assai

**Summary.** A movement in [[sonata form]] built on contrast: whispered phrases in bare octaves, then sudden crashes of sound across the keyboard. A small knocking figure in the bass keeps threatening, and the long [[coda]] ends not in triumph but in a fading growl.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a broken chord that falls and rises in a dotted rhythm, both hands two octaves apart, very quiet. It is played again one step higher.
- The knock: four low notes, three short and one long, the rhythm that opens the Fifth Symphony.
- Theme 2: a warm, hymn-like melody in A-flat major over a pulsing bass. Its shape echoes theme 1, turned to the major.

1. Listen for the knocking figure. It keeps coming back, sometimes as a soft warning in the bass, sometimes hammered out.
2. The same theme has several faces: whispered at the start, a hymn in theme 2, a shout in the coda.

### II. Andante con moto

**Summary.** A calm set of [[variation]]s on a simple, hymn-like theme in D-flat major. Each variation moves faster than the one before, then the theme returns in its plain form. The peace is broken at the very end.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: slow chords low in the middle of the keyboard, with very little melody; each half is played twice.
- The variations: the same harmony with faster and faster notes around it, rising higher on the keyboard.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft, dark chords low in the keyboard, almost like a hymn sung by low voices | The theme |
| ≈ 1:25 | The same chords, but the left hand now plays just after the beat, giving a gentle limp | Variation 1 |
| ≈ 2:50 | A flowing line of quicker notes in the right hand, a little higher | Variation 2 |
| ≈ 4:15 | Fast rippling notes, high and bright; the hands take turns with the theme and the ripple | Variation 3 |
| ≈ 5:40 | The plain chords of the theme return, but each phrase jumps between low and high | The theme comes back |
| ≈ 6:10 | A quiet, unsettled chord, then the same chord hammered loud | The music breaks off and goes straight into the finale |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the pace of the notes. The harmony barely changes from variation to variation; what changes is how fast the notes move: slow, then twice as fast, then faster again.

**No pause.** The finale follows without a break. On Spotify it is a new track, but in the score the last chord of the Andante leads straight into the first bars of the finale.

### III. Allegro ma non troppo – Presto

**Summary.** A long, almost unbroken stream of fast notes in F minor, in [[sonata form]]. Unusually, Beethoven asks for the second half, not the first, to be played twice. A wild, faster [[coda]] brings the sonata to a hard, tragic end.

**Main ideas**

- The alarm: one loud, harsh chord hammered over and over.
- The whirl: fast notes swirling in the right hand over short left-hand chords, the main theme.
- The Presto: a new, stamping theme in heavy chords, then the whirl at top speed.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A loud chord struck again and again, then a cascade of fast notes tumbling down | The introduction: a call to arms |
| ≈ 0:16 | Quiet but restless: fast notes swirl in the right hand over light chords in the left | The main theme |
| ≈ 1:00 | The swirl grows louder, with sharp accents and a more urgent idea | The second group, in C minor |
| ≈ 1:39 | The swirl moves to new keys; its pieces are passed between the hands | The [[development]] begins. Beethoven marks everything from here to the coda to be repeated |
| ≈ 3:00 | The main theme back in F minor | The [[recapitulation]] |
| ≈ 4:20 | The music of ≈ 1:39 again | The repeat of the development and recapitulation |
| ≈ 7:04 | Faster still: a new, stamping tune in heavy chords, played twice | The Presto [[coda]] |
| Near the end | The whirl at full speed, then broken chords crashing down to the bottom of the keyboard | The end, in F minor |

**Things to notice**

1. The finale hardly stops for breath. Try to find a moment when the fast notes are not running.
2. Listen for the Presto's new tune. It appears only once, and it sounds like a stamping dance.

**In this recording.** Gilels takes the long repeat of the second half, which is why his finale runs close to eight minutes. Without it, the movement lasts about five and a half.

### Threads

- **Quiet and loud.** All three movements live on sudden contrasts. The softest passages come right before the loudest.
- **Low notes.** The low F in the first movement, the deep hymn in the second, the plunge to the bottom of the keyboard at the end: the sonata keeps going down.
- **No way out.** The Andante's hymn is broken by a single chord, and the finale ends in the minor. The calm at the centre does not last.
