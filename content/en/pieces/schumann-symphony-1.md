### The big picture

- Until 1840 Schumann wrote almost only for the piano, and then, in the year of his marriage to Clara Wieck, mostly songs. Clara wanted more. She wrote in her diary that his imagination could not find enough room on the piano and that her dearest wish was for him to compose for orchestra.
- He sketched this symphony in four days, 23 to 26 January 1841, and finished the scoring by 20 February. Mendelssohn conducted the first performance in Leipzig on 31 March 1841, with the Gewandhaus Orchestra. It was warmly received.
- The idea of spring came from a poem by Adolf Böttger that ends with the words "in the valley, spring blooms forth". Schumann first gave the movements titles: "The Beginning of Spring", "Evening", "Merry Playmates" and "Spring in Full Bloom". He removed them before publication.
- Later he wrote to a conductor that the first trumpet call should sound "as if it came from on high, like a summons to awakening", and that the introduction might suggest the world turning green. He added that these images came to him only after the music was finished.
- In one line: winter breaking into spring (I), an evening song (II), a sturdy dance with two contrasting episodes (III), spring in full bloom (IV).

### I. Andante un poco maestoso – Allegro molto vivace

**Summary.** Trumpets and horns call out a short fanfare, and the whole orchestra answers. A slow, mysterious introduction follows, then the music speeds up, and the fanfare's rhythm becomes the quick main theme. It is a [[sonata form]] movement full of energy, with a new, singing tune saved for the end.

**Main ideas**

- The fanfare: a short, bold call in trumpets and horns, in the rhythm of the poem's last line.
- The Allegro theme: the same rhythm, now fast and bouncing, for the whole orchestra.
- Theme 2: a gentle, swaying tune in the woodwind.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** The "Evening" movement: a long, warm song passed from the violins to the cellos and then to the woodwind. At the very end the trombones enter softly with a quiet, falling phrase. It leads straight into the [[scherzo]].

**Main ideas**

- The song: a long, singing melody, first in the violins, then in the cellos.
- The trombone phrase: soft and solemn, a hint of the scherzo to come.

### III. Scherzo: Molto vivace

**Summary.** A strong, stamping dance in D minor, with two contrasting [[trio]] sections. The first trio is quick and light, in two beats instead of three; the second is a stream of running notes. The scherzo returns after each, and the movement ends quietly with a memory of the first trio.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: a sturdy, accented tune in three beats, grown from the trombones' phrase.
- Trio 1: quick, light chords tossed between wind and strings, in two beats.
- Trio 2: a busy stream of running notes.

### IV. Allegro animato e grazioso

**Summary.** "Spring in full bloom". After a rising scale for the whole orchestra, a light, dancing theme takes over. Before the themes return, the music stops: the horns play a soft call, and a solo flute answers with a free, birdlike run. Schumann asked for the movement not to be taken too frivolously.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a broad scale rising through the orchestra, like a door flung open.
- The main theme: light, tiptoeing, in the strings.
- The horn call and the flute's answer: a pause in the open air.

### Threads

- **Spring in winter.** The symphony was written in January, in a few days, in the first winter of Schumann's marriage. It looks forward to spring rather than describing it.
- **The trumpet call.** The opening rhythm returns as the Allegro theme and again at the recapitulation; the trombones' phrase at the end of the Larghetto becomes the scherzo's tune. The movements are linked by shared ideas.
- **A piano composer's orchestra.** It was Schumann's first finished symphony. The finale even borrows a theme from the last piece of his piano cycle *Kreisleriana* (1838).
- **Colour.** Schumann asked for three timpani, which was rare at the time, and added a triangle to the orchestra.
