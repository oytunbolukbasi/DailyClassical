### The big picture

- Boccherini, an Italian cellist who had settled in Spain, wrote this quintet in 1780 for his employer, the Infante Don Luis, younger brother of King Charles III. Since marrying a woman of lower rank in 1776, Don Luis had lived away from the court, and his household, Boccherini included, had moved to Arenas de San Pedro, a small town in the mountains west of Madrid. The music looks back at the capital from there.
- On the title page Boccherini wrote: "This little quintet represents the music that passes at night through the streets of Madrid, from the bells of the Ave Maria to the Retreat; and everything that does not follow the rules of composition must be forgiven for the sake of the truth of what is being represented." It is [[programme music]], rare in chamber music of the time.
- The five players keep pretending to be something else. Plucked strings ring like church bells, one violin plays a soldiers' drum, both cellos are laid across the players' knees and strummed like guitars, and a cello imitates a bassoon. Some passages are marked *con mala grazia*, "with bad grace": they are meant to sound rough.
- Boccherini doubted anyone outside Spain would understand it. In 1797, when the publisher Ignaz Pleyel was buying his quintets, he wrote that this one was "totally useless, and even ridiculous, outside Spain", because listeners could never grasp its meaning nor players play it as it should be played, and sent a symphony in its place. It stayed unpublished in his lifetime, yet it became one of his best-known works.
- The score runs its scenes together in five short movements; editions and recordings often number seven scenes, and this album groups them into four tracks. In one line: evening bells, the barracks drum and the blind men's minuet (I), a street procession praying the rosary (II), young men singing to their guitars (III), the night patrol marching back to barracks (IV).

### I. The Ave Maria bells – The soldiers' drum – Minuet of the blind beggars

**Summary.** Three short scenes without a break. At dusk the parish bells ring for the Ave Maria, the evening prayer; a drum answers from the soldiers' barracks; then blind street musicians strike up a clumsy [[minuet]], with the cellos played like guitars.

**Main ideas**

- The bells: four players pluck ([[pizzicato]]) the same few high open strings, each chord left to ring; the first cello strikes a low note just before each one, like the clapper hitting the bell.
- The drum: the first violin alone, as loud as it can, rattling on a single note.
- The minuet of the blind: a limping tune in the two violins over strummed chords from the viola and the cellos.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft plucked chords, the same few notes again and again, each one left to ring; a low note sounds just before each chord | The Ave Maria bells: "Tutti pizzicato", everyone but the first violin, imitating the bells |
| ≈ 0:45 | Everything else stops. One violin, very loud, hammers a single note in fast, drum-like rhythms, with pauses | The drum of the soldiers' quarter: the barracks join in the Ave Maria |
| ≈ 1:20 | A lame, lurching dance tune high in the violins; below it, thick chords strummed with the fingers | Minuetto dei ciechi, the minuet of the blind beggars, marked "con mala grazia". The cellists hold their instruments across their knees |
| ≈ 1:45 | The tune goes on, scratchier and more insistent | The second half of the minuet, marked "con asprezza", harshly |
| ≈ 2:10 | Everyone stops except one cello, strumming the same chords on its own | Instead of bringing the tune back, Boccherini leaves the second cello alone |
| ≈ 2:25 | A short pause, then the whole dance again from the beginning | Boccherini's direction: "after a short pause the whole minuet is repeated as it stands" |
| Last 15 seconds | The lone strumming cello once more, then silence | The end of the minuet |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the disguises in three and a half minutes: plucked strings as bells, a violin as a drum, cellos as guitars. Not one instrument plays the way it normally would.
2. Listen to the minuet's step. The tune stumbles, putting its weight in the wrong place, like a dancer with a limp; Boccherini wants it to sound awkward.

### II. The rosary

**Summary.** A procession passes, praying the rosary, as groups of singers did in the streets of 18th-century Madrid. A soft, slow prayer, played "without strict time", is answered by a loud, quick response from everyone. The exchange happens three times.

**Main ideas**

- The prayer: a slow, ornamented duet, the first violin low and the first cello high, moving together a third or sixth apart.
- The little bell: a few plucked notes, first from the second violin, later from the viola.
- The response: all five, loud, in a bouncing three-beat rhythm.
- The bassoon: in the later prayers the first cello plays everything on one string, imitating a bassoon in the procession.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A slow, soft duet; the two lines move together, stretch and pause, with no steady beat | The prayer, Largo assai, "dolcissimo e con grazia, senza rigor di battuta" |
| ≈ 0:15 | A few plucked notes from the second violin, between the phrases | "Pizzicato, imitando un campanello": a small hand bell |
| ≈ 1:20 | Suddenly loud and fast: all five together, in three | The response, Allegro |
| ≈ 1:35 | Slow and soft again; now the two violins sing the prayer, and a cello murmurs below in a reedy, nasal voice; the viola plucks the bell | "Largo come prima". The first cello plays "all on the third string, imitating the bassoon" |
| ≈ 2:55 | The loud, quick response again | The second Allegro |
| ≈ 3:10 | The prayer a third time, with the bassoon-like cello | Largo come prima |
| ≈ 4:25 | The response for the last time, ending on a long held chord | The last Allegro |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the pauses in the prayer. "Without strict time" lets the players stretch the phrases like people praying aloud, and the loud response always arrives with a firm beat.
2. In the second and third prayers, listen below the violins: the first cello, kept on one string, takes on the thick, buzzing sound of a bassoon.

### III. Passa calle ("Los Manolos") – The drum

**Summary.** Boccherini's note: "a way of playing and singing that the Spaniards call Passa calle, that is, 'pass along the street', with which, or with something a little different, they amuse themselves in the streets at night, singing and playing." The singers are *manolos*, the swaggering young men of Madrid's popular quarters, out with their guitars. At the end the soldiers' drum sounds again.

**Main ideas**

- The guitars: both violins pluck full chords throughout ("pizzicato sempre"), a strummed refrain over a plucked bass from the viola and second cello.
- The singer: the first cello, bowed, loud and "con mala grazia", sings the street song.
- The guitar solo: the second violin alone, rolling chords one note after another ([[arpeggio]]).

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Loud plucked chords, strummed in a quick three-beat rhythm, over a plucked bass | The refrain, Allegro vivo. Everyone plucks |
| ≈ 0:15 | A rough, loud tune in the cello's tenor range, over the strumming | The song: the first cello, "forte e con mala grazia" |
| ≈ 0:40 | Everyone stops but the second violin, which rolls slow chords from bottom to top | "Solo, Arpeggio": the guitar alone between verses |
| ≈ 0:55 | The strumming and the cello's song again | The next verse |
| ≈ 1:40 | The second violin alone again with its rolling chords; this time the song does not come back | The street singers move on |
| Last 20 seconds | A single violin, very loud, hammering one note | "Il primo violino imitando il tamburo": the drum again, announcing the Retreat |

**Things to notice**

1. Only one player uses the bow in the song: the first cello. Everything around it is plucked, which is why the passage sounds like a voice with guitars.

**In this recording.** The Cuarteto Casals was founded in Madrid in 1997. Here its own cellist, Arnau Tomàs, plays the second cello and the guest, Eckart Runge, cellist of the Artemis Quartet, plays the first: the bells' clapper, the bassoon in the rosary and the voice of this street song.

### IV. Ritirata (the Retreat)

**Summary.** The Retreat was the evening call of fifes and drums that sent soldiers back to their barracks at curfew. Boccherini's march tune comes from the real thing: an official 1761 book of fife and drum calls for the Spanish infantry. Four short sections repeat the march with different instruments and dynamics, as if the patrol were moving through the streets.

**Main ideas**

- The fifes: the two violins play the march tune together, a third apart.
- The drum: one instrument rolls on a single note, a fast [[tremolo]]; the job passes from player to player.
- The rattle: quick groups of six notes churning in the lower strings.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A brisk march tune in the two violins, side by side like fifes; below, a cello rattles on one note like a snare drum, and the others churn in quick groups of six | The Ritirata, Maestoso. First section; the first cello is the drum |
| ≈ 0:50 | The march again, the drum roll now lower down, in the second cello | Second section, marked very soft |
| ≈ 1:45 | Loud and soft bars take turns, as if the sound came and went on the wind; the drum is now the second violin | Third section |
| ≈ 2:40 | The fullest sound of the piece: both violins on the same tune, the viola drumming | Last section, marked very loud |
| Last 15 seconds | The march's last phrase and a final stop | The patrol has passed; the night's music is over |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the drum roll from section to section: first cello, second cello, second violin, viola.

### Threads

- **Instruments in disguise.** Bells, drums, guitars, a bassoon, a hand bell: almost every sound in the quintet is a string instrument imitating something heard in the street.
- **One evening, in order.** The scenes follow the clock, from the Ave Maria bells at dusk to the curfew. The soldiers' drum opens the night and comes back to close it.
- **A second version.** Boccherini later revised the last movement, turning the Retreat into a theme with eleven [[variation|variations]] and asking for it to begin "so softly that it is barely heard", approach and fade away; he also arranged the Retreat for other groups. In 1975 Luciano Berio laid four of these versions on top of one another in an orchestral piece. The stops here follow the 1780 score.
- **An Italian in Madrid.** Boccherini lived in Spain from 1768 until his death in Madrid in 1805. The quintet is a portrait of his adopted city by someone who knew its sounds well.
