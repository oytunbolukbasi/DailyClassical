### The big picture

- Bach wrote this concerto in Leipzig, probably in 1730 or early 1731, for the Collegium Musicum, a society of students and professional musicians that he directed from 1729. They gave free concerts at Gottfried Zimmermann's coffee house, where the audience paid only for its coffee. Bach's own handwritten parts for the two soloists survive from those years.
- There are two solo violins, a small string band and the [[continuo]] of harpsichord and bass instruments. The two soloists are not rivals. They are equal partners: one plays a phrase, the other answers or takes it over, and often they play the same tune a moment apart.
- Bach learned the Italian concerto by studying Vivaldi, and the outer movements follow its plan: a refrain for the band, the [[ritornello]], comes back in different keys between the soloists' passages. But Bach fills it with [[counterpoint]], independent lines woven together.
- A few years later he arranged the whole concerto for two harpsichords, a tone lower in C minor (BWV 1062). Today the violin version is by far the better known, above all for its singing slow movement.
- In one line: a busy, serious dialogue (I), a long duet of two singing voices (II), a fierce chase (III).

### I. Vivace

**Summary.** The movement opens like a [[fugue]]: one group of violins in the band starts a busy tune, a second group enters with it a little higher, and the texture fills out. When the two soloists arrive they bring a new, livelier idea, which one plays and the other immediately repeats. The band's fugue tune and the soloists' dialogue take turns to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The fugue tune: a determined line of quick notes, passed from part to part in the band.
- The soloists' theme: a leaping, bright figure that one violin plays and the other echoes, a step higher or lower.

### II. Largo ma non tanto

**Summary.** A slow, swaying movement in F major, in the lilting rhythm of a [[siciliano]]. The band plays soft, steady chords underneath, and above them the two soloists sing one long duet: one starts a melody, the other enters with the same melody a few bars later, and their lines wind around each other, crossing and parting.

**Main ideas**

- The duet melody: a long, singing line, first in one violin, then in the other.
- The accompaniment: quiet, gently pulsing chords in the band, never pushing.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | Soft, rocking chords in the band; one solo violin begins a long, singing melody | The duet begins in F major |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow one violin for a whole minute, then switch to the other. Neither is the accompaniment; each has the melody in turn.

**In this recording.** The English Concert play on period instruments, and Trevor Pinnock directs from the harpsichord. Their Largo lasts six and a half minutes and keeps moving; the Oistrakhs' recording (also recommended) takes about a minute longer.

### III. Allegro

**Summary.** A fast, driving finale in D minor. It opens with the two soloists in a close [[canon]]: the second violin plays the same tune as the first, only one beat behind, so the two seem to be chasing each other. The band answers with hard, forceful chords, and the chase goes on through several keys before the opening returns.

**Main ideas**

- The chase: the soloists' tune, played by both violins one beat apart.
- The band's answer: short, strong chords and rushing figures, almost stormy.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The two solo violins start almost together, the second a beat behind the first; the band drives underneath | The opening canon |
| ≈ 0:30 | The full band takes over, loud and insistent | The [[ritornello]] |

| ≈ 2:00 | The chase returns in another key | The canon, now away from home |

**Things to notice**

1. At the start, listen for the echo: the second violin is exactly one beat behind the first. It is the same principle as a round, only very fast.

### Threads

- **Two equals.** In all three movements the two violins share the material. Bach gives neither the leading part for long.
- **Imitation everywhere.** A fugue in the band (I), a canon in the duet (II), a close canon in the chase (III): the concerto is built from voices copying each other.
- **A concert in a coffee house.** The concerto was written for public concerts by students and professionals in Leipzig, not for the church. In the 20th century George Balanchine made a ballet of it, *Concerto Barocco*, in which two ballerinas follow the two violins.
