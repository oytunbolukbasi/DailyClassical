### The big picture

- In January 1924 George Gershwin, a 25-year-old Broadway songwriter, read in a newspaper that he was writing a "jazz concerto" for the bandleader Paul Whiteman. He had turned the idea down; Whiteman talked him round, and the concert was five weeks away.
- Gershwin said the shape of the piece came to him on a train to Boston, in its rattle and rhythm: "a sort of musical kaleidoscope of America". He wrote it for two pianos; Whiteman's arranger Ferde Grofé scored it for the band, finishing eight days before the concert. His brother Ira suggested the title after visiting an exhibition of paintings by Whistler.
- It was first played on 12 February 1924 at Aeolian Hall in New York, at a long concert called "An Experiment in Modern Music", with Gershwin at the piano. He improvised part of his solo and wrote it down only later. The audience cheered; the critics were divided.
- It is a [[rhapsody]]: one movement, free in form, made of contrasting episodes rather than a plan. It mixes the concerto with jazz, ragtime and Broadway song, and its melodies are coloured by bent [[blue note]]s.
- In one line: a clarinet's wail, a city's bustle, one big slow tune, and a blazing return to the start.

### I. Rhapsody in Blue

**Summary.** One movement of about a quarter of an hour, played without a break. Piano and orchestra take turns: the orchestra announces a tune, the piano takes it apart in long solos, and the music keeps changing speed and mood. Halfway through, the bustle stops for one long, slow melody, which returns at full strength near the end.

**Main ideas**

- The opening theme: the clarinet's rising wail and the bluesy, jaunty tune that follows. It comes back throughout the piece.
- The "train" tune: a quick, chugging, repeated idea, light and percussive.
- The slow tune: a broad, warm melody for the strings, the most famous moment after the opening.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the very first notes. Many clarinettists make the slide sound like a voice, a wail rather than a scale.
2. Count how often the opening tune comes back, in different speeds and colours. It holds the loose structure together.
3. Grofé's scoring includes saxophones and a banjo, instruments from the dance band rather than the symphony orchestra. Listen for the saxophones in the band passages.

**In this recording.** Bernstein plays the piano and conducts at the same time. The Columbia Symphony Orchestra was a recording orchestra that Columbia Records assembled from New York players for sessions; it plays a version for full symphony orchestra, based on Grofé's later scoring, not the 1924 jazz-band original.

### Threads

- **A concert for jazz.** Whiteman's concert set out to show that jazz could stand in a concert hall. The Rhapsody was the second-to-last item on a long programme, and it was the one people remembered.
- **Loose by design.** Critics then and later found the piece formless. Leonard Bernstein, who loved it, wrote in 1955 that you could cut out parts of it and it would still be the *Rhapsody in Blue*. Its unity comes from its themes, which return again and again, not from a classical plan.
- **New York.** Gershwin heard the piece in a train's rattle and called it a portrait of America's "metropolitan madness". It has been linked to New York ever since, from Woody Allen's *Manhattan* to *Fantasia 2000*.
