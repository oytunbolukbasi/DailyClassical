### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it in 1801–02, aged about thirty-one, as the second of three sonatas published together as Op. 31. In April 1802 his brother Karl offered it to the publisher Breitkopf & Härtel, though Beethoven had already promised the set to Hans Georg Nägeli in Zurich. The brothers quarrelled bitterly over it.
- These were also the months in which Beethoven faced his growing deafness. In October 1802, in the village of Heiligenstadt, he wrote the letter to his brothers now known as the Heiligenstadt Testament.
- The nickname is not Beethoven's. His secretary Anton Schindler, often unreliable, said that when he asked what this sonata and the "Appassionata" meant, Beethoven told him: "Just read Shakespeare's *Tempest*." Some scholars think he may have meant a different book altogether.
- The first movement keeps stopping. Slow, hushed chords interrupt the fast music, and later the piano plays two free, speaking phrases, like a singer's [[recitative]].
- In one line: a storm interrupted by voices (I), a slow, noble song with a distant drum (II), a restless, rolling movement that never stops (III).

### I. Largo – Allegro

**Summary.** A slow, rolled chord, quiet and mysterious, then a few quick, anxious notes; again a slow chord, again the quick notes. Only then does the storm break. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but the slow chords keep coming back, and at the start of the [[recapitulation]] they open into two lonely, speaking phrases.

**Main ideas**

- The Largo: a quiet chord played as a slow upward ripple, ending in a pause.
- Theme 1: a rising arpeggio in the bass, loud, answered by a pleading little figure in the right hand, over restless triplets.
- Theme 2: anxious, pushing figures in A minor, with sharp accents.
- The recitative: a single line in the right hand, slow and free, like a voice speaking.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A slow, noble movement in B-flat major. It opens with a rolled chord, like the first movement, and answers it with a short phrase high up. A soft, distant rumble in the bass, like a muffled drum, accompanies the second part of the theme. There is no real middle section: the opening simply returns, more richly decorated.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a rolled chord low down, answered by a short phrase high in the treble.
- The drum: a quick, soft rumble of notes in the bass under the right hand's chords.
- Theme 2: a gentle, flowing melody in F major.

### III. Allegretto

**Summary.** A restless, rolling movement in D minor in a steady stream of quick notes that never stops. A short figure of four notes, like a horse's hoofbeats or turning wheels, drives everything. It builds to several loud climaxes, then fades away quietly.

**Main ideas**

- The rolling figure: four quick notes, rising and falling over and over.
- Theme 2: sharper and louder, in A minor.
- The run: a long passage for the right hand alone, without the left hand, leading back to the theme.

### Threads

- **Interrupted music.** In the first movement slow chords and recitatives break into the storm; even the Adagio stops on one frozen chord before its return.
- **Rolled chords.** The first and second movements both begin with a chord played as a slow ripple, and the finale's rolling figure is a quick version of the same idea.
- **Voice and orchestra.** The piano imitates a singer in the recitatives, a drum in the Adagio and a whole storm in the outer movements.
- **Quiet endings.** All three movements end softly. For a sonata nicknamed after a storm, there is no triumphant close.
