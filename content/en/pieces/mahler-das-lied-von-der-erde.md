### The big picture

- In the summer of 1907 three blows fell on Mahler at once: he resigned as director of the Vienna Court Opera, his elder daughter Maria died, aged four, and doctors found that he had a heart condition. The same year Hans Bethge published *Die chinesische Flöte* (*The Chinese Flute*), free German versions of Chinese poems, most of them from the 8th century. Mahler set seven of them in *Das Lied von der Erde*, written in 1908–09 at Toblach in the Dolomites, where he spent his summers.
- He called it "a symphony for tenor and alto (or baritone) voice and orchestra", but did not give it a number: it would have been his ninth, and he feared the "curse of the ninth" (since Beethoven, no major composer had completed more than nine symphonies). He wrote a Ninth Symphony afterwards anyway, and died in May 1911 without hearing either work.
- Bruno Walter conducted the first performance in Munich on 20 November 1911, six months after Mahler's death.
- Six songs, sung alternately by a tenor and a low female voice: drinking, autumn loneliness, youth, beauty, drunkenness in spring, and a long farewell that is almost as long as the other five together. The poems are by Li Bai (four songs), Qian Qi, Meng Haoran and Wang Wei. The last lines of the work are Mahler's own.
- In one line: a wild drinking song about death (I), autumn and a weary heart (II), two light pictures of youth and beauty (III, IV), a drunkard in spring (V), and the farewell (VI).

### I. Das Trinklied vom Jammer der Erde

**Summary.** A drinking song with death at the table. The horns whoop, the tenor raises his cup and sings against a huge orchestra. Three times a verse ends with the same refrain, "Dunkel ist das Leben, ist der Tod" ("Dark is life, and dark is death"), each time a semitone higher. Before the last refrain comes a nightmare picture: an ape howling on the graves in the moonlight.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: a wild, upward whoop that opens the song and returns between verses.
- The refrain: a falling phrase on "Dunkel ist das Leben", quieter and darker each time, and higher.
- The vision of the earth: a slower passage about the sky and the earth that last forever, while man lives a hundred years at most.

1. Follow the refrain. Each time it comes back a semitone higher, and each time the world around it is darker.

**In this recording.** Klemperer recorded the work over more than two years, from February 1964 to July 1966, between the end of the old Philharmonia Orchestra and the birth of the New Philharmonia, which is why both names appear. Fritz Wunderlich sang his three songs in November 1964; he died less than two years later, aged 35. The six songs are tracks 1–6 of the Spotify album.

### II. Der Einsame im Herbst

**Summary.** Autumn by a lake. Muted violins murmur a slow, aimless figure and an oboe sings a lonely line above it. The voice describes the frost on the grass and the fading scent of the flowers, then turns inward: "My heart is weary." The song rises once to a passionate cry, "Sun of love, will you never shine again?", and then sinks back into the mist.

**Main ideas**

- The murmur: a quiet, circling figure in muted violins, like mist moving over water.
- The oboe: a slow, drooping melody that answers the voice.
- The cry: a broad, aching climax on "Sonne der Liebe".

**Things to notice**

1. The orchestra here is small, like chamber music: a handful of instruments at a time. Notice how much loneliness Mahler gets from a single oboe.

### III. Von der Jugend

**Summary.** A little picture in porcelain. In a pavilion of green and white porcelain in the middle of a pond, reached by a bridge of jade, friends sit drinking, chatting and writing verses. The music is light and dainty, on a five-note scale that sounds Chinese to Western ears. In the middle everything is reflected upside down in the water.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a skipping melody built on the [[pentatonic scale]], in flute, oboe and triangle.
- The reflection: a slower, dreamier middle section.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A bright, skipping tune in the woodwinds, with a triangle | The pavilion in the pond |
| ≈ 0:15 | The tenor sings lightly, in quick, neat phrases | "Mitten in dem kleinen Teiche steht ein Pavillon" ("In the middle of the little pond stands a pavilion") |
| ≈ 1:30–2:30 | Slower, softer, more dreamlike | The pond reflects everything upside down |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the notes of the tune: it mostly uses only five pitches. That is the [[pentatonic scale]], the simplest way Mahler had to suggest China.

### IV. Von der Schönheit

**Summary.** Young girls are picking lotus flowers on the riverbank, their reflections in the water. Suddenly a band of young men gallops by on horseback; the orchestra bursts into a loud, rattling march with trumpets, tuba and tambourine. The horses neigh and shy, and then calm returns. The most beautiful girl looks after one of the riders, and her longing ends the song.

**Main ideas**

- The girls: a graceful, swaying melody in strings and woodwinds.
- The riders: a sudden march, loud and clattering, the only place in the work with tuba, timpani and tambourine.
- The glance: a slow, tender phrase at the end.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A gentle, swaying melody; flute and violins | Girls picking lotus flowers on the bank |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how the march arrives without warning and leaves just as fast. Mahler packs a whole cavalry charge into little more than a minute and a half.

### V. Der Trunkene im Frühling

**Summary.** "If life is only a dream, why work and worry?" A drunkard sleeps all day, wakes to hear a bird singing that spring has come, and drinks again until the moon rises. The music staggers and lurches, but in the middle there is a moment of pure beauty: the bird's song, played by a solo violin and woodwinds.

**Main ideas**

- The stagger: a lurching tune full of sudden accents and changes of speed.
- The bird: a high, chirping duet for solo violin and flute.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Woodwinds and horns in a cheeky, lurching tune; the tenor enters at once | "Wenn nur ein Traum das Leben ist" ("If life is only a dream") |
| ≈ 1:20 | Everything goes quiet and high; a solo violin and a flute chirp like a bird | The drunkard wakes to birdsong: spring has come |
| ≈ 2:30 | The voice softens, almost moved | He listens to the bird |
| ≈ 3:20 | The lurching tune returns; the tenor fills his cup again | He drinks and sings until the moon rises |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the bird in the middle. For a moment the drunkard is touched by spring, then he turns his back on it and drinks.

### VI. Der Abschied

**Summary.** Two poems joined into one long farewell. In the first, by Meng Haoran, evening falls: the sun sets, the moon rises, the brook sings, tired people go home, and a man waits for his friend. A long, dark interlude for the orchestra alone, like a funeral march, separates it from the second, by Wang Wei: one man dismounts and offers the other the cup of farewell; the friend answers that life has not been kind to him and that he is going into the mountains, to his homeland, never to wander again. Then come Mahler's own lines: the dear earth blooms everywhere in spring and grows green again, forever. The last word, "ewig" ("forever"), is repeated until it fades into silence.

**Main ideas**

- The low note: a deep, held note in the low strings with soft strokes on the [[tam-tam]], and a turning figure for the oboe above. It opens the movement and returns at each new stage.
- The voice: much of it like heightened speech, a [[recitative]] over sustained notes and a flute's winding line.
- The march: a long, heavy procession for the orchestra alone, the emotional centre of the work.
- "Ewig": the word sung again and again, ever softer, over a shimmering chord of celesta, harps and mandolin.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A deep, dark note in the bass, a muffled gong, and an oboe's turning figure | The opening, *Schwer* ("heavy") |
| ≈ 1:30 | The voice enters, almost speaking, over sustained notes; a flute winds around it | "Die Sonne scheidet hinter dem Gebirge" ("The sun departs behind the mountains") |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the last chord. Mahler adds one extra note to the major chord, so it never sounds completely finished. Benjamin Britten said the chord seemed "printed on the atmosphere".
2. Count the "ewig"s if you can. There is no final cadence; the voice and the orchestra simply stop being heard.

### Threads

- **The earth stays, we go.** The first song says it: the sky is blue forever, the earth will bloom each spring, and man lives "not a hundred years". The last song answers it with the same picture, spring returning forever, but now as consolation.
- **Two voices.** The tenor gets the songs of wine and youth, the low female voice the songs of loneliness, beauty and farewell. They never sing together.
- **A symphony of songs.** Mahler called it a symphony, and it is built like one: a fast opening, a slow movement, lighter songs in the middle that work like a [[scherzo]], and a vast finale. Leonard Bernstein called it Mahler's greatest symphony.
