### The big picture

- Haydn spent two long spells in London, in 1791–92 and 1794–95, invited by the violinist and impresario Johann Peter Salomon. He wrote twelve symphonies for London audiences, and No. 104, composed there in 1795, is the last of them. It is also the last symphony he wrote.
- It was played at a concert of Haydn's own music, which he directed, at the King's Theatre on 4 May 1795 (some sources date its first performance a few weeks earlier, in the same theatre). Haydn noted in his diary that the audience was delighted and that he had earned 4,000 gulden in one evening, adding: "such a thing is possible only in England."
- The nickname "London" is a little arbitrary, since all twelve were written for London. In Germany it is often called the "Salomon" symphony.
- It is scored for pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, horns and trumpets, with timpani and strings.
- In one line: a solemn call out of the dark and a bright, single-minded Allegro (I), a gentle song with a storm in the middle (II), a sturdy dance (III), a folk-like finale over a drone (IV).

### I. Adagio – Allegro

**Summary.** The symphony begins with a loud call on two bare notes, then quiet, anxious music in D minor. After about two minutes the clouds lift and the violins start a quiet, cheerful tune in D major. Haydn builds the whole fast section from that one tune: where a second theme would normally appear, he brings back the first in a new key. It is a [[sonata form]] with almost a single theme.

**Main ideas**

- The call: the whole orchestra on two notes, D and A, with no third note to say whether the music is major or minor.
- The Allegro tune: a light, smiling melody in the violins, first heard quietly.

**Things to notice**

1. In the opening seconds, notice that you cannot tell whether the music is happy or sad. The two notes leave it open, until the quiet strings choose the minor.
2. Count how often the Allegro tune comes back. Nearly everything in the movement grows from it.

**In this recording.** Jochum recorded all twelve London symphonies with the London Philharmonic for Deutsche Grammophon in the early 1970s; No. 104 was made in October 1971. He does not repeat the exposition in the first movement, which is why it lasts about nine minutes. On the Spotify album, a set of all twelve, the symphony is tracks 21–24.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A gentle, songlike theme in G major for the strings, the kind of tune you might hum. It is repeated with changes, then interrupted by a loud outburst that wanders through distant keys. When the theme returns, the woodwinds, especially the flute, add new colour.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a simple, graceful melody in the strings, in short balanced phrases.
- The outburst: the same melody turned loud and dark in the minor.

### III. Menuetto: Allegro

**Summary.** A [[minuet]], but a sturdy, outdoor-sounding one with strong accents. The middle section, the [[trio]], moves to a distant key, B-flat major, and gives the tune to the oboe and bassoon.

**Main ideas**

- The minuet: a strong, stamping tune in three for the full orchestra.
- The trio: a lighter, gentler tune for oboe and bassoon over soft strings.

### IV. Finale: Spiritoso

**Summary.** The finale opens with a sound from the countryside: a low note held underneath, like the [[drone]] of a bagpipe, and a lively, folk-like tune above. The tune is often said to come from a Croatian folk song, a claim that is disputed. Haydn sets it in a [[sonata form]] and, before bringing it back, lets the music linger in a strange, distant key.

**Main ideas**

- The tune: a bouncy melody in the violins, over a long-held bass note.
- The drone: a single low note sustained under the tune, as a bagpipe or hurdy-gurdy would.

### Threads

- **From darkness to light.** The symphony opens with a stark call in D minor and ends in a bright, rustic D major. The journey is made in the first two minutes and again across the whole work.
- **One idea at a time.** In the first movement a single tune does the work of two; in the finale one folk melody carries everything. Haydn shows how much can come from very little.
- **A farewell.** This is Haydn's last symphony, written at the height of his fame, for an audience that had paid generously to hear it.
