### The big picture

- Bach wrote four orchestral suites. He called them *ouvertures*, after their long opening movements, and they were not planned as a set. The oldest source for No. 3 is a set of parts from about 1730 in Leipzig, written out partly by Bach himself and partly by his son Carl Philipp Emanuel and his pupil Johann Ludwig Krebs.
- In 1729 Bach took over the Collegium Musicum, a music society founded by Telemann, which gave free concerts at Gottfried Zimmermann's coffee house in Leipzig, including works for orchestra. A suite like this one may well have been heard there.
- A suite of this kind was a German imitation of French court music: a grand [[French overture]], then a string of dances. This one is festive, with three trumpets, timpani, two oboes and strings.
- The second movement, the Air, is for strings alone. In 1871 the violinist August Wilhelmj arranged it in C major so that the melody could be played entirely on the violin's lowest string, and it became famous as the "Air on the G String".
- In one line: a grand ceremonial opening (I), a slow song for strings (II), and three quick French dances (III–V).

### I. Ouverture

**Summary.** A slow, majestic opening in jerky dotted rhythms, with trumpets and drums, played twice. Then the tempo leaps forward: the instruments enter one after another on a busy theme, as in a [[fugue]], and the first violin steps out for solo passages, as if this were a concerto. At the end the slow, dotted music returns briefly.

**Main ideas**

- The slow music: stately chords with a long-short "dotted" rhythm, like a royal entrance.
- The fast theme: a lively run of notes that each part takes up in turn.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the change of speed at about two and three quarter minutes. The slow, heavy dotted music gives way to something light and quick.

**In this recording.** Pinnock and The English Concert recorded the suite in June 1978 on period instruments. In the overture they repeat only the slow opening section, not the fast one, which is why the movement lasts about six minutes. On the Spotify album the suite is tracks 8–12, after Suite No. 2.

### II. Air

**Summary.** The trumpets, drums and oboes fall silent. Over a steady bass that walks in even steps, the first violins hold a long, high note and then unwind one of the best-known melodies Bach wrote, while the second violins and violas weave their own lines around it. The Air has two halves, the first six bars long and the second twelve, and each is played twice.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: a long held note that blossoms into slow, winding phrases in the first violins.
- The bass: even, walking notes that often leap an octave, keeping the slow music moving.

**Listening stops**

1. Listen to the bass rather than the melody. It never stops walking, and it is what keeps such a slow movement from standing still.

### III. Gavotte I–II

**Summary.** Two gavottes, a lively French dance in two beats that starts halfway through the bar. The first, with trumpets and drums, is played; then a second gavotte with a different tune; then the first comes back. Each has two halves, and each half is repeated.

**Main ideas**

- Gavotte I: a bright, bouncing tune for the whole orchestra.
- Gavotte II: a second tune of its own, which then gives way to the return of the first.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A bright, bouncy dance tune, with trumpets and drums | Gavotte I |
| ≈ 1:20 | A different tune, in the same lively step | Gavotte II |

**Things to notice**

1. A gavotte starts on the second half of the bar, so each phrase seems to take a small step before it lands. Tap along and feel where the strong beat falls.

### IV. Bourrée

**Summary.** The shortest movement, just over a minute: a quick, cheerful French dance in two, driven along by the trumpets and drums. It has two halves, each played twice.

**Main ideas**

- The bourrée tune: a brisk, rising phrase that starts just before the beat.

### V. Gigue

**Summary.** A fast, skipping finale in 6/8, with the trumpets ringing out over the strings and oboes. Like the other dances it has two halves, each repeated, and it ends the suite in festive mood.

**Main ideas**

- The gigue tune: a bouncing figure in groups of three, tossed between the orchestra's sections.

### Threads

- **Ceremony and song.** The trumpets and drums give the outer movements their sense of occasion. The Air, where they are silent, is the still centre of the suite.
- **French manners, German hands.** The overture and the dances are French forms, the music of Louis XIV's court. Bach takes them over and fills them with his own [[counterpoint]], several lines moving at once.
- **A second life.** Wilhelmj's 1871 arrangement turned the Air into a concert favourite on its own, and it has been arranged and borrowed ever since, as far as pop music: the 1967 song "A Whiter Shade of Pale" used a bar or two of it as a starting point.
