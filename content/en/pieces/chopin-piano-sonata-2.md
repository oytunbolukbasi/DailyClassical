### The big picture

- The funeral march came first, probably in 1837. Two years later, in the summer of 1839, Chopin built a whole sonata around it at Nohant, George Sand's country house in central France. It was published in 1840.
- He wrote to a friend that summer that he was writing a sonata in B-flat minor that would contain the march his friend already knew. Of the finale he added: "The left hand and the right hand gossip in unison after the March."
- It puzzled people from the start. Robert Schumann wrote that Chopin had brought together four of his maddest children under one roof, and he found the finale more like mockery than music.
- The march was played at Chopin's own graveside in Paris on 30 October 1849, in an arrangement for orchestra. Since then it has been heard at the state funerals of John F. Kennedy, Winston Churchill and Queen Elizabeth II, among many others.
- In one line: a breathless storm with a hymn inside it (I), a hammering [[scherzo]] with a gentle song in the middle (II), the funeral march (III), a whispering whirlwind (IV).

### I. Grave – Doppio movimento

**Summary.** Four slow, heavy bars, like a question asked in the dark, and then the music breaks into a breathless, agitated run. A calm, broad hymn interrupts it. The movement is in [[sonata form]], but with a twist: when the themes return, the breathless first theme is left out, and the hymn comes back on its own.

**Main ideas**

- The Grave: a slow, dark phrase in octaves, low on the piano.
- Theme 1, breathless: short, gasping two-note figures in the right hand over pounding chords in the left.
- Theme 2, the hymn: a broad, slow-moving melody in D-flat major, played as if sung.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the opening four bars again in the stormy middle section. They are hidden in the bass, slow, under everything else.
2. The breathless first theme appears in the exposition but never in the recapitulation. Chopin brings back only the hymn.

**In this recording.** Pollini takes the exposition repeat (≈ 2:25). The first editions disagree about where the repeat should start, at the slow opening or at the fast music; most pianists go back to the fast music, and the timings here assume Pollini does too.

### II. Scherzo

**Summary.** A fierce, hammering [[scherzo]]: leaping chords, repeated notes and sudden accents, played with great force. In the middle, the tempo slows for a [[trio]], a simple, swaying song in a warm major key. The scherzo returns, and at the very end the gentle song comes back to close the movement quietly.

**Main ideas**

- The scherzo: leaping chords and quick repeated notes, both hands jumping across the keyboard.
- The trio: a simple, swaying melody in G-flat major, like a song.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the end. A loud, angry scherzo finishes softly, with the gentle song from the middle, as if the storm has simply walked away.

### III. Marche funèbre. Lento

**Summary.** The most famous funeral march ever written. The left hand rocks between two heavy chords like tolling bells, and the right hand plays a slow, solemn melody. In the middle comes a long, tender song in a major key, like a [[nocturne]], a memory of happier days. Then the march returns.

**Main ideas**

- The march: two low chords alternating like bells, and a slow, grave melody above, in B-flat minor.
- The trio: a long, calm, singing melody in D-flat major over a softly rocking left hand.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Low, heavy chords swing back and forth like bells; above them, a slow, solemn tune | The march, in B-flat minor, quiet at first |
| ≈ 1:10 | The march grows louder, rising to a great, heavy climax | The march's middle phrase, *fortissimo* |

| ≈ 2:35 | Everything changes: a long, calm, singing melody over a gently rocking accompaniment | The [[trio]], in D-flat major, like a nocturne |
| ≈ 5:45 | The bell-chords return, very soft | The march again |
| ≈ 6:50 | The climax again, louder than before | The march's middle phrase |
| Last minute | The march sinks back and fades away | The end, in B-flat minor |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the left hand in the march. It never stops swinging between its two chords, and the [[sustain pedal]] lets them ring like bells.
2. In the trio, notice how the melody seems to float. It is a song, not a march; the bells have stopped.

### IV. Finale. Presto

**Summary.** Barely a minute and a half of whispering, racing notes. Both hands play the same notes, an octave apart, very quietly and very fast, with no tune and no chords. It ends with one loud blow. Anton Rubinstein, the story goes, compared it to the wind howling around gravestones.

**Main ideas**

- A single line of fast notes, doubled in both hands, soft and blurred.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Both hands race in a soft, whispering stream of fast notes; no melody, no chords | The finale, in [[unison]] (octaves) |
| ≈ 0:40 | The opening run of notes again, as if starting over | The second half |
| ≈ 1:18 | A single sudden, loud chord | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. Try to hear a tune. There isn't one, and there are no chords until the very end: only the two hands, the same notes, blurring like wind.

**In this recording.** Pollini was 42. In 1960, aged 18, he had won the International Chopin Piano Competition in Warsaw; Arthur Rubinstein, who chaired the jury, is reported to have said that the young man played better than any of the judges.

### Threads

- **Death.** The funeral march is the centre, but the whole sonata circles round it: the dark opening, the storm, the whisper at the end.
- **Storm and song.** Every movement sets fury against calm: the gasping theme and the hymn, the hammering scherzo and its song, the march and its trio.
- **Two hands as one.** The finale strips the piano down to a single line in both hands. Chopin called it the two hands gossiping in unison.
- **A sonata that broke the rules.** Schumann was baffled by it. Its four movements are joined by mood more than by form.
