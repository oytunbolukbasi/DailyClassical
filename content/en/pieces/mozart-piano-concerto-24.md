### The big picture

- Mozart finished the concerto in Vienna on 24 March 1786, three weeks after No. 23, and played it himself at the Burgtheater in early April. At the same time he was finishing *The Marriage of Figaro*: the opera is K. 492, the concerto K. 491.
- The opera is almost all in major keys; the concerto is one of only two piano concertos Mozart wrote in a minor key. The other is No. 20 in D minor.
- It has the largest orchestra of any Mozart concerto, with both oboes and clarinets, trumpets and drums. The wind instruments often push the strings aside and take the lead.
- Mozart did not write the piano part out in full. In places his manuscript gives only the outline of a run, which he filled in as he played. The manuscript is now at the Royal College of Music in London.
- Beethoven admired it. According to a later account, after hearing it he told a colleague that they would never be able to do anything like it. Brahms wrote a cadenza for it.
- In one line: a dark, searching drama (I), a song of great simplicity (II), a set of variations that refuses to turn to the major at the end (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** The orchestra begins quietly, all playing the same restless, winding theme, before breaking out in full force. When the piano enters, it does not play that theme but a lonely melody of its own. The movement is a [[double exposition]] [[sonata form]], longer than any first movement Mozart had written in a concerto, and it ends not with a bang but in a hush.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a dark, winding line played quietly by the orchestra in unison. In thirteen bars it touches all twelve notes of the [[chromatic scale]] before it settles in C minor.
- The piano's entry: a quiet, questioning solo melody, heard first when the piano comes in.
- The wind themes: gentler tunes passed between the flute, oboes, clarinets and bassoons.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** After the drama, a song in E-flat major of almost childlike simplicity. The piano plays it alone, and the orchestra repeats it. Between its returns come two contrasting sections led by the wind instruments, one in C minor and one in A-flat major. The trumpets and drums are silent.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a short, plain melody of four bars, first played by the piano with no accompaniment.
- The wind episodes: the oboes, clarinets, flute and bassoons sing in dialogue with the piano.

### III. Allegretto

**Summary.** A theme and eight [[variation]]s in C minor. The strings and winds play the theme, a grave, march-like tune; the piano enters only in the first variation. Two variations turn to the major for a moment, but the movement ends in C minor, dark to the last chord.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: two short phrases, each repeated, in a steady, march-like step.
- The variations: the piano decorates the tune, the wind instruments take whole phrases, and the key turns to A-flat major and C major and back.
- The last variation: a change to a lilting six-beat rhythm, after the cadenza, with strange, haunting chords.

### Threads

- **Darkness kept.** The first movement ends in a hush, and the finale refuses the bright major ending that No. 20 allows itself. Only the Larghetto and two variations offer light.
- **Winds.** With oboes and clarinets together, the wind section is unusually rich; in every movement the winds have whole passages to themselves.
- **The piano's freedom.** Mozart left parts of the solo unwritten. Every pianist has to fill in some of it, and to choose or write a cadenza.
