### The big picture

- The piece takes its title and its idea from *The Lark Ascending* (1881), a poem by George Meredith about the song of the skylark, which rises as the bird climbs out of sight. Vaughan Williams copied twelve of its lines at the head of his score.
- He wrote it in 1914 for violin and piano, for the violinist Marie Hall. That August war broke out, and Vaughan Williams, nearly 42, volunteered: he served as a stretcher-bearer in France and Greece and later as an artillery officer. The piece waited until the war was over.
- Marie Hall gave the first performance, with piano, in Shirehampton near Bristol on 15 December 1920. The version for violin and small orchestra followed at the Queen's Hall, London, on 14 June 1921, conducted by Adrian Boult. *The Times* heard in it "the clean countryside, not … the sophisticated concert-room".
- Vaughan Williams called it "A Romance", his word for quiet, contemplative music. Nothing in it is borrowed from folk song, but its tunes sound as if they could be. The orchestra is small: flutes, oboe, clarinets, bassoons, two horns, a triangle and strings.
- In one line: the lark rises alone (opening cadenza), sings over the fields (the folk-like tunes), and climbs out of hearing (closing cadenza).

### I. The Lark Ascending

**Summary.** A single span of music that begins and ends with the violin alone, with no beat to follow. In between, the orchestra paints the land below: two folk-like tunes, a dance-like middle section, and a return of the opening. Critics have heard the solo part as the sky and the orchestra as the ground.

**Main ideas**

- The lark: free, rising figures in the solo violin, trills and turns built from a five-note [[pentatonic scale]], often played softly over the fingerboard.
- The first tune: a calm, folk-like melody the violin sings when the orchestra returns.
- The flute tune: a gentle country melody that moves the music "down to earth".

| ≈ 5:30 | The flutes play a new, gentle country tune; the violin weaves around it | *Allegretto tranquillo*: the music comes down to the fields |
| ≈ 7:20 | Trills in the violin, a light tap of the triangle, then the oboe with a skipping tune | A livelier, dance-like section, in a new key. The triangle is the only percussion in the piece |
| ≈ 11:10 | The violin now plays the flutes' country tune itself, very quietly | The country tune returns |
| ≈ 11:50–13:30 | The opening music comes back, swelling once more in the orchestra before it fades | The return of the opening (*Tempo del principio*) and a last *Largamente* |
| Last 90 seconds | The orchestra drops out; the violin alone climbs higher and higher, until its last notes vanish | The closing cadenza. It reaches a very high D, then falls back a little and stops |

**Things to notice**

1. In the cadenzas there is no beat at all. The violinist decides how long each note lasts, as a bird would.
2. Compare the first and last minutes. The piece ends where it began, with the violin alone, but higher and fainter.

**In this recording.** Iona Brown plays with Neville Marriner and the Academy of St Martin in the Fields; on the same album she is also one of the four soloists in the *Tallis Fantasia*. The Argo LP of 1972 had John Constable's *The Cornfield* on its cover. On the Spotify album *The Lark Ascending* is track 3, after the *Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis* and the *Fantasia on Greensleeves*.

### Threads

- **Sky and earth.** The violin's free cadenzas belong to the bird; the orchestra's steady, folk-like tunes belong to the people and fields below.
- **Before and after the war.** Written in 1914 and first heard in 1920–21, the piece looks back to a peaceful countryside. Some listeners hear a quiet sadness under its calm.
- **A rising line.** From the first cadenza to the last, the violin keeps climbing, until it disappears.
