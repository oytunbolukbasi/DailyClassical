### The big picture

- The music began in the 1840s as the overture to *Les quatre élémens* (The Four Elements), a set of choruses on poems by Joseph Autran about the earth, the winds, the waves and the stars. Liszt abandoned the choral cycle, and in 1853–54 he revised the overture as a stand-alone piece.
- Only then did it get its title, from an ode by the French poet Alphonse de Lamartine. The preface printed with the score asks: what is our life but a series of preludes to an unknown song, whose first solemn note is sounded by death? It goes on to love, the storms that destroy its illusions, a retreat into the calm of the countryside, and the call to battle.
- Liszt conducted the first performance on 23 February 1854, in Weimar, where he was court music director. The score was published in 1856.
- It is the earliest orchestral work to carry the title "symphonic poem", a form Liszt invented: a single movement for orchestra that follows a poetic idea.
- In one line: a question in the dark, a hymn, two love songs, a storm, a country idyll, a march to battle and the hymn again.

### I. Les préludes

**Summary.** One continuous piece in five large sections, all made from a single three-note figure (a step down, then a leap up) heard in the first bars. Each section transforms it: into a grand hymn, two love songs, a storm, a pastoral dance and a march. At the end the hymn of the opening returns. This reshaping of one idea, [[thematic transformation]], is the heart of the [[symphonic poem]].

**Main ideas**

- The three-note figure: a quiet, questioning phrase in the strings, at the very start.
- The hymn: the same figure made grand and broad, in trombones and low strings, with sweeping strings above.
- Love theme 1: a warm melody in the cellos.
- Love theme 2: a swaying song for four horns and violas, with harp.
- The pastoral tune: a light dance in six beats, passed between the woodwinds.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 4:40 | Four horns and the violas sing a swaying song, with harp chords | The second love theme, like a boat song |
| ≈ 6:50 | Low, uneasy rumbling; trembling strings; the music speeds up | The storm approaches, *Allegro ma non troppo* |
| ≈ 7:30–9:05 | Raging strings, brass blows, timpani rolls and shrieking woodwind; then a trumpet call on repeated notes | The storm breaks, *Allegro tempestoso* |
| ≈ 9:05 | Quiet again: an oboe plays the first love theme, then violins and harp | Calm after the storm |
| ≈ 10:05 | A light, swinging tune in six beats, passed between horn, woodwinds and strings | The *Allegretto pastorale*: country peace |
| ≈ 11:40 | The love theme and the pastoral tune combine and gradually speed up | The music gathers itself for action |
| ≈ 13:55 | Trumpets and horns blaze the love theme as a fanfare; drums and a bugle-like call | *Allegro marziale animato*: the call to battle |
| ≈ 15:25 | The grand hymn of the opening returns, with the full orchestra | The *Andante maestoso* again, to close |

**Things to notice**

1. Sing the three-note figure from the start to yourself. Then listen for it in the hymn, in the storm and in the march. Liszt hides it almost everywhere.
2. Compare the second love theme at ≈ 4:40 with the march that follows the fanfare after ≈ 13:55. The same song, once sung softly by horns, comes back as a triumphal march.

**In this recording.** Karajan's performance is broad: 17:14, where the other recordings checked take between about 14½ and 17 minutes.

### Threads

- **One idea, many faces.** Every theme in the piece grows from the three notes of the opening. Liszt used the same technique, thematic transformation, in many later works, among them his Piano Sonata in B minor.
- **Music first, poem later.** Research has shown that the music was finished before the Lamartine title and programme were added. The programme fits the music; it did not create it.
- **A new kind of piece.** Liszt wrote thirteen symphonic poems in all, and *Les préludes* is by far the most often played. The form was taken up by Smetana, Saint-Saëns, Sibelius and Richard Strauss, among others.
