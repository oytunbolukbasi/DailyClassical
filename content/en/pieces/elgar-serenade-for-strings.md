### The big picture

- In 1886 Alice Roberts, the daughter of a major-general, began piano accompaniment lessons with a young Worcester violin teacher, Edward Elgar. He was eight years younger, a Catholic, and worked in his father's music shop; her family disapproved, and she was disinherited. They married on 8 May 1889.
- By 1891 Elgar could not earn enough from composing in London, and the family moved back to Malvern, where he taught and conducted local ensembles. He wrote the Serenade there in the spring of 1892, as a present for their third wedding anniversary. On the manuscript of a piano-duet version he noted that "Braut", German for "bride", meaning Alice, "helped a great deal to make these little tunes".
- It is the first of his works that Elgar said he was happy with: "I like 'em (the first thing I ever did)". A publisher turned it down as "practically unsaleable"; it was first played in public in Antwerp in 1896, and in Britain only in 1899, the year the *Enigma Variations* made him famous.
- It is music for strings alone: violins, violas, cellos and double basses, often divided into more parts. Three short movements, about thirteen minutes in all.
- In one line: a gentle, rocking morning song (I), a long, singing slow movement at the centre (II), a swaying finale that turns back to the first movement's tune (III).

### I. Allegro piacevole

**Summary.** *Piacevole* means "pleasantly". The movement rocks gently in a slow two, like a lullaby or a morning song, and never raises its voice for long. A small figure in the violas starts it, keeps coming back, and ends it.

**Main ideas**

- The viola figure: a short, tapping, dotted rhythm, heard alone at the start.
- The main tune: a soft, rocking melody in E minor in the first violins, more wistful than sad.
- The middle tune: a wider, arching melody that turns to the major, dips briefly into the minor and settles again.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Count how often the viola figure comes back. It is the movement's signature, and it is heard both at the start and at the very end.
2. Listen for the moment the middle melody dips into the minor. It lasts only a few bars, and then the music relaxes into the major again.

**In this recording.** The Sinfonia of London of 1962 was not a concert orchestra but a band of London's leading players, founded in 1955 to record film music; in 1958 it recorded the soundtrack of Hitchcock's *Vertigo*. Barbirolli's tempos are broad: this movement takes almost four minutes, where many performances take about three and a half.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** The heart of the work, and its longest movement. After a short introduction the first violins sing one long melody that seems to unfold without a break; the critic Ernest Newman called it one of the finest and most sustained Elgar ever wrote. The music swells to a warm climax and then closes with the phrase it began with.

**Main ideas**

- The introduction: a short, falling phrase in the lower strings, half question, half sigh.
- The melody: a long, slow line in the first violins, rising in waves, with the other strings moving underneath.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the first violins from the start of the melody to the climax. Notice how rarely the line stops for breath.
2. The movement ends with the phrase it began with. Listen for it in the last minute.

### III. Allegretto – Come prima

**Summary.** A light, swaying finale in G major, in a lilting 12/8 time. It seems to be heading for a quick, happy ending, but near the close the rhythm slows into the 6/8 of the first movement and its rocking tune returns. The Serenade ends where it began, now in E major. *Come prima* means "as at first".

**Main ideas**

- The finale tune: a lilting, swaying melody, simple and good-humoured.
- The return: the rocking main tune of the first movement, back at the end.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A light, swinging tune in the violins; the beat falls in long groups of three | The finale tune, in G major |
| ≈ 0:40 | The tune passes to the lower strings while the violins answer above | The tune is shared out |
| ≈ 1:20–2:00 | Warmer and fuller; a short swell, then the music relaxes | The middle of the movement |
| ≈ 2:05 | The swing slows into a gentler rocking; the first movement's tune returns | *Come prima*: the opening of the work comes back |
| Last 40 seconds | Soft chords, settling into E major | The work ends quietly |

**Things to notice**

1. When the first movement's tune returns, notice that it is in the major. The Serenade began in E minor and ends in E major.
2. Compare the swing of this movement with the rocking of the first. One counts in long groups of three, the other in shorter ones, and Elgar slides from one into the other.

### Threads

- **A present.** Elgar wrote the Serenade for Alice for their third wedding anniversary, when he was still an unknown local musician. He later said it was the first of his works he really liked.
- **Strings alone.** No wind, no brass, no percussion. All the colour comes from how Elgar spreads the strings: high or low, divided or together, melody or accompaniment.
- **Round in a circle.** The finale closes with the first movement's tune, so the work ends where it started, a little brighter.
