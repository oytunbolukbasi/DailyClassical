### The big picture

- Mozart wrote the concerto in Salzburg in 1777, when he was 21, for Giuseppe Ferlendis, an oboist from Bergamo who had just joined the Archbishop of Salzburg's court orchestra. The orchestra is small: two oboes, two horns and strings.
- That autumn Mozart left Salzburg to look for a post, and took the concerto with him. In Mannheim he gave a copy to the court oboist Friedrich Ramm. In February 1778 he wrote home that Ramm had played it for the fifth time, that it was "making a great sensation", and that it was now Ramm's *cheval de bataille*, his war-horse.
- In 1778, short of time for a commission from a Dutch amateur flautist, Mozart seems to have turned the oboe concerto into a flute concerto, a tone higher in D major. Mozart's own score of the oboe version was later lost.
- For more than a century the work was known only as Flute Concerto No. 2. In 1920 the conductor Bernhard Paumgartner found an old set of parts for the oboe version in the archive of the Mozarteum in Salzburg, and an eight-bar sketch in Mozart's hand, found in 1971, confirmed that the oboe came first.
- In one line: a bright, open Allegro (I), a slow song like an opera [[aria]] (II), a cheeky rondo whose tune Mozart later gave to a singer (III).

### I. Allegro aperto

**Summary.** *Aperto* means "open" or "frank", a marking Mozart liked at this time, and the movement is exactly that: bright, confident, in plain daylight. It is in [[sonata form]] with a [[double exposition]]: the orchestra plays the main tunes first, then the oboe enters and makes them its own. Near the end the orchestra stops for the soloist's [[cadenza]].

**Main ideas**

- The opening tune: brisk and festive, full orchestra with horns, then a softer answer in the violins.
- The oboe's entry: one long, high note that floats above the orchestra before the oboe takes up the tune.
- The second theme: lighter and more playful, with the oboe trading short phrases with the strings.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the oboe's first note. Instead of starting with the tune, it holds one note so long that you notice the instrument before the melody.
2. Compare the orchestra's version of the opening tune with the oboe's. The notes are the same; the oboe adds runs and turns.

**In this recording.** Stankiewicz, the London Symphony Orchestra's principal oboe, recorded the concerto live at LSO St Luke's in London in October 2019. His cadenza in this movement is a dazzling showpiece; the one in the finale is much plainer.

### II. Adagio non troppo

**Summary.** A slow song in F major, "not too slow", that sounds like an aria from an opera with the oboe as the singer. The main melody opens with a wide leap upwards that gives it a sense of longing. After a quieter middle stretch the melody returns, and a short solo cadenza comes before the close.

**Main ideas**

- The main melody: slow and singing, with a wide upward leap near its start.
- The second idea: more ornamented, the oboe turning and decorating long notes.

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the wide leap upwards in the main melody. It is the moment the song reaches out, and it comes back each time the melody does.
2. An oboist breathes like a singer. Notice where the long phrases pause for breath.

### III. Rondo: Allegretto

**Summary.** A lively, cheeky finale. It is a [[rondo]]: a bouncy refrain keeps coming back between contrasting episodes. Five years later Mozart used almost the same tune in his opera *The Abduction from the Seraglio* (1782), for Blonde's aria "Welche Wonne, welche Lust" ("What joy, what delight").

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: short, perky phrases that bounce along in two beats to a bar.
- The episodes: quick runs and leaps for the oboe, and a central stretch that wanders into new keys.

### Threads

- **Oboe or flute.** The same music exists as an oboe concerto in C major and a flute concerto in D major. For more than a century only the flute version was known; today the oboe version is accepted as the original.
- **The voice of an aria.** The oboe is treated like a singer throughout: long held notes, breathing phrases, a slow movement that sounds like opera. The finale's tune later became an aria.
- **A young man's travelling music.** Mozart wrote the concerto just before leaving Salzburg to seek his fortune, and it travelled with him: to Mannheim, where it became Ramm's war-horse, and into a flute concerto on the way to Paris.
