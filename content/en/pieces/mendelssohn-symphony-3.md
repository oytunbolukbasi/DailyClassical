### The big picture

- In the summer of 1829 Mendelssohn, then 20, toured Scotland on foot with his friend Karl Klingemann. On 30 July, at dusk, he visited the ruined chapel of Holyrood Palace in Edinburgh. He wrote home that it was roofless, overgrown with grass and ivy, and that he thought he had found there the beginning of his "Scottish" symphony. With the letter he sent the first bars of the opening tune.
- The same journey took him to the island of Staffa and gave him *The Hebrides* overture. The symphony was much harder. He worked on it in Italy, gave up after 1831, and finished it in Berlin only on 20 January 1842, thirteen years after the visit.
- He conducted the first performance in the Leipzig Gewandhaus on 3 March 1842. That summer he met Queen Victoria and Prince Albert in London, and the Queen accepted the dedication.
- It was the last of his five symphonies to be completed, but the third to be published, hence the number. Mendelssohn never put "Scottish" on the score, and he asked for the four movements to be played without the usual pauses between them.
- In one line: a dark ballad that ends in a storm (I), a quick dance with a Scottish lilt (II), a song interrupted by a march (III), a war-like finale that turns into a hymn (IV).

### I. Andante con moto – Allegro un poco agitato

**Summary.** A slow, grey introduction built on the tune Mendelssohn wrote down at Holyrood. Then the same tune, sped up and made restless, becomes the main theme of a fast movement in [[sonata form]]. Near the end a storm breaks out, and the movement closes by returning to the slow opening.

**Main ideas**

- The Holyrood tune: a slow, sad melody in oboes and violas, low and soft, like an old ballad.
- The Allegro theme: the same tune in a quick, rocking 6/8 rhythm, whispered by the violins.
- Theme 2: a clarinet melody in E minor, still dark; Mendelssohn does not allow a sunny contrast.

2. In the storm, listen to the strings. Their scales surge up and down like waves breaking.

### II. Vivace non troppo

**Summary.** After the storm, the clouds lift. A quick, light dance in F major, played straight on from the first movement. Its tune uses the five-note scale of folk music and a jumpy short-long rhythm, the [[Scotch snap]], so it sounds Scottish without quoting any real Scottish tune.

**Main ideas**

- The clarinet tune: a cheerful, skipping melody with a snapped rhythm, over busy strings.
- The horn calls: short fanfares, as if heard from a distance.

### III. Adagio

**Summary.** The slow movement sets a tender song against a stern march. The song, in A major, is played by the violins over a soft, plucked-sounding accompaniment. The march, in the minor, comes in the wind and brass with sharp dotted rhythms. They alternate, and the song has the last word.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a long, calm melody in the first violins.
- The march: a heavy, dotted tread in wind and brass, dark and serious.

### IV. Allegro vivacissimo – Allegro maestoso assai

**Summary.** Mendelssohn first called this movement "Allegro guerriero", war-like. The violins attack a fierce theme with sharp dotted rhythms, and the music fights its way through in A minor. Then it fades almost to nothing, and a new section begins in A major: a broad, solemn hymn, the symphony's final transformation of the Holyrood tune.

**Main ideas**

- The war theme: fast, jagged, in the violins, with sharp dotted rhythms.
- The hymn: a broad melody in A major and in 6/8, first in low, dark instruments, then for full orchestra.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 0:00 | The violins burst in with a fierce, fast theme in sharp, dotted rhythms | The war-like main theme, in A minor |

**Things to notice**

1. Mendelssohn is reported to have wanted the closing hymn to sound like a men's choir. Listen to the low, dark instruments that carry it first.
2. Some listeners have disliked this happy ending. The conductor Otto Klemperer wrote his own, closer in mood to the rest of the movement. Decide whether the hymn feels earned.

### Threads

- **One tune.** The Holyrood melody returns in many forms: the restless Allegro, the clarinet dance, the closing hymn. Mendelssohn grows a whole symphony from it, a kind of [[thematic transformation]].
- **No pauses.** The four movements are played straight through, so the symphony unfolds like a single ballad.
- **Scotland remembered.** Mendelssohn never called the work "Scottish" in print, and there are no real folk tunes in it. The Scotland is the one he remembered: grey skies, ruins, a storm, a dance.
