### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the six quartets of Op. 33 in 1781, ten years after his last set. He offered copies to subscribers in December 1781, saying they were written "in a completely new and special way". They are lighter and more conversational than his earlier quartets, and in each the minuet is replaced by a [[scherzo]], Italian for "joke".
- At least one of them was played on Christmas Day 1781 in the Vienna apartment of the visiting Grand Duke Paul of Russia, later Tsar Paul I. Haydn dedicated the set to him, which is why they are known as the "Russian" quartets. They were printed by Artaria in Vienna in 1782.
- The English nickname "The Joke" came later. It refers to the end of the finale, where the music breaks into pieces separated by longer and longer silences. A story says Haydn wrote it to win a bet that the ladies in the audience would start talking before the music stopped; it is told as a story, without evidence.
- The set is often said to have spurred Mozart, newly settled in Vienna, to write his own six quartets, which he dedicated to Haydn in 1785.
- In one line: a friendly conversation built from one phrase (I), a stamping peasant dance with a sliding village fiddler (II), a solemn song that begins in the low voices (III), a quick dance that cannot decide when to end (IV).

### I. Allegro moderato, cantabile

**Summary.** The first violin sings a friendly, easy-going tune over soft, pulsing chords, and almost everything in the movement grows out of its opening phrase. It is a [[sonata form]] in which the four players keep passing the phrase between them, like people in conversation.

**Main ideas**

- The opening phrase: a short, smiling tune in the first violin, starting with a little upbeat.
- The repeated notes: a passage where all four instruments churn out quick repeated notes, sounding for a moment like a whole orchestra.

### II. Scherzo: Allegro

**Summary.** Not a courtly minuet but a country dance: heavy repeated chords for stamping feet, and a few cheeky surprises in the phrasing. In the middle section, the [[trio]], the first violin plays like a village fiddler, sliding from note to note.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a sturdy, stamping tune in three beats, with thick, repeated chords.
- The trio: the first violin's tune with slides, over a steady chug in the second violin and long held notes below.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

**Things to notice**

1. In the trio, listen to the first violin's slides. Haydn wanted them; 19th-century editors found them vulgar and took them out of their editions.

### III. Largo e sostenuto

**Summary.** A slow, solemn song. It begins, for the first time in Haydn's quartets, with only the viola and cello, the two lowest instruments; the violins then sing the same melody. Gentle song and forceful outbursts take turns, and each time the melody returns it is dressed in a richer texture.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: a broad, hymn-like tune, first in the low voices.
- The outbursts: short, forceful chords with silences between them.

### IV. Finale: Presto

**Summary.** A quick, bubbly [[rondo]] in a lilting six-time: a catchy refrain that keeps returning between contrasting sections. At the end Haydn stops everything for a few slow bars, then brings back the refrain broken into two-bar pieces, with longer and longer silences between them, until no one can tell whether it is over.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a light, skipping tune made of short two-bar phrases.
- The broken ending: the same phrases, separated by silence.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |

**Things to notice**

1. During the silences at the end, notice yourself waiting. That uncertainty is the point of the joke: the music ends on a beginning.

### Threads

- **Conversation.** In all four movements the tune travels: the first movement's phrase is passed around, the slow movement starts in the low voices, and the trio gives the first violin a solo turn as a village fiddler.
- **Jokes.** A minuet turned into a peasant dance, slides that later editors removed, and an ending that keeps the listener guessing. Haydn's humour is in the timing.
- **Gravity.** Between the jokes, the Largo is the quartet's serious centre, and the finale's sudden Adagio borrows its solemn tone for a moment before the punchline.
