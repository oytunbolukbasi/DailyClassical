### The big picture

- In the summer of 1936 Barber, aged 26, was living in Austria with his partner, the composer Gian Carlo Menotti. There he wrote a string quartet, and on 19 September he told a friend he had just finished its slow movement: "it is a knockout!"
- The same year he arranged that slow movement for string orchestra. In January 1938 he sent it to Arturo Toscanini, who returned it without comment. Barber was annoyed, until he learned the conductor had already memorised it.
- Toscanini conducted the first performance on 5 November 1938 with the NBC Symphony Orchestra, in a radio broadcast from Studio 8H in New York. He is reported to have called it "semplice e bella": simple and beautiful.
- It became the music of public grief. It was broadcast at the announcement of President Roosevelt's death in 1945 and played after the funeral of President Kennedy in 1963, and it has been heard in films such as *Platoon* (1986). In 1967 Barber set it for choir as *Agnus Dei*.
- Barber grew tired of its fame. "They always play that piece," he said in 1978. "I wish they'd play some of my other pieces."
- In one line: one melody, rising step by step to a single climax, then a silence and a return.

### I. Adagio for Strings

**Summary.** The piece is built like an arch. A slow melody moves almost entirely by step, as if climbing a staircase, and passes through the string sections as it rises. It reaches a high, loud climax of four chords, breaks off into silence, and returns to where it began, ending on a chord that does not resolve.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: long, slow and chant-like, moving by step, first in the first violins.
- The chords beneath: sustained harmonies that change reluctantly, one note at a time, so the music seems suspended.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The violins hold a single soft note; the lower strings come in just after, and a slow melody begins to climb | The melody, in B-flat minor. The late entry of the lower strings creates a gentle tension from the first bar |
| ≈ 1:30 | The melody reaches a little higher, then sinks back; the harmony shifts underneath | The first long phrase completes |
| ≈ 3:10 | The melody moves down to the cellos, darker and warmer, while the violins hold notes above | The theme passes to the lower strings |
| ≈ 4:50 | All the strings begin to climb together, higher and higher, growing louder and more intense | The long build towards the climax |
| ≈ 5:45–6:10 | Four loud chords at the very top of the violins' range, then the music stops dead | The climax, and a long silence |
| ≈ 6:15 | Very soft chords, as if the music is gathering itself again | After the silence |
| ≈ 6:45 | The opening melody returns, quietly, in the violins | The arch closes: the beginning comes back |
| Last 40 seconds | The melody stops on a chord that seems to wait for an answer that never comes; the sound fades | The unresolved ending |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the melody step by step. It hardly ever leaps; that is why it sounds so inevitable, and so like a chant.
2. Listen to the silence after the climax. It is part of the music: Barber writes a pause, and the players let it hang in the air.
3. The beat is irregular. Barber slips in bars of five, six or three beats among the fours, so the music never settles into a pulse.

**In this recording.** Neville Marriner and the Academy of St Martin in the Fields recorded the Adagio in October 1975 at St John's Smith Square in London, for an Argo album of American music. On Spotify the track is labelled "Live", although the original release gives only the venue and the month. The Adagio is track 1, followed by Ives's Third Symphony and Copland's *Quiet City*.

### Threads

- **An arch.** Quiet beginning, one climb, one climax, a silence, a quiet return: the shape of a single breath.
- **Old sounds, modern piece.** The stepwise melody and simple chords recall Renaissance church music and [[plainchant]], although the piece was written in 1936.
- **From quartet to orchestra to choir.** The same music exists as a quartet movement, for string orchestra and as Barber's own choral *Agnus Dei*.
