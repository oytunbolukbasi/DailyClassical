### The big picture

- Haydn wrote the quartet in 1797, aged 65, after his second stay in London. It is the third of six quartets dedicated to the Hungarian Count Joseph Erdődy, published in 1799 as his Op. 76.
- The slow movement is a set of variations on *Gott erhalte Franz den Kaiser* ("God save Franz the Emperor"), a hymn Haydn had just written for Emperor Francis II. It was first sung on the emperor's birthday, 12 February 1797, at the Burgtheater in Vienna. According to a later account, Haydn had heard *God Save the King* in England and wanted his own country to have a song like it.
- The tune outlived the empire. With new words written in 1841, it became the melody of the German national anthem, which is still sung today.
- Haydn loved the hymn. In his last years he often played it at the piano, and his servant wrote that it was the last music Haydn ever played.
- In one line: a bright Allegro with a rustic dance in the middle (I), the hymn and four variations (II), a sturdy minuet with a minor-key trio (III), a stormy finale that only finds C major at the end (IV).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** A bright, busy first movement in [[sonata form]]. It grows from a short opening figure that passes between all four players, and in the middle it suddenly turns into a rough country dance.

**Main ideas**

- The opening figure: five notes in the first violin, falling and rising, firm and clear. One scholar has read them as a hidden greeting to the emperor.
- The country dance: a stamping tune over a held, droning bass, like a bagpipe.

### II. Poco adagio, cantabile

**Summary.** The hymn, then four variations on it. In this kind of [[variation]] the tune itself hardly changes; instead it passes from player to player, and each time the others weave new music around it.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: a slow, dignified tune in the first violin, with the other three in plain, full chords.
- The variations: the tune moves to the second violin, then the cello, then the viola, and finally back to the first violin.

### IV. Finale: Presto

**Summary.** A fast, fierce finale in [[sonata form]] that begins not in C major but in C minor, with three hammered chords. The bright home key is reached only near the end.

**Main ideas**

- The hammered chords: three loud chords that open the movement and come back as a signal.
- The driving rhythm: quick notes in groups of three that keep the music pressing forward.

### Threads

- **The hymn.** The slow movement gives each of the four players a turn with the tune.
- **Shared voices.** In the first movement too, the opening figure passes between all the instruments.
- **From minor to major.** The trio's minor key and the finale's stormy C minor make the final arrival of C major sound like a victory.
