### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it in 1825, aged fifty-four and by then deaf. It is the second of three quartets for Prince Nikolai Galitzin, a Russian music lover who had asked him for them.
- While he was working on it Beethoven fell seriously ill and had to stop for weeks. When he recovered, he wrote the slow centre of the quartet and headed it *Heiliger Dankgesang eines Genesenen an die Gottheit, in der lydischen Tonart*: "Holy song of thanksgiving of a convalescent to the Deity, in the Lydian mode".
- It was played twice in private in September 1825, in a Vienna inn, with Beethoven present, and in public on 6 November by the Schuppanzigh Quartet. It appeared in print only after his death, which is why it is numbered 15 though it was written before Nos. 13 and 14.
- It has five movements instead of the usual four, arranged around the long hymn: two quicker movements before it, two after.
- In one line: a troubled opening (I), a gentle old-fashioned dance (II), a hymn of thanks (III), a brief march that breaks off (IV), a passionate finale that turns to the major (V).

### I. Assai sostenuto – Allegro

**Summary.** A slow, hushed opening of four long notes, then a fast movement full of sudden stops and changes of mood. Beethoven runs through his themes three times, each time in a different key, and analysts still argue about how to label its form.

**Main ideas**

- The four-note figure: two pairs of notes, a half step up and then a leap, played slowly by the cello and taken up by the others.
- Theme 1: a tense, dotted phrase in A minor, passed between the cello and the first violin.
- Theme 2: a gentler, swaying tune in a major key.

### II. Allegro ma non tanto

**Summary.** A gentle dance in A major, closer to a [[minuet]] than to a [[scherzo]]. Its middle section imitates country music: a high fiddle tune over a droning bass, like bagpipes or a hurdy-gurdy.

**Main ideas**

- The dance: a short, rising figure that the four instruments play together, then pass around.
- The trio tune: a high, skipping melody in the first violin over a long, held low A. Beethoven based it partly on an earlier dance of his own, an Allemande for piano.

### III. Molto adagio – Andante

**Summary.** The heart of the quartet: Beethoven's hymn of thanks after his illness. A slow, plain [[chorale]] in an old church scale alternates with quicker, brighter music marked "Neue Kraft fühlend" ("Feeling new strength"). The hymn comes three times, each time more deeply felt.

**Main ideas**

- The hymn: slow chords, one line at a time; each line is introduced by short phrases that overlap gently between the instruments.
- "New strength": livelier music in D major, with trills, leaps and dancing rhythms.
- The scale: the hymn is in the [[Lydian mode]], an old church scale. It sounds almost like F major, but one note, B, is a half step higher, which gives it a floating, unworldly sound.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Very slow and soft: short, overlapping phrases, then all four play a long line of the hymn together in plain chords | The hymn, in the Lydian mode |
| ≈ 4:05 | Suddenly brighter and quicker: trills and leaping figures, the first violin dancing | "Feeling new strength", in D major |
| ≈ 5:40 | The hymn returns; around its plain chords the other instruments now weave quicker lines | The hymn, second time |
| ≈ 9:45 | The bright music again, more richly decorated | "Feeling new strength" again |
| ≈ 11:15 | The hymn for the last time, slower and fuller; the four lines grow together towards a climax | Marked "Mit innigster Empfindung", "with the most heartfelt feeling" |
| Near the end | A quiet chord, high and held, fading away | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the hymn's chords. They avoid the familiar pull of an ordinary major key, and the music seems to hover. That is the old mode at work.
2. Notice how the music after each hymn grows livelier: the convalescent, in Beethoven's own words, feels his strength returning.

**Time.** At about seventeen minutes, this one movement lasts as long as some whole quartets by Haydn. Most of that time belongs to the three slow hymns.

### IV. Alla marcia, assai vivace – Più allegro – Presto

**Summary.** A short, crisp march in A major, like a walk back into everyday life. It breaks off abruptly, and the first violin, alone over a trembling accompaniment, leads straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The march: a brisk tune in dotted rhythms, in two short halves, each repeated.
- The violin's speech: free, dramatic phrases like an opera singer's [[recitative]].

### V. Allegro appassionato – Presto

**Summary.** A passionate, driving [[rondo]] in A minor. Its main tune is one Beethoven had first sketched for the finale of the Ninth Symphony, before he decided to end that work with voices. At the end it speeds up and turns to A major.

**Main ideas**

- The main theme: a sighing, passionate melody in the first violin over a restless accompaniment in the other three.
- The contrasts: calmer episodes between returns of the theme.
- The turn to the major: in the Presto the theme is transformed into a bright, singing tune.

### Threads

- **Illness and recovery.** The hymn at the centre is a thank-you for recovery, and the quartet moves from a troubled A minor opening to a bright A major ending.
- **The old and the new.** Beethoven reaches back to an old church mode for the hymn, and to a dance he had written years earlier for the second movement, inside one of the most forward-looking works he wrote.
- **Four voices.** Listen for who leads: the cello at the start, the first violin in the trio, the recitative and the finale, and all four together in the hymn.
- **A famous listener.** The poet T. S. Eliot wrote to a friend that he had the A minor quartet on the gramophone and found it inexhaustible.
