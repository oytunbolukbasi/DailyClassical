### The big picture

- Sibelius trained as a violinist and as a young man wanted, in his own words, to be a celebrated violinist at any price. He realised he had started too late. This is the only concerto he wrote, and it is for his own instrument.
- The first version, played in Helsinki on 8 February 1904, was a failure: it was finished too late and was very hard to play. Sibelius withdrew it, cut it and revised it.
- The revised version, the one played today, was first heard in Berlin on 19 October 1905, with Karel Halíř as soloist and Richard Strauss conducting.
- The orchestra is a full partner, almost as in a symphony. In the first movement a long [[cadenza]] for the violin alone takes the place of the middle section, the [[development]].
- In one line: a lonely song over quiet strings (I), a warm, dark hymn (II), a stamping dance that Donald Tovey called "a polonaise for polar bears" (III).

### I. Allegro moderato

**Summary.** The violin enters almost at once, alone and high above a soft cushion of strings, with a long, cool melody. The movement alternates between the soloist and big, dark passages for the orchestra. At its centre the violin plays alone for several minutes, and the movement ends at top speed.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, cool and wide: a long melody for the violin over very soft strings, echoed by a clarinet.
- Theme 2, warm and heroic: a broader melody that rises to passionate octaves.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how quietly the concerto begins. The violin's first note emerges from almost nothing.
2. In the long cadenza there is no orchestra at all, yet the music keeps developing the themes. It is the movement's middle section, played by one instrument.

**In this recording.** Hilary Hahn recorded the concerto in Stockholm in March 2007 with Esa-Pekka Salonen, a Finnish conductor and composer. Her tempi are broad, so the first movement lasts over 17 minutes. On the Spotify album Schoenberg's Violin Concerto comes first; the Sibelius is tracks 4–6.

### II. Adagio di molto

**Summary.** A slow, song-like movement. After a short phrase in the woodwinds, the violin sings a long melody over plucked strings. The orchestra interrupts with a stormy passage, the violin answers with restless double stops, and the melody returns at a warm climax before the movement fades.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a long, warm melody for the violin, with dark brass chords underneath.

1. Listen under the violin's song in the first minutes. The brass chords do not simply support the melody: they rub against it, which gives the warmth a shadow.

### III. Allegro, ma non tanto

**Summary.** A heavy, stamping dance. Timpani and low strings set up a rhythm and the violin enters with a bold tune on its lowest string. A swaying second tune in the orchestra brings contrast, and the violin shows off in every way possible on the way to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The stamp: a rhythm in timpani and low strings, one long note and two short.
- Theme 1: a bold, rugged tune on the violin's lowest string.
- Theme 2: a swaying, waltz-like tune, first in the orchestra.

### Threads

- **A violinist's concerto.** Sibelius gave up his hopes of a solo career, but the concerto shows how well he knew the instrument: its dark low string, its high register and its fireworks.
- **Symphonic, not just a showpiece.** The orchestra has long passages of its own, and the first movement's cadenza does real musical work.
- **Darkness to D major.** The concerto begins in a cool D minor and ends in a stamping D major.
