### The big picture

- In September 1850 Robert and Clara Schumann moved to Düsseldorf on the Rhine, where he had become the city's director of music. The welcome was warm and he was in high spirits. He wrote this symphony in about five weeks, in November and December 1850.
- He visited Cologne and its cathedral, then still unfinished, in late September and again in early November. The fourth movement was first headed "in the character of the accompaniment to a solemn ceremony". It is often linked with the ceremony that made the Archbishop of Cologne a cardinal on 12 November 1850, but Schumann's diary suggests he was not there.
- He conducted the first performance in Düsseldorf in February 1851, and the audience applauded between the movements. It was the last symphony he composed. It is called No. 3 only because his D minor symphony of 1841 was revised and published later, as No. 4.
- It has five movements instead of four, and "Rhenish" is not his title: the name goes back to his first biographer, Wilhelm Joseph von Wasielewski. Schumann removed the descriptive headings before publication, wanting listeners to hear the music on its own terms.
- In one line: a striding, sunlit opening (I), a rolling country dance (II), a quiet interlude (III), a solemn ceremony under high vaults (IV), a festive finale (V).

### I. Lebhaft

**Summary.** No introduction: the whole orchestra plunges straight into a broad, striding theme. It is written in three, but it swings across the bar lines as if in slow, wide beats of two. The movement is in [[sonata form]], with a short, wistful second theme, a long development and a horn call that announces the return.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1, everyone: a heroic, upward-surging tune whose accents fall across the beat.
- Theme 2, oboes: a gentle, sighing melody in G minor.
- The horn call: Theme 1 stretched out into long notes, played by the four horns.

### II. Scherzo: Sehr mäßig

**Summary.** Called a scherzo but marked "very moderate", this is really a [[ländler]], a country dance in three. Its rolling tune, first in the lower strings and bassoons, keeps coming back in new dress. Many listeners hear in it the flow of the Rhine.

**Main ideas**

- The ländler: a sturdy, rolling melody in C major, low in the orchestra.
- The middle section: a softer tune in A minor in the horns and woodwinds, over a long-held note.

### III. Nicht schnell

**Summary.** A short, quiet interlude in A-flat major, with no trumpets, trombones or timpani. Clarinets, violins and violas pass gentle songs between them, and a little rising four-note figure keeps nudging the music on. Schumann first called it an intermezzo.

**Main ideas**

- The first song: a soft melody in the clarinets.
- The second song: a warmer line in the violins.
- The third song: a low, dark melody in the violas, with the cellos.

### IV. Feierlich

**Summary.** The cathedral movement. After a sharp chord, horns and trombones begin a slow, solemn theme in E-flat minor; the trombones have been silent until now. Schumann builds it up as strict [[counterpoint]], in the style of Bach: the theme enters in voice after voice, faster and slower, until it fills the orchestra like the sound of an organ under high vaults.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a slow, solemn line built from wide, hollow intervals.
- The interlude: a short answering figure in the strings that keeps returning.
- The fanfare: a bright call in the brass, near the end, in a far-off key.

### V. Lebhaft

**Summary.** The doors open and the music steps out into daylight: a quick, light-footed finale in E-flat major, full of tunes. Near the end the solemn theme of the cathedral movement returns, now quick and bright, and the symphony ends in a dash.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a nimble, bouncing tune with lively accents, for the whole orchestra.
- Theme 2: a crisp, detached tune in the strings.

### Threads

- **The Rhine and its cathedral.** The symphony grew out of Schumann's first months in the Rhineland. The fourth movement remembers the great unfinished cathedral at Cologne, and the finale carries its theme back out into the streets.
- **Five movements.** Between the dance (II) and the finale (V) Schumann places two slow movements of very different weight: a light interlude (III) and the solemn ceremony (IV).
- **Music without titles.** He wrote descriptive headings and then removed them. He felt that once the eye is pointed at something, the ear can no longer judge freely.
