### The big picture

- Elgar had sketched ideas for a symphony in E-flat as early as 1903. A trip to Italy with his wife in the spring of 1909, above all a visit to Venice, set the plan going again. He sketched it during 1910 and wrote out the full score in two months of hard work early in 1911, finishing on 28 February. At the foot of the score he wrote two place names, "Venice" and "Tintagel", the Cornish village where he had stayed in April 1910 at the summer home of his close friend Alice Stuart-Wortley.
- The score is headed with two lines by Shelley: "Rarely, rarely, comest thou, / Spirit of Delight!" The "Spirit of Delight" is the leaping, then plunging [[motto]] heard in the first bars; it comes back all through the symphony and once more at the very end.
- King Edward VII died in May 1910, while Elgar was at work, and the symphony is dedicated to his memory. The slow movement sounds like a funeral march, but Elgar told his publisher it was conceived before the king's death; friends he had lost, among them Alfred Rodewald in 1903, are also part of its story.
- Elgar conducted the first performance on 24 May 1911 at the Queen's Hall in London. The hall was not full and the applause was polite rather than warm; afterwards he told the violinist W. H. Reed that the audience sat there "like a lot of stuffed pigs". The symphony took years to find its public, helped by Adrian Boult, whose performance in 1920 made Elgar say his reputation was safe in his hands.
- In one line: energy and a ghostly night (I), a funeral march (II), the bright square outside and a fever that breaks in on it (III), and a long, calm close in which the Spirit of Delight returns (IV).

### I. Allegro vivace e nobilmente

**Summary.** The longest movement, and the busiest: a crowd of short themes, one after another, in a rhythm that swings in groups of three. In the middle the energy drains away and something stranger takes over, which Elgar described as a love scene in a garden at night "when the ghost of some memories comes through it".

**Main ideas**

- The Spirit of Delight: a wide leap upwards, then a plunge, in strings and high woodwind, the motto of the whole symphony.
- The quiet theme: a soft, wistful violin melody, which Elgar wanted played very quietly but with full expression.
- The cello song: a long, slow, singing line, tinged with nostalgia.

1. Listen for the falling shape of the motto in the first seconds. Once you know it, you will hear it in every movement, slowed down, darkened or broken up.

As a young man Barenboim worked often with John Barbirolli, one of the great Elgar conductors. Most recordings of the symphony have been made by British orchestras; here a German orchestra brings a dark, rich string sound to it.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** A slow march in C minor, with the tread of a funeral procession. Elgar connected its opening with the interior of St Mark's in Venice. Two great climaxes rise out of it, and when the march returns an oboe sings a free lament above it.

**Main ideas**

- The march: a grave melody in winds and brass over soft chords throbbing on the second and fourth beats.
- The sigh: a short, falling figure in the woodwind.
- The second theme: a hushed, lyrical melody in the strings, which later grows into a climax marked *[[nobilmente]]*, "nobly".

**Things to notice**

1. At the oboe's lament Elgar told the orchestra to imagine "a great crowd of silent people" watching a beloved sovereign pass, and the oboist to play freely: "It must sound as if it belonged outside somewhere". Listen to how loosely the oboe line floats over the strict march beneath it.

### III. Rondo. Presto

**Summary.** Out of the church into the sunlit square: Elgar said the main theme came from the "broken accent" of a tune played by street musicians in the Piazza San Marco. The [[rondo]] theme keeps coming back between other episodes, until a nightmare of hammering drums breaks in.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: quick, light and jerky, tossed between strings and woodwind.
- The sweeping theme: a loud, rushing minor-key melody in the strings.
- The hammering: a beat pounded out by drums, brass and tambourine that swamps everything.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | A quick, skittering tune, passed back and forth between strings and woodwind | The rondo theme |
| ≈ 0:35 | The strings sweep in loudly in the minor, with accents in brass and drums | The second theme |
| ≈ 2:10 | The skittering tune again, now in earnest | The rondo theme returns |
| ≈ 3:15–4:05 | Gentle woodwind melodies, like a pastoral scene; softly in the background the timpani tap a repeated beat | A calm episode, with a warning underneath |
| ≈ 4:20–5:25 | The tapping grows into a relentless pounding in drums, brass and tambourine; the ghostly figure from the first movement returns | The climax: "the madness that attends the excess or abuse of passion", Elgar said |
| ≈ 5:30 | The storm fades as if nothing had happened; the earlier themes return | The [[recapitulation]] |
| Last 20 seconds | A final rush and a hard, triumphant chord in C major | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. Elgar linked the pounding to lines in Tennyson's *Maud*, where a man imagines himself dead and buried and hears "the hoofs of the horses beat, beat into my scalp and brain". Notice how the beat starts quietly, long before it takes over.

### IV. Moderato e maestoso

**Summary.** A broad, steady finale in which, as Elgar wrote to his publisher, the sorrow of the earlier movements is "smoothed out and ennobled". It ends quietly, with the Spirit of Delight returning slowly, like a sunset.

**Main ideas**

- The walking theme: a calm, swaying melody in the cellos and low woodwind, every bar in the same rhythm.
- "Hans himself": a broad *nobilmente* theme, which Elgar linked with the conductor Hans Richter, his friend and champion.
- The motto: the Spirit of Delight, back at the end, now slow and soft.

**Things to notice**

1. The trumpet's high note at the climax is written to last one bar. At one performance the trumpeter Ernest Hall held it for two; Elgar was delighted, and many trumpeters have held it longer ever since.

### Threads

- **The Spirit of Delight.** The motto that opens the symphony returns in every movement in some form, from the ghost passage of the first to the slow, glowing reprise at the very end.
- **Venice and Tintagel.** The two places written on the score: the dark church and the bright square of Venice in the middle movements, and Tintagel, which has not been tied to any one passage.
- **A quiet ending.** Elgar's First Symphony ended in triumph; this one fades away. That may be one reason the first audience was cool, and why later listeners have come to treasure it.
