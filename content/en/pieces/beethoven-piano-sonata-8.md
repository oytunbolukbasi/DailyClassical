### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote it mostly in 1798, in his late twenties, when Vienna knew him above all as a fierce pianist. It was published at the end of 1799 and dedicated to Prince Karl Lichnowsky, one of his chief patrons.
- The name is not a later nickname. The first edition was titled *Grande Sonate pathétique*, and the title most likely came from Beethoven himself. Apart from *Les Adieux*, the nicknames of his other sonatas, such as "Moonlight", were added later by others. "Pathétique" means full of pathos: passionate and grave, not pitiful. The same word was later given to Tchaikovsky's Sixth Symphony.
- The slow, heavy opening does not stay at the door. It breaks back into the fast music twice. A reviewer noticed this as early as 1800.
- It was a hit at once. In 1804 the ten-year-old Ignaz Moscheles, later a famous pianist, copied it out by hand from a library copy because he could not afford to buy it. His teacher warned him against such "eccentric" music.
- In one line: thunder and flight (I), a song (II), a restless chase (III).

### I. Grave – Allegro di molto e con brio

**Summary.** A slow introduction of massive chords and sighs, then a fast, driving movement in [[sonata form]]. The slow music keeps coming back, and every return stops the storm in its tracks.

**Main ideas**

- The Grave: a heavy chord struck hard that dies away at once, answered by soft, sighing chords in a jagged, dotted rhythm.
- Theme 1: a quick line climbing in the right hand over a rumbling left hand that shakes between two low notes an octave apart.
- Theme 2: one hand keeps a soft pulse of chords in the middle while the other leaps across it, picking out a melody now deep in the bass, now high in the treble, with little grace notes.
- Theme 3: brighter, with quick rocking figures in both hands under a held high note.

**Things to notice**

1. Count the slow Grave music: at the start, before the development, and just before the end. Both returns are far shorter than the opening.
2. Listen to the left hand in the fast music. Its quick shaking between octaves is how one piano imitates a whole section of strings rumbling.

**In this recording.** Barenboim takes the repeat of the exposition (≈ 3:45) and goes back to the start of the fast music, not to the slow introduction. Some pianists, András Schiff among them, repeat the introduction too.

### II. Adagio cantabile

**Summary.** One of the best-known melodies Beethoven wrote, sung slowly in the middle of the keyboard. The movement is a simple [[rondo]]: the tune comes back three times, always in the same key, with two darker episodes between.

**Main ideas**

- The main melody: calm and warm, in a low-middle register, like a voice.
- First episode: a darker, more anxious melody in a minor key.
- Second episode: a hushed minor-key section over a quicker, rippling accompaniment in groups of three.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 0:00 | A slow, singing melody in the middle of the keyboard; under it a soft, steady murmur of short notes and a low bass | The main theme, played [[cantabile]]. One player, three layers |
| ≈ 0:35 | The same melody an octave higher, fuller and brighter | The theme repeated in a higher register |
| ≈ 1:10 | Darker; a new, more anxious melody in a minor key | The first episode, in F minor, moving to E-flat major |
| ≈ 2:05 | The main melody, back in its first, middle register, heard only once | The first return |
| ≈ 2:40 | Very quiet, in the minor; the murmur becomes a quicker ripple in threes | The second episode, in A-flat minor |
| ≈ 3:00 | Sudden loud accents; the music slips into a far-off key and finds its way back | A brief turn to E major |
| ≈ 3:40 | The main melody again, now carried on the rippling accompaniment from the episode | The last return. The tune is the same; what is under it has changed |
| ≈ 4:50 | Short phrases reach up high, then soft closing chords low in the piano | The [[coda]] |

**Things to notice**

1. The melody never changes key. All three times it is in A-flat major. Listen instead to what happens underneath it.
2. The tune spends most of its time near middle C, the range of a cello or a tenor voice, not the bright top of the piano.

### III. Rondo. Allegro

**Summary.** A quick, restless [[rondo]] back in C minor. The main tune keeps returning between brighter episodes, and the movement ends in a sudden, hard rush.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a quick, nervous tune in the right hand over a light, rocking left hand.
- First episode: a smoother, flowing tune in E-flat major.
- Middle episode: slow-moving lines in several voices in A-flat major, almost like a hymn.

### Threads

- **An orchestra in two hands.** Rumbling octaves like low strings, massive chords like a full orchestra, a singing line like a solo voice: one instrument plays every part.
- **High and low.** The hands cross in the first movement, the slow tune moves up an octave in the second, and the finale's last run tumbles from the top of the keyboard to the bottom.
- **The pedal.** In the Adagio the [[sustain pedal]] lets the low notes keep ringing while the hands move on, which is how one player can hold three layers together.
- **C minor.** Beethoven came back to this key for some of his most dramatic music, including the Fifth Symphony.
