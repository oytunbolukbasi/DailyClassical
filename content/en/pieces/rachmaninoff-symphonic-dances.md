### The big picture

- The Symphonic Dances were Rachmaninoff's last major work, and the only one he wrote entirely in the United States. He composed them in the summer and autumn of 1940, mostly at Orchard Point, an estate in Centerport on Long Island, overlooking the Sound. He was 67, and had left Russia more than twenty years before.
- He first called them *Fantastic Dances*, with the movements titled "Noon", "Twilight" and "Midnight". He dropped the titles before publication, but the idea of a day, or a life, moving towards night stays in the music.
- He dedicated the work to Eugene Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra, who gave the premiere on 3 January 1941. He also made a version for two pianos, and played the music through for the choreographer Michel Fokine, who wanted to make a ballet of it; Fokine's death in 1942 ended the plan.
- The music is full of memories: the theme of his First Symphony, a failure of 1897; the old chant of the dead, the [[Dies irae]]; and a chant of resurrection from his own *All-Night Vigil* of 1915. On the last page of the score he wrote: "I thank Thee, Lord".
- In one line: a march with a nostalgic song for saxophone (I), a ghostly waltz (II) and a midnight battle between death and resurrection (III).

### I. Non allegro

**Summary.** "Not fast": a march with sharp edges. Quiet pulsing in the strings, a little falling figure in the woodwinds, then heavy, hammered chords for the whole orchestra. In the middle the music slows and an alto saxophone, rare in a symphony orchestra, sings a long, nostalgic melody over the woodwinds alone. At the end the orchestra recalls, now in a glowing major key, the theme of his First Symphony.

**Main ideas**

- The march: a three-note falling figure and stamping, staccato chords.
- The saxophone melody: long, sad and Russian in feeling, over a soft bed of oboes, clarinets and cor anglais.
- The First Symphony theme: heard only at the end, transformed from minor into major.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the saxophone. Rachmaninoff writes the melody without any strings at all, so that its reedy, vocal sound stands out.
2. Listen to the theme at the end. In 1897 it belonged to the symphony whose disastrous premiere left him unable to compose for some three years; here, more than forty years later, it returns calm and in the major.

### II. Andante con moto (Tempo di valse)

**Summary.** A [[waltz]], but a strange one. It starts hesitantly, with fragments and muted chords; a solo violin and then the woodwinds try out the tune. The metre keeps shifting and the tempo ebbs and flows, so that the dance never quite settles. It swells to a sweeping climax and then slips away, like dancers seen through a window at dusk.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: muted, stuttering chords, like a ghostly invitation to dance.
- The waltz tune: a melancholy, sliding melody first for solo violin and woodwinds, then the strings.

### III. Lento assai – Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The "Midnight" movement. After a slow, dark opening, a restless dance breaks out, haunted by the [[Dies irae]], the medieval chant of the Day of Judgement that runs through so much of Rachmaninoff's music. A slow middle section brings a lamenting, falling figure in the strings and tolling bells. Then the dance returns, and a second chant enters: "Blessed art thou, O Lord" from his *All-Night Vigil*, a song of resurrection. Over it he wrote "Alliluya". The two chants fight it out to the end.

**Main ideas**

- The *Dies irae*: the chant of death, sometimes plain, sometimes hidden inside the dance.
- The resurrection chant: a broad, solemn melody from the *All-Night Vigil*.

**In this recording.** Jansons recorded the Dances with the St Petersburg Philharmonic in its own hall in September 1992, together with Rachmaninoff's Third Symphony; on Spotify the Dances are tracks 4–6 of that album. The violin solos are played by Vladimir Ovcharek.

### Threads

- **A day that becomes a life.** Noon, twilight, midnight: the original titles are gone, but the three dances still move from bright energy through a fading waltz to the night.
- **Old music remembered.** The First Symphony in movement I, the *All-Night Vigil* in movement III: in his last work Rachmaninoff looks back at his Russian past.
- **Death and resurrection.** The *Dies irae* haunts the whole work; the last movement answers it with a chant of resurrection and a word of thanks on the final page.
