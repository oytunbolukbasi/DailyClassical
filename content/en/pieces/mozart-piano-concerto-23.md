### The big picture

- Mozart entered the concerto in his own catalogue of works on 2 March 1786, while he was busy with *The Marriage of Figaro*. It is the middle one of three piano concertos he finished that winter, and he probably played it himself at one of his Lent subscription concerts in Vienna.
- The paper of the manuscript shows that he had started it in about 1784. When he came back to it, he crossed out the oboes and wrote in clarinets. The orchestra is small: one flute, two clarinets, two bassoons, two horns and strings, with no trumpets or drums.
- Mozart wrote the first movement's [[cadenza]] straight into the score, instead of on a separate sheet as he usually did.
- The slow movement is in F-sharp minor, the only movement Mozart wrote in that key. The pianist András Schiff calls it one of the most sorrowful pieces Mozart ever wrote, and it sits between two sunny movements in A major.
- In one line: a warm, singing Allegro with a surprise new tune (I), a lonely lament in a swaying rhythm (II), a quick finale full of new tunes and sudden turns (III).

### I. Allegro

**Summary.** A gentle, singing first movement in [[sonata form]] with a [[double exposition]]: the orchestra presents the main tunes first, and only then does the piano enter and present them again. Almost everything is soft and lyrical, but small shadows keep passing over it.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a calm tune in the violins, answered by the woodwinds. Its second chord is already slightly clouded.
- Theme 2: a quieter, sliding melody that begins in the strings and gradually draws in bassoon and flute.
- The new theme: a quiet tune that the orchestra keeps back until the piano has finished its exposition. The whole [[development]] is built on it.

**Things to notice**

1. The piano waits more than two minutes before it plays a note. Listen to how quietly it enters, with the same tune the strings began with.
2. The new theme at about 4:50 sounds like an afterthought, but the whole middle of the movement grows out of it.

### II. Adagio

**Summary.** A slow lament in F-sharp minor in a swaying [[siciliano]] rhythm. The piano begins alone, like a singer without accompaniment, and a brighter middle section only briefly lifts the mood.

**Main ideas**

- The lament: a slow, lilting tune for piano alone with unusually wide leaps, down and up.
- The consolation: a brighter tune in A major, introduced by the flute and the clarinet.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The piano alone plays a slow, rocking melody that leaps far down and back up | The main theme, in F-sharp minor |
| ≈ 0:50 | The orchestra answers softly; the clarinet, the violins and the bassoon follow each other and rub against each other | The orchestra takes up the lament. The clashing notes are deliberate |
| ≈ 2:30 | The mood brightens: the flute and the clarinet play a warm new tune and the piano replies | The middle section, in A major |
| ≈ 3:50 | The piano returns to the rocking minor melody | The opening section returns |
| ≈ 6:05 | Very quiet: the violins play with the bow, the lower strings pluck, and the piano's line thins out | The [[coda]]. The movement fades out in the minor |

**Things to notice**

1. In the coda, only the lower strings play [[pizzicato]]; the violins keep their bows. Mozart's manuscript shows this clearly, but older printed editions had all the strings plucking.

**Mozart's own word.** The score says Adagio, a truly slow tempo that Mozart rarely asked for in his concertos. Spotify's track list calls the movement "Andante"; that title is not Mozart's.

### III. Allegro assai

**Summary.** A fast, high-spirited finale that seems never to stop moving. It is a [[rondo]]: the piano's opening tune keeps coming back, but in between Mozart brings in new tunes and sudden changes of key, like scene changes in a comic opera.

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a quick, bouncing tune that the piano plays alone first.
- The piano's own tune: a gentler melody the orchestra never introduced.
- Two surprises: an episode in F-sharp minor and a clarinet tune in D major.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |

| ≈ 1:40 | A plaintive minor melody in the flute and bassoon over the strings; the piano takes it up and swerves into a brighter key | The second theme, in E minor |

| ≈ 4:05 | A simple, smiling tune in the clarinet and the woodwinds, the piano accompanying; then the piano plays it too | The surprise turn to D major |

**Things to notice**

1. Count how many different tunes appear. Mozart keeps adding new ones where you expect the refrain.
2. Listen for the clarinet at about 4:05. It is the same instrument that replaced the oboes in this concerto.

**In this recording.** Daniel Barenboim plays the piano and directs the Berlin Philharmonic from the keyboard, with no separate conductor.

### Threads

- **Clarinets.** Mozart swapped the oboes for clarinets, and they colour every movement: answering the first theme, bringing the light in the Adagio, and leading the surprise tune in the finale.
- **The piano starts alone.** In the first movement the piano waits for the orchestra to finish. In the second and third, it begins on its own.
- **F-sharp minor.** The key of the slow movement comes back as a shadow in the middle of the finale.
