### The big picture

- In 1879 the University of Breslau (today Wrocław, in Poland) gave Brahms an honorary doctorate. Brahms, who hated public fuss, sent back a short note of thanks. The conductor Bernhard Scholz, who had proposed him, explained that the university expected a musical thank-you, and joked: "Compose a fine symphony for us!" Brahms answered in the summer of 1880 with this overture.
- He called it "a very boisterous potpourri of student songs". It is built on four songs that German students sang at their gatherings, from a solemn hymn to a drinking song and the old Latin student anthem *Gaudeamus igitur* ("So let us rejoice"). The joke was to bring them, scored for a large orchestra and woven together with real craft, into an academic ceremony.
- Brahms conducted the first performance himself, in Breslau, on 4 January 1881, as part of the university's celebration of his degree. Drinking songs on such a solemn occasion annoyed some of the professors and delighted others.
- It is a [[concert overture]], written as a piece on its own, not to open a play or an opera. Brahms wrote it alongside a darker companion, the *Tragic Overture*, Op. 81. For it he used one of the biggest orchestras of all his works, with piccolo, contrabassoon, tuba, bass drum, cymbals and triangle.
- In one line: a hushed, mysterious march, three student songs in turn, a romp through all of them, and *Gaudeamus igitur* in full splendour.

### I. Allegro – Maestoso – Animato – Maestoso

**Summary.** One continuous piece in four sections. It opens quietly, almost secretively, then the songs arrive one after another: a solemn hymn in the trumpets, a broad tune in the strings, a comic song in the bassoons. The middle tumbles through them, and the end is *Gaudeamus igitur*, played by the whole orchestra as a grand finale.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: a hushed, tiptoeing march in C minor, short notes in the strings.
- "Wir hatten gebauet ein stattliches Haus" ("We had built a stately house"): a solemn song of the student fraternities, first played softly by the trumpets.
- "Hört, ich sing das Lied der Lieder" ("Hear, I sing the song of songs"), the *Landesvater*: a broad, ceremonious tune.
- "Was kommt dort von der Höh" ("What's that coming from the hill"), the *Fuchslied* or freshmen's song: light and cheeky, introduced by two bassoons.
- *Gaudeamus igitur*: the student anthem, saved for the end.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the bassoons around four minutes in. Brahms gives the freshmen's song to the instruments most likely to make an audience smile.
2. At the end, under the grand tune, listen to the strings racing up and down in scales. Brahms shows his "academic" skill even while celebrating.

**In this recording.** Szell recorded the overture with the Cleveland Orchestra in Severance Hall, its home, in October 1966, alongside Brahms's Fourth Symphony. His performance lasts nearly eleven minutes; most take about ten.

### Threads

- **A joke with serious craft.** Brahms answered a learned honour with student songs, but he wove them together with all the skill of a "doctor" of music.
- **Two overtures.** The *Academic Festival Overture* and the *Tragic Overture* were written side by side in 1880, one bright and one dark.
- **Songs everyone knew.** The audience in Breslau recognised every tune. *Gaudeamus igitur* is still sung at graduation ceremonies in many European countries.
