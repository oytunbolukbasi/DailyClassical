### The big picture

- Schubert was 22 when he wrote it, in 1819, after a summer walking and making music in Upper Austria with his friend the singer Johann Michael Vogl. In the small town of Steyr they met Sylvester Paumgartner, a wealthy music lover and amateur cellist, who asked for a quintet.
- Paumgartner loved Schubert's song *Die Forelle* ("The Trout", 1817), about a trout darting in a clear brook until an angler muddies the water and catches it. He asked for variations on it. They became the fourth movement, and gave the quintet its name.
- The line-up is unusual: piano, violin, viola, cello and double bass, not the usual string quartet. The double bass takes care of the low notes, which leaves the cello free to sing, and leaves the piano free to play high and light. Schubert probably took the idea from music by Johann Nepomuk Hummel arranged for the same five instruments.
- It has five movements, not the usual four, and was published only in 1829, a year after Schubert's death.
- In one line: a bright, open-air Allegro (I), a slow walk through three songs (II), a quick, stamping dance (III), the trout song and its variations (IV), a folk-flavoured finale (V).

### I. Allegro vivace

**Summary.** A loud chord, a quick upward ripple on the piano, and the music opens out like a window thrown open in summer. The movement is in [[sonata form]], full of sudden turns into unexpected keys, and the piano spends much of its time high up, playing the tune in both hands an octave apart.

**Main ideas**

- The upward ripple: a fast run of notes climbing the piano, first heard after the opening chord. It comes back again and again, like a fish flicking upward.
- Theme 1: a calm, wide melody first played by the violin, gently answered by the piano.
- Theme 2: a tender tune passed between the violin and the cello, in a new key.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the double bass. It plays the lowest line, under everyone, and that is why the cello can sing high melodies.
2. The piano often plays the tune in octaves, both hands the same notes far apart. It gives a clear, bell-like sound that floats above the strings.

**In this recording.** Curzon and the Vienna players leave out the repeat of the exposition, so the movement is about four minutes shorter than in recordings that take it.

### II. Andante

**Summary.** A slow movement built from three different songs, one after another. Then all three come back in new keys, so the movement falls into two matching halves. Each song has its own colour: the piano alone, the viola and cello together, and a lilting tune with a skipping rhythm.

**Main ideas**

- Song 1: a calm, simple melody on the piano in octaves.
- Song 2: a darker, longing duet for viola and cello.
- Song 3: a tune with a skipping, dotted rhythm, light and graceful.

### IV. Andantino (theme and variations)

**Summary.** The heart of the quintet. The strings play the melody of Schubert's song *Die Forelle*, and then each [[variation]] gives the tune to a different instrument and dresses it differently. At the end the piano plays the song's own rippling accompaniment, the trout darting in the water.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: the song's melody, simple and cheerful, played by the strings alone.
- The rippling figure: quick notes rising and falling in the piano, the original song's picture of the fish in the stream.

| ≈ 4:40 | Slower and more thoughtful; the cello leads a wandering, wistful version of the tune into distant keys | Variation 5, starting in B-flat major |

**Things to notice**

1. Follow the melody as it moves: strings, piano, viola and cello, double bass, then cello alone. Every variation gives it to someone new.
2. In the last variation, the rippling piano part is taken from the song. In *Die Forelle* it pictures the trout swimming in the stream.

### V. Finale. Allegro giusto

**Summary.** A lively finale with a strong, folk-dance flavour. Each phrase starts with a hammered accent. Like the slow movement, it falls into two matching halves: everything in the first half comes back in the second, in a different key.

**Main ideas**

- The main tune: begins with a single loud, accented note, then a lively, bouncing melody.
- The second tune: a smoother, more lyrical melody, passed between strings and piano.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | One loud, hammered note, then a quick, bouncing tune in the strings; the piano answers | The main tune, with a folk-dance flavour |
| ≈ 0:50 | The piano takes over with fast, bright runs | The music moves towards a new key |
| ≈ 1:30 | A smoother, singing tune, in the violin and then the piano | The second tune |
| ≈ 2:30 | Lively dotted rhythms and short, punchy chords | The first half winds up |
| ≈ 3:30 | The hammered note and bouncing tune again, but in another key | The second half: a copy of the first, moved to a new key |

**Things to notice**

1. When the hammered note returns around the middle, the music is starting again from the top, but in a different key. Listen for the moment it slides back home.

**In this recording.** The repeat of the finale's first half is optional, and many players leave it out. Its length here (6:54) suggests Curzon and the Vienna players play each half once.

### Threads

- **Water.** The trout song is the centre, but rippling, flowing figures run through the whole quintet, from the piano's upward flicks in the first movement to the song's accompaniment in the variations.
- **The double bass.** Putting a double bass in a chamber group frees everyone else: the cello can sing, and the piano can play high and light, often in octaves.
- **Halves that match.** The slow movement and the finale both play their material twice, the second time in new keys. Schubert enjoys how a key change alone can change a tune's colour.
- **Holiday music.** Written after a summer in the country with friends, for an amateur cellist and his circle, it is music for sharing, not for showing off.
