### The big picture

- Debussy wrote the quartet in 1892–93, when he was 31 and still little known, in the same years as his *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune*. It is his only string quartet, and the only work he ever published with an opus number.
- He meant to dedicate it to his friend and fellow composer Ernest Chausson, who had reservations about it. Debussy began a second quartet to answer his doubts, but their friendship broke off and the second quartet was never finished.
- The Ysaÿe Quartet, led by the Belgian violinist Eugène Ysaÿe, gave the first performance at a concert of the Société nationale de musique in Paris on 29 December 1893. The reaction was mixed: one Belgian critic called it more of a hallucination than a dream.
- The whole work grows from one short theme. It opens the first movement, drives the second, is hinted at in the third and returns, transformed, in the finale. This is [[cyclic form]], a technique associated with César Franck and his circle, but the harmonies, the old church scales and the play of colour are entirely his own.
- In one line: a bold theme (I), the same theme plucked and dancing (II), a muted dream (III), a slow awakening that ends in a rush of energy (IV).

### I. Animé et très décidé

**Summary.** All four instruments launch the theme together, forcefully, with a quick little turn in it. The theme uses an old church scale, so it sounds both ancient and strange. The movement follows [[sonata form]] loosely: a second, gentler melody, a restless middle where the theme is broken up and passed around, and a return of the opening before a vigorous close.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: short and decisive, with a quick turn in the middle, played by all four together. Everything in the quartet comes from it.
- The second melody: a long, singing line in the first violin, softer and warmer.

### II. Assez vif et bien rythmé

**Summary.** The scherzo. The viola plays the main theme again and again, bowed and stubborn, while the other three pluck around it in shifting, off-beat patterns. In the middle the bows come back and the theme is stretched into a long, singing line. Then the plucking returns. Manuel de Falla heard in this movement one of the most beautiful Andalusian dances ever written.

**Main ideas**

- The theme from the first movement, reshaped by the viola into a short, repeated figure, an [[ostinato]].
- The plucked accompaniment: [[pizzicato]] chords and rhythms that pull against the beat, like a guitar.

### III. Andantino, doucement expressif

**Summary.** The slow movement, played with [[muted]] strings for most of its length. It is the most intimate music in the quartet: soft, slow melodies in a warm, distant key, with a more passionate middle section that begins with the viola alone. The first movement's theme is only hinted at. Then the mutes return and the music fades into silence.

**Main ideas**

- The opening melody: slow and veiled, rising and falling gently over soft, close harmonies.
- The viola's melody: a longer, more searching line that starts the middle section.

### IV. Très modéré – Très mouvementé et avec passion

**Summary.** The finale starts slowly, almost groping in the dark, with fragments that recall the slow movement and the theme from the start. The pace gathers until the music breaks into a fast, passionate main section. The theme returns in many new shapes, and at the end it bursts out bright and fast, as a whirlwind close.

**Main ideas**

- The slow introduction: low, searching lines that gradually speed up.
- The fast music: a restless, driving idea built from the opening theme.
- The theme's last disguise: fast, bright and in a major key, at the very end.

### Threads

- **One theme, many shapes.** The same short theme opens the work, dances in the scherzo, is hinted at in the slow movement and blazes out at the end.
- **Four voices as an orchestra.** Plucking, muting, rapid tremolos and close, rich chords give the quartet a range of colours that sounds larger than four instruments.
- **Old scales, new harmonies.** Debussy borrows church modes and harmonies that move freely, without the usual pull towards home. Critics in 1893 were puzzled; ten years later Ravel modelled his own quartet on this one.
