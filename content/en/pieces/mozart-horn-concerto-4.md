### The big picture

- Mozart wrote his horn concertos in Vienna for his friend Joseph Leutgeb, a horn player he had known since childhood in Salzburg and one of the finest soloists of his day. On 26 June 1786, while *The Marriage of Figaro* was new on the stage, Mozart entered this concerto in his catalogue of works as "a Waldhorn concerto for Leitgeb": *Waldhorn*, "forest horn", is the German name of the instrument.
- Mozart teased Leutgeb in his manuscripts; on another concerto he called him an "ass, ox and simpleton". This score is written in four colours of ink, red, green, blue and black. It has long been read as a joke, but scholars have suggested the colours were a code for how loud or how softly to play.
- Leutgeb played the [[natural horn]], without valves. It can sound only certain notes by lip alone; the others are made by moving the hand inside the bell, which also muffles them. Mozart's runs and his song-like slow movement asked a great deal of him.
- "No. 4" is a publisher's number: the concerto known as No. 1 was in fact the last, left unfinished in 1791. Mozart's manuscript of this one survives only in part: the whole first movement is missing.
- In one line: a broad, good-humoured opening with a [[cadenza]] (I), a gentle song (II), and a galloping hunting [[rondo]] (III).

### I. Allegro maestoso

**Summary.** The orchestra sets out the main ideas first, then the horn enters and makes them its own, in long singing lines and quick runs. Along the way Mozart gives the horn chromatic lines, full of notes that the natural horn could only reach with the hand in the bell. Near the end the orchestra stops and the soloist plays a [[cadenza]] alone.

**Main ideas**

- The opening tune: broad and confident, with a firm, marching step.
- The horn's songs: smooth, long-breathed phrases, often in the instrument's warm middle range.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how smoothly the horn moves between high and low notes in the long phrases. On the valveless horn of Mozart's time, every change of note was a matter of lip and hand.

**In this recording.** Dennis Brain was 32 when he recorded the four concertos with Karajan in November 1953, on a modern valve horn. He died in a car crash four years later, aged 36. His cadenza in this movement, taken down from this recording, is now printed in the Bärenreiter edition of the concerto.

### II. Romance: Andante cantabile

**Summary.** A romance was a gentle, song-like piece, and this one is almost a song without words for the horn. The tune comes back several times between short episodes, and Mozart asks for moments of echo, the horn answering itself more softly.

**Main ideas**

- The song: a calm, rocking tune in three beats.
- The echoes: short phrases repeated very softly, like a sound from far off.

### III. Rondo: Allegro vivace

**Summary.** The horn's old life as a hunting instrument comes back in a bouncing tune in six-time, built mainly from the notes of the natural horn's own scale. The tune returns again and again, like a refrain, between solo episodes. It is one of Mozart's best-known melodies; in 1963 Flanders and Swann set it to comic words as "Ill Wind".

**Main ideas**

- The refrain: a galloping hunting call, short notes bouncing over a steady pulse.
- The episodes: longer solo passages for the horn between the returns.

1. Hum the refrain after you have heard it twice. Its notes are almost all the ones a horn without valves plays naturally, which is why it sounds like a call across the fields.

### Threads

- **A friend.** All three movements were made for one player, Leutgeb, and for what he did best: singing lines and agile calls.
- **The instrument.** The concerto is written for a horn without valves; modern players, like Brain, play it on a valve horn, while period players return to the natural horn and its hand-stopped colours.
- **From song to hunt.** The first movement mixes march and song, the Romance is pure song, and the finale goes back to the horn's origin, the hunt.
