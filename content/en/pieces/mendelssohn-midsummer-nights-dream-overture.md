### The big picture

- In 1826, at home in Berlin, Felix Mendelssohn read Shakespeare's *A Midsummer Night's Dream* in the German translation by August Wilhelm Schlegel, made with help from Ludwig Tieck. At seventeen and a half he finished this overture, on 6 August that year.
- It is a concert overture: a piece for the concert hall that sums up the play in music, not the prelude to a staging. It was first performed in Stettin (today Szczecin, Poland) on 20 February 1827, conducted by Carl Loewe.
- Sixteen years later, King Frederick William IV of Prussia asked Mendelssohn for music for a production of the play. The overture became the first number of that incidental music, Op. 61, first heard at Potsdam in 1843, which also includes the famous *Wedding March*.
- The musical scholar George Grove called the overture "the greatest marvel of early maturity that the world has ever seen in music".
- In one line: four magic chords, the fairies' scurrying, the court of Athens, the young lovers, the clumsy workmen and a donkey's bray, and the fairies again, who have the last word.

### I. Allegro di molto

**Summary.** One movement in [[sonata form]], in which each theme stands for a group of characters from the play. Four slow chords in the woodwinds open a door into the fairy world; the violins scurry very quietly and very fast; then the full orchestra brings in the human world of the duke's court, the lovers and the workmen. The fairies take over again, and the four chords close the door.

**Main ideas**

- The four chords: long, soft woodwind chords, each one changing the colour a little.
- The fairies: the violins, divided into many parts, playing quick, light notes almost in a whisper, in E minor.
- The court: a loud, festive theme for the whole orchestra, in E major.
- The lovers: a warm, sighing melody.
- The workmen and Bottom: a clumsy dance tune, and the strings imitating a donkey's "hee-haw" in big leaps down.
- A low voice: the score includes an [[ophicleide]], a deep brass instrument with keys, which adds weight under the loud passages. Today it is often replaced by a tuba.

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to how quiet the violins are at the start, and how long they keep it up. Mendelssohn asks for very fast, very soft playing from the whole section.
2. Count the four chords at the very beginning and the very end. They frame the overture like the beginning and end of a dream.

**In this recording.** Peter Maag recorded the overture and incidental music with the London Symphony Orchestra in Kingsway Hall in February 1957. Reviewers of its CD reissue still praise the orchestra's sparkle, though the 1957 sound is a little limited.

### Threads

- **A play in miniature.** Each theme is a character or a group from Shakespeare: fairies, court, lovers, workmen. The overture follows their worlds rather than the plot.
- **Weber.** Carl Maria von Weber, whose fairy opera *Oberon* was first staged in London in April 1826, died that June. Mendelssohn's overture borrows a theme from it, perhaps as a tribute.
- **Sixteen years later.** When Mendelssohn wrote the rest of the music for the play in 1842–43, he took ideas from his teenage overture, such as the donkey's bray for the workmen's dance and the four chords for the very last moment of the play.
