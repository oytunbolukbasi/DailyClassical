### The big picture

- In the spring of 1913 Holst spent a holiday in Spain with the writer Clifford Bax, who introduced him to astrology. Holst called it his "pet vice" and cast his friends' horoscopes. *The Planets* is about the planets' characters in astrology, not about astronomy: each one "brings" something, war, peace, jollity, old age.
- He wrote it between 1914 and 1916 and finished the orchestration in 1917. Earth is left out, and Pluto had not been discovered. Holst called the movements "a series of mood pictures".
- The orchestra is huge: six horns, four trumpets, a tenor tuba, two timpani players, celesta, organ, two harps, and in the last movement a choir of women's voices, out of sight.
- Adrian Boult conducted the first performance on 29 September 1918 in the Queen's Hall, London, for about 250 invited guests; Holst's friend Balfour Gardiner paid for it. The first complete public performance came in November 1920. The work made Holst famous, and the fame made him uneasy for the rest of his life.
- In one line: war (I), peace (II), a quicksilver messenger (III), a festival (IV), old age (V), a magician (VI) and a mystic who disappears into silence (VII).

### I. Mars, the Bringer of War

**Summary.** A march that cannot march: it has five beats to the bar instead of two or four, and the hammering rhythm never lets go. It starts very quietly and grows into one of the loudest, most dissonant climaxes in the orchestral repertoire. Holst wrote it in mid-1914, before the First World War broke out, but it has been heard as a picture of mechanised war ever since.

**Main ideas**

- The rhythm: a five-beat pattern, tapped out by strings hitting the strings with the wood of the bow, harp and timpani.
- The theme: a slow, rising line in the low brass and woodwinds that climbs a few notes and slides back.
- The fanfare: a call in the tenor tuba, answered by trumpets, in the quieter middle.

### II. Venus, the Bringer of Peace

**Summary.** After the war, stillness. A horn asks a quiet question, flutes and oboes answer, and harps and celesta rock gently underneath. The middle brings a warm violin melody and a tender oboe solo; then the opening returns, more transparent than before.

**Main ideas**

- The horn call: four rising notes, very soft, answered by falling chords in the woodwinds.
- The violin melody: a high, singing line, first for a solo violin and later for all the violins.
- The oboe solo: a long, romantic tune at the heart of the movement.

### III. Mercury, the Winged Messenger

**Summary.** The shortest movement, a [[scherzo]] in quick, flickering notes. Little figures dart from instrument to instrument, often in two keys at once, so the music never quite lands. In the middle a solo violin plays a simple tune, as if the messenger stopped for a moment.

**Main ideas**

- The flicker: a fast, light figure in six beats, thrown between woodwinds and strings.
- The tune: a short, folk-like melody, first on a solo violin, high.

### IV. Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity

**Summary.** A festival. Bustling strings and six horns set off a string of dance tunes, each bigger than the last. In the middle the music slows to half speed for a broad, noble melody that Holst later turned into a famous hymn. The festivities return, the melody comes back once more, and a final dash ends on a single loud chord.

**Main ideas**

- The opening: busy strings and a bouncing horn tune that pushes against the beat.
- The dance tunes: several, one after another, some stamping in three beats.
- The big tune: a slow, broad melody, first in the strings and horns. In 1921 Holst set it as the hymn tune "Thaxted", for the words "I vow to thee, my country".

### V. Saturn, the Bringer of Old Age

**Summary.** Said to have been Holst's own favourite movement. A slow procession: two chords tick back and forth like a clock while a low line plods beneath them. A solemn melody in the trombones grows into a crushing climax with clanging bells, and then, unexpectedly, peace: old age accepted.

**Main ideas**

- The clock: two soft chords swaying back and forth in flutes, harps and bassoons.
- The procession: a slow, heavy tune in the low strings, then a solemn melody in the trombones.
- The ending: a calm, high, gently moving music, with harps and flutes.

### VI. Uranus, the Magician

**Summary.** Four loud notes in the brass, like a spell, then a clumsy dance in the bassoons that gets more and more out of hand. It reaches a huge climax, with the organ sweeping up and down the keyboard, and suddenly collapses into near silence.

**Main ideas**

- The spell: four slow, loud notes, first in the brass, then in the tubas and the timpani.
- The dance: a jaunty, galumphing tune in the bassoons, in a heavy six-beat swing.
- The march: a pompous tune in the brass that joins the dance.

### VII. Neptune, the Mystic

**Summary.** The quietest music in the work, from first note to last. Flutes and harps drift in a rocking five-beat motion, chords clash softly, and then women's voices, singing without words from out of sight, join the orchestra. At the end the orchestra falls silent and the voices fade away as a door between them and the hall is slowly closed.

**Main ideas**

- The drift: soft, swaying figures in flutes, oboes and piccolo.
- The chords: two unrelated chords played together by the brass, a cold and strange sound.
- The voices: a choir of women, unseen, singing wordless lines.

**Listening stops**

| ≈ 0:00 | Flutes play a soft, rocking figure, joined by piccolo and oboes | The opening, *pianissimo*, in five beats |

**Things to notice**

1. Do not stop the music before it ends. The last chord is not resolved, and the voices disappear rather than finish, one of the first fade-outs in music.

### Threads

- **Astrology, not astronomy.** The titles come from the planets' characters in astrology, partly from the writings of the astrologer Alan Leo. Each movement is a mood, not a picture of a planet.
- **The rhythm of five.** Mars and Neptune, the first and last movements, are both in five beats to the bar: one hammering, the other floating. The same uneven pulse makes war and mystery.
- **Bells and light.** Celesta, harp and glockenspiel return again and again (Venus, Mercury, Neptune) for a cold, starry glitter, and the bells of Saturn mark the passing of time.
