### The big picture

- Written between 1888 and 1894. The first movement began as a separate funeral piece, *Totenfeier* (Funeral Rites). Mahler conducted the first complete performance in Berlin on 13 December 1895.
- For years he could not find an ending. At the conductor Hans von Bülow's funeral in 1894 he heard a sung setting of Klopstock's hymn "Die Auferstehung" (The Resurrection). It struck him "like lightning". He kept the first verses and wrote the rest of the text himself.
- The forces are huge: soprano and alto soloists, a choir, an organ, church bells, and horns, trumpets and drums placed [[offstage]], out of sight.
- Mahler asked for a pause of five minutes after the first movement. Most performances take a shorter break.
- In one line: a funeral (I), a memory of happy days (II), life as an empty whirl (III), a simple prayer (IV), the end of the world and a resurrection (V).

### I. Allegro maestoso

**Summary.** A funeral, and a question: is there anything after death? Violent outbursts alternate with a tender melody that seems to come from another, happier world.

**Main ideas**

- Theme 1: a furious growl in the cellos and basses under trembling violins.
- Theme 2: a soft, rising melody in the violins, in a bright and distant key.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Violins and violas shiver; cellos and basses snarl upwards, again and again | The opening. Mahler marks it "with complete gravity and solemnity" |
| ≈ 1:00 | Heavy, marching chords in the full orchestra, then wailing woodwinds | The funeral march |
| ≈ 1:40 | The storm clears. Violins sing a gentle, rising melody | Theme 2, in E major, far from the home key |
| ≈ 3:00 | The snarling basses return | The themes are presented again, changed |
| ≈ 5:30 | Quiet: harp, cor anglais and violins drift in a pastoral dream | The start of the [[development]] |
| ≈ 8:30 | Low woodwinds and brass intone a slow, solemn tune | The [[Dies irae]], the chant for the dead, which will return in the finale |
| ≈ 11:00 | The music piles up to a shattering climax and falls down a chromatic scale | The crisis of the movement |
| ≈ 12:30 | The opening shiver and snarl, at full force | The [[recapitulation]] |
| Last minute | A plunging scale in the full orchestra, then a few hard chords | The funeral ends in C minor, without answers |

**Things to notice**

1. Mahler wanted silence after this movement. If you can, pause here before going on.

### II. Andante moderato

**Summary.** A slow, graceful country dance, "never hurried", like a snapshot of happier days. Two darker episodes interrupt it.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Strings in a gentle, swaying tune in three | A [[Ländler]], a slow Austrian country dance |
| ≈ 2:00 | Running triplets in the strings, more restless | The first episode |
| ≈ 3:30 | The dance returns; cellos add a warm countermelody underneath | The Ländler, richer |
| ≈ 5:00 | The running figures return, louder, with brass | The second episode, now stormy |
| ≈ 7:00 | The dance tune again, but now the strings pluck it | The tune played [[pizzicato]], light as a memory |
| Last minute | Harp and soft strings fade out | The memory closes |

### III. In ruhig fließender Bewegung

**Summary.** A [[scherzo]] that never stops flowing. It is based on Mahler's song about Saint Anthony preaching to the fishes, who listen, nod, and swim off unchanged. Under the humour lies a view of life as pointless motion.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Two hard timpani strokes, then softer ones; violins start an endless winding line | The scherzo begins |
| ≈ 1:00 | A shrill clarinet plays a sly, slightly tipsy tune | The humour turns sour |
| ≈ 3:30 | Trumpets and strings in a brighter, more open tune | The [[trio]] |
| ≈ 5:30 | The winding violins return | The scherzo again |
| ≈ 8:30 | The flow breaks off in a huge, dissonant scream from the full orchestra | Mahler called this a "cry of despair". It will open the finale |
| Last minute | The winding line runs on, thinner, and stops | Motion without meaning |

### IV. Urlicht

**Summary.** After the scream, a prayer. An alto sings a simple song from *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* (The Boy's Magic Horn), the German folk-poetry collection Mahler loved: a soul longing for God and for release from pain.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The alto alone sings "O Röschen rot!" (O little red rose) | The voice enters with no introduction |
| ≈ 0:20 | Soft brass, low and hushed, like a distant hymn | A [[chorale]] in trumpets, horns and bassoons |
| ≈ 2:00 | The pace quickens; a solo violin glitters | In the poem an angel tries to turn the singer away. The singer refuses |
| ≈ 3:00 | The voice rises, slow and certain | The soul will find its way back to God |
| Last seconds | A long, quiet chord | The finale bursts in almost at once |

### V. Im Tempo des Scherzos

**Summary.** Half an hour of music in two parts. First the orchestra alone: the end of the world, the dead marching out of their graves, voices calling from far away. Then, out of total silence, a choir sings of resurrection.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The scream from the third movement crashes in at full force | The finale begins |
| ≈ 1:30 | Distant horns call from out of sight over hushed strings | The first [[offstage]] call, a voice from far away |
| ≈ 3:30 | Soft woodwinds, then a quiet, noble tune in the trombones | The [[Dies irae]], then the resurrection theme the choir will sing |
| ≈ 9:30 | Two long drum rolls swell from nothing to a roar | The dead rise. A frantic march begins |
| ≈ 15:00 | A pleading melody in the orchestra while a band offstage blares and thunders | The march collapses into chaos |
| ≈ 18:30 | Horns and trumpets call from far away; a flute imitates a bird above the silence | Mahler's "great summons", the last call before judgement |
| ≈ 21:30 | The choir enters almost inaudibly, the orchestra nearly silent: "Aufersteh'n" (Rise again) | Klopstock's hymn. The soprano soars out of the choir |
| ≈ 27:00 | The alto sings an urgent plea | Mahler's own words begin: "O believe, my heart" |
| ≈ 30:00 | Soprano and alto in duet, then the choir rises on "wings" | The music climbs towards E-flat major |
| ≈ 32:00 | Full choir, organ and orchestra at maximum power, then pealing bells | The resurrection. The bells ring out to the end |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the space: some of the brass is meant to come from outside the hall. In a good recording you can hear the distance.
2. The choir's first entry is marked to be sung as quietly as possible. Turn the volume up a little before ≈ 21:30, not after.

**In this recording.** Klemperer knew the symphony from the inside. In 1905, aged 20, he was put in charge of the offstage band at a Berlin performance, met Mahler there, and later got his first conducting post on Mahler's recommendation.

### Threads

- **From funeral to resurrection.** The symphony starts in C minor, at a graveside, and ends in E-flat major, in light.
- **The chant for the dead.** The [[Dies irae]] appears in the first movement and again in the finale, where the resurrection theme finally answers it.
- **Near and far.** Calls from offstage horns, trumpets and drums give the symphony a sense of distance that no other work of its time had.
