### The big picture

- A requiem is usually the Catholic Mass for the dead, sung in Latin, praying for the souls of the departed. Brahms, a Protestant from Hamburg, did something different. He chose his own texts from Martin Luther's German Bible, and his first words are for those left behind: "Blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted." He said he would gladly have called it "a human Requiem". "German" refers to the language.
- His mother died in February 1865, and the loss probably moved him to write it; the death of his mentor Robert Schumann in 1856 may also lie behind it. The second movement uses music he had sketched in 1854, the year of Schumann's breakdown. By August 1866 six movements were finished.
- The first three movements were tried out in Vienna on 1 December 1867, and the performance went badly. The first full performance of the six movements, in Bremen Cathedral on Good Friday, 10 April 1868, with Brahms conducting, was a great success and made his name.
- In May 1868 he added a movement for soprano, now the fifth, with the words "I will comfort you, as one whom his mother comforteth". The complete work in seven movements was first heard in Leipzig on 18 February 1869. It is Brahms's longest work.
- In one line: comfort for the mourners (I), all flesh is as grass (II), a man asks how long his life will be (III), the lovely dwellings of the Lord (IV), a mother's comfort (V), the last trumpet and death's defeat (VI), and blessed are the dead (VII).

### I. Selig sind, die da Leid tragen

**Summary.** "Blessed are they that mourn." A soft, dark opening without violins: only the lower strings, horns and woodwinds, over a long held bass note. The choir enters almost in a whisper. In the middle the music moves from tears to joy: those who sow in tears shall reap in joy.

**Main ideas**

- The colour: no violins at all in this movement, so the sound stays low and warm, with divided violas and cellos.
- The choir's first phrase: a gentle rising step, then a softer fall, on "Selig sind".

**Listening stops**

**Things to notice**

1. Listen for the moment the harp appears at the very end. It is the first time you hear it, and it brings a sudden light.

### II. Denn alles Fleisch, es ist wie Gras

**Summary.** A funeral march, but in three beats instead of two, slow and heavy, with timpani pounding beneath. "For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass." A lighter section asks for patience, like the farmer waiting for rain. The march returns, louder, and then the music bursts into the major: "But the word of the Lord endureth for ever", and a joyful, fast section about the redeemed coming home.

**Main ideas**

- The march: a slow tread in three, muted strings, the choir singing the melody quietly in [[unison]] at first.
- The answer: a fast, bright B-flat major, the voices entering one after another.

### III. Herr, lehre doch mich

**Summary.** The baritone soloist sings a prayer: "Lord, teach me that I must have an end." The choir takes up his words. Doubt grows, "my life is as nothing before thee", until the question "Lord, what do I wait for?" turns to "my hope is in thee". The movement ends with a [[fugue]] over a single low D held for the whole section.

**Main ideas**

- The prayer: the baritone alone, answered by the choir with the same words.
- The fugue: a strong subject passed between the voices, all resting on a held bass note.

**Listening stops**

### IV. Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen

**Summary.** The centre of the work, and its calmest movement: a gentle waltz-like song in three beats, "How lovely are thy dwellings, O Lord of hosts". It is often sung on its own.

**Main ideas**

- The melody: a falling, swaying phrase in the woodwinds, then in the choir.
- A livelier middle section in which the voices overlap.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Woodwinds play a gentle, swaying tune; the choir answers softly | "Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen" ("How lovely are thy dwellings", Psalm 84:1) |
| ≈ 1:30 | A warmer, more longing phrase | "Meine Seele verlanget" ("My soul longeth") |

**Things to notice**

1. Notice the lilt of three beats to a bar. After two dark movements, it is the first music that almost dances.

### V. Ihr habt nun Traurigkeit

**Summary.** The movement Brahms added after the Bremen premiere. The soprano soloist sings "You now have sorrow, but I will see you again." Under her the choir sings, slowly and softly, "I will comfort you, as one whom his mother comforteth". Many hear in it Brahms's grief for his own mother, though he never said so.

**Main ideas**

- The soprano's line: high, floating and tender.
- The choir's line: slow, even notes underneath, like a steady hand.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Soft strings, then the soprano alone, high and gentle | "Ihr habt nun Traurigkeit" ("Ye now therefore have sorrow", John 16:22) |
| ≈ 1:25 | The choir enters below her in slow, quiet notes | "Ich will euch trösten" ("I will comfort you", Isaiah 66:13) |
| ≈ 2:40 | The soprano sings a new phrase in a brighter key | "Sehet mich an" ("Behold with your eyes", Sirach 51:27) |
| ≈ 4:40 | The choir's comforting words return, the soprano soaring above | "wie einen seine Mutter tröstet" ("as one whom his mother comforteth") |
| Near the end | Soprano and choir fade together | A quiet close |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the two layers at once: the soprano moving freely above, the choir moving slowly below.

### VI. Denn wir haben hie keine bleibende Statt

**Summary.** The most dramatic movement. "For here we have no lasting city." The baritone announces a mystery: we shall all be changed, at the last trumpet. The music explodes into a fast, stormy section, "Death, where is thy sting?", and ends with a great [[fugue]] in C major: "Lord, thou art worthy to receive glory."

**Main ideas**

- The wandering: soft, uncertain steps in the choir, over plucked strings.
- The storm: fast, loud, in three beats, with the full orchestra.
- The fugue: a long, broad subject in C major, built up voice by voice.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The choir sings softly, as if wandering, over [[pizzicato]] strings | "Denn wir haben hie keine bleibende Statt" ("For here have we no continuing city", Hebrews 13:14) |
| ≈ 1:50 | The baritone alone, mysterious | "Siehe, ich sage euch ein Geheimnis" ("Behold, I shew you a mystery", 1 Corinthians 15:51) |
| ≈ 3:30 | Tension builds in the choir | "zu der Zeit der letzten Posaune" ("at the last trump") |
| ≈ 4:00 | Suddenly fast and loud, the whole orchestra in a storm | "Denn es wird die Posaune schallen" ("For the trumpet shall sound"), *Vivace* |
| ≈ 5:15 | The choir hurls out questions | "Tod, wo ist dein Stachel? Hölle, wo ist dein Sieg?" ("O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?") |
| ≈ 7:00 | A broad, majestic fugue in C major | "Herr, du bist würdig" ("Thou art worthy, O Lord", Revelation 4:11) |
| Last minute | The fugue reaches its climax and ends in a blaze of C major | The triumphant end of the movement |

**Things to notice**

1. Count the voices entering at the start of the fugue: sopranos and altos, tenors, basses, each with the same broad tune.

### VII. Selig sind die Toten

**Summary.** "Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord." After the storm, calm. The sopranos begin high and slowly; the music grows warm and serene. At the end Brahms brings back the music of the first movement, and the harp returns: the last word of the work is the same as the first, "selig", "blessed".

**Main ideas**

- The opening: the sopranos alone, a long, rising line, answered by the basses.
- The return: the closing music of the first movement, with harp.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The sopranos sing a long, high, rising phrase; the basses answer | "Selig sind die Toten" ("Blessed are the dead", Revelation 14:13) |
| ≈ 3:30 | A brighter key, a gentler movement | "Ja, der Geist spricht, dass sie ruhen von ihrer Arbeit" ("Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours") |
| ≈ 6:00 | The opening words and melody return | "Selig sind die Toten" again |
| ≈ 8:00 | The harp returns, and the music of the first movement's ending is heard again | The circle closes |
| Last minute | Soft, rippling harp and a last, quiet "selig" | The same word that began the work ends it |

**Things to notice**

1. Compare the last minute with the last minute of the first movement. The music is almost the same; the words now speak of the dead instead of the mourners.

**In this recording.** Otto Klemperer recorded the Requiem in Kingsway Hall in 1961 with two of the great German singers of the time, Elisabeth Schwarzkopf and Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau. Klemperer, then in his mid-seventies, takes slow, weighty tempi, which makes this one of the longer recordings.

### Threads

- **Comfort for the living.** Almost every movement moves from sorrow to consolation. Only the central fourth and the last are calm throughout.
- **A symmetry.** The first and last movements begin "Selig sind" and share their ending; the second and sixth are the dramatic ones; the third and fifth begin with a soloist. The fourth stands in the middle.
- **No dogma.** Brahms left out any mention of Christ's death and redemption. When the cathedral's music director in Bremen asked him to add it, he refused.
