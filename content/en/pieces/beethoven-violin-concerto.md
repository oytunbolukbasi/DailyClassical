### The big picture

- Beethoven wrote the concerto in 1806 for Franz Clement, one of the leading violinists in Vienna, and Clement gave the premiere on 23 December 1806 at the Theater an der Wien. Beethoven is believed to have finished the solo part so late that Clement had to play some of it at sight.
- The premiere was not a success, and the concerto was rarely played for almost forty years. In 1844, twelve-year-old Joseph Joachim played it in London with Felix Mendelssohn conducting, and from then on it became one of the central works for the violin.
- Instead of fireworks, the soloist mostly sings: long, high melodies and decorations above the orchestra. The opening five drumbeats return throughout the first movement.
- Beethoven also arranged the concerto for piano and orchestra. For that version he wrote a long first-movement [[cadenza]] in which the timpani join the soloist; this recording uses it, adapted for the violin.
- In one line: a broad, serene first movement held together by a drumbeat (I), a hushed set of variations (II), a hunting-song rondo that follows without a break (III).

### I. Allegro ma non troppo

**Summary.** Five soft strokes on the timpani, then a calm tune in the woodwinds. The orchestra presents all the themes first; only after three and a half minutes does the violin enter, climbing from low to very high, and go through them again in its own way. That is the Classical [[double exposition]]. The movement is long and unhurried, and the drumbeat keeps coming back underneath.

**Main ideas**

- The drumbeat: five even, quiet strokes, first on the timpani, later in the strings, winds and brass.
- Theme 1: a calm, gentle tune in the woodwinds.
- Theme 2: a melody that rises and falls in scales, first in the major, then in the minor.

**Things to notice**

1. Count how many times you hear the drumbeat of five even notes. It moves from the timpani to the strings, the woodwinds and the trumpets.
2. In the cadenza, listen for the timpani. A cadenza is usually for the soloist alone; here the drum joins in.

**In this recording.** Schneiderhan plays Beethoven's own cadenzas, taken from the piano version of the concerto and adapted for the violin by Schneiderhan himself. Many violinists play Fritz Kreisler's cadenzas instead. On the Spotify album the concerto is tracks 1–3, followed by Mozart's Violin Concerto No. 5.

### II. Larghetto

**Summary.** A slow, hushed movement in G major. The muted strings play a simple theme; then horn, clarinet and bassoon take it in turn while the violin weaves fine decorations above them. The violin sings a new melody of its own, the theme returns, and an orchestral outburst leads to a short solo that runs straight into the finale.

**Main ideas**

- The theme: a slow, simple melody for [[muted]] strings.
- The violin's song: a tender melody of its own, heard twice.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | Muted strings play a slow, hushed theme | The theme, in G major. The movement is a set of quiet [[variation]]s on it |
| ≈ 1:15 | The solo violin enters, weaving fine decorations high above while the horn, then the clarinet, carry the theme | The violin decorates rather than leads |
| ≈ 5:20 | The violin sings a new melody of its own, simple and tender | A second theme, for the soloist |
| ≈ 7:40 | The theme returns quietly in the strings, with the violin floating above | |
| Last 40 seconds | A sudden loud outburst from the orchestra; then the violin alone plays a short, free passage | A brief [[cadenza]] that leads straight into the finale ([[attacca]]) |

**Things to notice**

1. For most of the movement the violin does not play the theme at all. It decorates it, high above, while the orchestra's instruments take turns with the melody.

### III. Rondo: Allegro

**Summary.** A [[rondo]] with a hunting-song theme in six beats, first played low on the violin and then shouted back by the whole orchestra. Between its returns come a bright episode and a sadder one in G minor. After the cadenza the theme surfaces in a surprising key before the concerto ends in high spirits.

**Main ideas**

- The rondo theme: a jaunty hunting tune, played first low on the violin.
- The G minor episode: a slower-feeling, sad melody shared by the violin and the bassoon.

**Listening stops**

| Time | What you hear | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≈ 0:00 | The solo violin, low and jaunty, plays a hunting-call tune; the orchestra answers it loudly | The rondo theme |
| ≈ 1:30 | Rapid runs in the violin and a bright new tune; horns sound hunting calls | The first episode |
| ≈ 2:30 | The hunting tune returns | The rondo theme |
| ≈ 3:25 | A gentler, sadder tune in G minor, sung by the violin and then the bassoon | The middle episode |
| ≈ 4:45 | The hunting tune again | The rondo theme |
| ≈ 7:30–8:40 | The violin plays alone | The [[cadenza]] |
| ≈ 8:40 | The violin plays the tune quietly in an unexpected key; the oboe answers | A surprise detour before the home key |
| Last 20 seconds | A last, quiet echo of the tune, then two loud chords | The end |

**Things to notice**

1. Listen to the G minor episode. It is the only time the finale looks back to the stillness of the slow movement.

### Threads

- **The drumbeat.** Five even notes open the concerto, return all through the first movement and come back in the timpani during Beethoven's cadenza.
- **Song, not show.** The violin spends most of the concerto singing and decorating, high above the orchestra, rather than competing with it.
- **Linked movements.** The slow movement does not end: a short solo carries it straight into the finale.
